# PART 1 - How to read this book fast without missing anything

## 1.1 What the book is, in one paragraph

A memoir of an ex-Goldman quant turned startup founder (AdGrok), Y Combinator alumnus, acqui-hire negotiator and Facebook ads product manager. Its through-line: ad tech is finance for attention; incumbents keep markets inefficient; startups are experiments with other people's money in which team, leverage and timing matter more than ideas; and Facebook's monetization was shaped less by foresight than by structural luck (mobile). Three parts: Part One (Goldman, Adchemy, the YC decision), Part Two (YC, fund-raising, the lawsuit, the acquisition dance), Part Three (Facebook).

# PART 2 - Chapter by chapter

## Chapter 1: Prologue, The Undertakers of Capitalism, The Human Attention Exchange

### Central argument
Wall Street and ad tech are the same business, putting a price on human perception, and both are being taken over by machines. Martínez opens with the April 2012 Zuck meeting that green-lit retargeting, then rewinds to his Goldman Sachs quant days to establish the book's lens: markets are run by whoever makes prices, inefficiency is profitable to incumbents, and quants and engineers are enablers rather than stars.

### Full stories

**The April 13, 2012 Zuck meeting.** Facebook's IPO was imminent and revenue was plateauing; Open Graph and Sponsored Stories were a market failure. Martínez and colleagues brought three proposals to Zuckerberg: using Like-button browsing data (Zuck said no) and two ways to join external data (retargeting got a yes: "yes to retargeting, but no to using social plugins"). Beforehand, Sheryl acted as gatekeeper, holding pre-meetings to shape the message for Zuck: the "Supreme Court of Sheryl." Martínez's observation is that such decisions are "made on gut feel, the residue of whatever historical politics were in play, and the ability to cater persuasive messages to people either busy, impatient, or uninterested." The meeting sets the stage for the outside-data pivot of Chapters 12-13. The chapter also paints Facebook's geography: proximity to Zuck as status, the Aquarium, HACK in the courtyard, and Ads kept "at arm's length... like sweaty underwear."

**Goldman Sachs and SLK.** Martínez's Goldman years frame everything. Goldman's acquired broker SLK went from hundreds of traders and two programmers (2000) to two traders and twenty quants with fast machines (2007). Via Andreessen, the generalization: two future job types, people who tell computers what to do and people told by computers what to do. Finance saw it first; ad tech (real-time bidding), then Uber, Airbnb and Instacart follow. Goldman's ecology: partners as feudal lords (Elisha Wiesel), traders as chainsaw jugglers, sales as "complete tools," quants as "eunuchs at the orgy" who get knee calluses. Goldman could compute risk across desks "to five decimals" and never lost its cool.

**Why Goldman wouldn't move CDS to exchanges.** With few players, total information asymmetry and price-making power, transparency only invited competition. "Goldman felt more comfortable owning a small market than merely participating in a larger one," a pattern Martínez says he saw again at Facebook. Also: a CDS is unlike car insurance because anyone can buy or write protection on someone else's "car," including the thief.

**The trading-floor gambling and the ID lottery (worked example).** The White Castle burger contest, push-up over/unders and the Friday ID-in-a-sack lottery. With a $2,000 pot and ten IDs left, fair value is $200 each; options on IDs traded above fair value, and the premium rose as fewer remained (irrational overbidding for large, likelier payouts, plus Friday cash pressure) until a memo banned betting. Lesson: markets deviate from fair value in predictable, exploitable directions when payouts are large and participants have outside pressures.

**The bonus number and Scott Weinstein.** The bonus number was your entire worth; the "roach-motel collar" tied people in. Scott Weinstein, who prized security, was fired after twenty years: "the best advice... that I ignored to my extreme peril."

**The DUI gambit.** Over the limit at 0.91, Martínez offered the officer a taxi under observation instead of a station blood test, reframing the encounter from castigation to haggling. Lesson: "if you want to be a startup entrepreneur, get used to negotiating from positions of weakness."

**Adchemy and the throwaway RTB engine.** A *NYT* item, a whim CV, a Mustang and a Zeitgeist Bloody Mary led him to Adchemy, betting that tech would be "the last man standing." At Adchemy he built a throwaway RTB engine to feed the CEO's pitch; it later made him "light-years ahead" of Facebook's product team. Anti-pattern: building tech to support a CEO's bullshit, but keep the knowledge.

### Evidence
- **SLK headcount (Goldman, 2000 vs. 2007):** hundreds of traders and two programmers vs. two traders and twenty quants. Proves the computers-replace-decision-makers thesis; caveat: a single firm illustration.
- **Facebook's data barely moved monetization:** a year of ingesting posts, check-ins, Likes and friends did little (the evidence for "Facebook data isn't that valuable"; formalized by Project Chorizo in Ch 8).
- **Programmatic buying (Right Media, 2007):** shifted power from publishers to advertisers with first-party data, e.g., Amazon's purchase data beats "35-year-old male in Ohio."
- **The ID lottery:** observed premiums above the $200 fair value; proves predictable mispricing under large payouts. Caveat: a floor anecdote, not a controlled study.
- Cheatsheet numbers: Goldman average pay 2005 $521K; grad stipend $19K.

### Key terms
Price on perception; derivative; CDS; SLK lesson; RTB (a stock exchange for attention: every page load emits a sell order for one share of your brain); direct response vs. brand; first-party data; programmatic buying; "every new medium imitates the last" (radio read books; TV filmed quiz shows; internet ads copied 1836 newspaper boxes; websites are still called "publishers"); Only Good News/"Supreme Court of Sheryl."

### So what
When you meet any "data" business, ask what it really sells and who makes the price. Prefer first-party purchase data to demographics. Expect incumbents to resist transparency. Negotiate by reframing the encounter, not by pleading.

## Chapter 2: Knowing How to Swim, Abandoning the Shipwreck

### Central argument
How to diagnose a doomed startup from the inside, why you should leave it, and how to get into Y Combinator. Adchemy showed every terminal symptom; the three-person team applied to YC with a five-miracle idea, won a ten-minute interrogation on team quality, and escaped Adchemy through harassment, retention bribes and an immigration threat, arriving at the book's cynical fourteen-line creed.

### Full stories

**Adchemy's decay.** The author's former employer displayed all four signs: a lone surviving founder (the Team page purged like a Stalin photo); ~$6M/month of lead-gen revenue unrelated to the product (a trust fund that "makes a drug addict's spiral more long lasting"); a rotating set of beta-customer logos every quarter; and loyal employees despite mistreatment. Murthy broke the author's finger with a baseball and mocked him for months; staff still bought stock and lost savings. Adchemy's later end (2014): after $130M+ raised, a fire-sale "acquisition," and employees who had bought stock lost savings: "Their Stockholm syndrome lasted to the very end."

**The team.** Matthew McEachen (MRM, Adchemy's employee #11 and author of half its codebase, family man, "the silverback"), Argyris Zymnis (Stanford AI lab, Greek, non-citizen) and the author (words and bets). The team's first idea, linking a bar code to a local ad, needed five miracles. Their product pivoted until it ended as "Plan J."

**The YC application (March 1-29, 2010).** Procrastinating by reading PG essays, the author noticed "Deadline March 3," handed Argyris the printout (the "gateway drug": PG's "How to Start a Startup") and spent two days at Peet's writing the application ("the Goldman Sachs of advertising"; the Craigslist man-in-the-middle hack as the answer to "what non-computer system have you hacked?"). The boys built a demo. They took the last interview slot on the last day (be first or last on any list). The interview was ten minutes, four partners firing overlapping questions ("How do you join the online click to the physical person?" "So how do you get paid again?"), a countdown clock, Jessica silent, no demo reached; an alum trashed the idea afterward. Over beer at the Rose & Crown, PG called with the acceptance; the author blundered by saying "I should probably ask him first," the WRONG ANSWER, because commitment should be decided before the offer. MRM was convinced by phone, packaged as provisional, and the acceptance came by voicemail. Pattern: apply late and fast, sell the team, pre-commit.

**YC people.** PG's twice-yearly boot camp (small money, ship in three months, Demo Day); Jessica Livingston (*Founders at Work*, "a startup anarchist's cookbook"), Robert Morris ("He's never wrong"), Trevor Blackwell (Anybots' landlord).

**Leaving Adchemy.** McEachen said goodbye to Murthy, who didn't look up or shake his hand; the author admits he was doing the same thing: "he was merely trading Murthy for me" ("every Jobs has his Wozniak"). Retention pressure came as equity, fear and legal threats. Adchemy's immigrant executives threatened to report their former employee to ICE for planning to work on an F-2, "former slaves turned slave owners"; the author blurted his legal workaround to the party that wanted to hurt him (an anti-pattern). The truth ("we have no products, no clients") made them a threat; expect quarantine.

**British Trader and Zoë.** An ex-Deutsche Bank derivatives trader met on Match; pregnant after weeks; Zoë born March 2010 during YC applications: "life is what happens when you're making other plans."

### Evidence
- **Adchemy lead-gen revenue ~$6M/month**, with no product clients: shows a cash cow masking product failure.
- **Murthy and the baseball:** the Stockholm-syndrome anecdote; staff still bought stock.
- **Adchemy's outcome (2014):** $130M+ raised, fire-sale acquisition, employee losses. Caveat: this is the notes' retrospective in Ch 5.
- **PG's endorsement of the "X of Y" tagline** ("the Goldman Sachs of advertising"): now cliché.
- **YC interview:** no demo reached; yet accepted: evidence that YC judges team quality, using the idea as a probe.

### Key terms
Four signs of a doomed startup; miracle count; pivot ("a panicked sprint"); rage equation ((society's valuation of you - your self-valuation) x ego); X of Y tagline; H-1B peonage; stealth/NDA as a loser tattoo; "Writing as engineered deception" (language exists "to better deceive, not inform"); the fourteen-line creed; "Every Jobs has his Wozniak."

**The creed (paraphrase):** investors have more money than time; employees more time than money; entrepreneurs are the seductive go-betweens; startups are experiments with other people's money; marketing is like sex, only losers pay; culture is what goes without saying; no rules, only laws; success forgives all sins; people who leak to you leak about you; meritocracy is propaganda; greed and vanity are the twin engines; most managers survive via inertia and politics; lawsuits are expensive feints; capitalism is an amoral farce in which everyone is complicit.

### So what
Before joining or investing, check who left, where the money really comes from, whether logos become contracts and whether loyalty is earned or induced. Count miracles before any pitch, investment or job offer. Decide commitments among cofounders before the offer arrives. Treat retention pressure as confirmation to leave.

## Chapter 3: War Face, Like Marriage, Speed Is a Feature, D-Day

### Central argument
Inside YC: weekly dinners, partner access, the "YC mafia" network, prestige and Demo Day. Google's keyword auction prints money while the SMB last mile is a graveyard. Equal-equity founder teams fail ("Have a leader!"). Content marketing manufactured AdGrok's existence. The book's title comes from Netflix's chaos monkey as a metaphor for entrepreneurs who pull the plug on whole industries.

### Full stories

**YC dinners.** Marissa Mayer arrived with a Google handler and shook; Reid Hoffman "thundered like an emperor," the best speaker.

**The GrokPad and the GrokBar.** A Mountain View one-bedroom with Home Depot door desks, near Red Rock Coffee and Fenwick & West ("an entity we would, sadly, come to know well"). The GrokBar was a browser bar (ripped from the DiggBar) showing, on each page of an online store, which Google keywords drive sales and at what cost. The pitch: AdWords is an undammed river of money; take a fraction.

**The three founders.** MRM can fix anything (Leatherman on a street sign), designs well, inflates his own centrality, chases shiny bugs and is emotionally fragile; Argyris is a renaissance hacker, pugnacious "emotional IED," works on whatever he likes; the author supplies words, bets and the unwanted peacemaker role. Equal thirds produced decision paralysis and rotating two-against-one factions. PG diagnosed the leadership gap in ten minutes of a walk. About six of thirty batch companies had disputed leadership and crumbled or had coups. AdGrok's eventual undoing was the founders' natures, not an enemy. Geoff Ralston: "people don't really change, they just become better actors."

**Prototype Day and the rehearsal.** The author tripped over the projector cord, delivered five seconds of demo, "a complete fucking fiasco" - then AdGrok took second place in the Demo Day rehearsal vote. The T-shirt arms race: AdGrok wore logo shirts at rehearsal; by Demo Day every team had rush-ordered its own. Rapportive's polished Prototype Day pitch dismayed him, yet two years later both companies had the same acqui-hire exit: "This too shall pass."

**The New York post (full worked example).** Trigger: Ron Conway and PG praised New York's startup scene at dinner. Thesis: New York's economy is monopolies of information (banks, agents, rental brokers) that go unchallenged, while San Francisco builds Craigslist and makes the pimp obsolete. Two nights writing; a Tuesday 9 a.m. post to Hacker News (Monday too groggy, Thursday drifting, Friday for burying news); friends upvote; #1 within minutes; a Scoble tweet; the single-server blog melts; MRM clones the blog across new AWS machines; traffic jumps from about a dozen views/day to 50,000; Business Insider and 20/20 call. The Goldman post was held in reserve for Monday's attention decay. The result: thousands of sign-ups and a viral post every 3-4 weeks. Principles: media attention is like sex, only good and better; pick the biggest nerve; seed upvotes; reply to fans; egg on trolls; double down when it works; have servers ready.

**Demo Day.** About 30 companies, 2.5 minutes each, three salvos in descending investor importance, two rehearsals (batch, then alumni), no actual demos (investors care about the business), hours of "nut-to-butt" networking; the running-of-the-bulls waiting feeling.

**Chaos monkey.** Netflix's tool randomly kills servers to test resilience; entrepreneurs are "society's chaos monkeys" (Uber vs. medallions, Airbnb vs. hotels, Tinder vs. dating), fed by VC "bananas." Whether society survives them, and at what human cost, is society's problem, not the zoo's.

### Evidence
- **Google makes $70B** via auctions on every query. Mechanism: bid x estimated click probability. Words are the products: insurance, loans, mortgage, lawyer; "mesothelioma" at ~$90/click.
- **ROAS = revenue / cost - 1**, e.g., "nevada cheap divorce": 47%.
- **SMB graveyard:** Clickable ($32M), Lexity ($6M), Trada ($19M) all died; SMBs pay $50-100/month and cancel fast.
- **YC batch leadership failures:** ~6 of 30 companies had disputed leadership.
- **Blog traffic:** from ~a dozen views/day to 50,000; #1 on Hacker News. Caveat: a single viral event, but repeated every 3-4 weeks.
- **YC's network:** ~80% of the consumer/infrastructure stack can be rebuilt from YC companies, with discounts and preferential adoption.

### Key terms
What YC sells; ROAS; SMB trap; last-mile thesis; Have a leader; Vulcan mind-meld; chaos monkey; Demo Day; This too shall pass; "Not every problem has an engineering solution. That doesn't mean you can't sell one."

### So what
Use YC's network as customers and logos. Search advertising rewards intent at its apex. Avoid Mom-and-Pop unless you partner with or build on someone who owns them. Appoint one leader (51%+ or genuine mind-meld) before you start. Launch with provocation, timing and doubling-down, with scalable servers; at Demo Day pitch the business.

## Chapter 4: A Conclave of Angels, Hill of Sand, Widening Gyre, ¡No Pasarán!

### Central argument
A field manual for seed fund-raising (angels vs. micro-VCs, convertible notes and caps, dilution, VC hierarchy and the "do you have decision-making power?" test) interrupted by Adchemy's personal trade-secret lawsuit at peak post-Demo Day momentum. AdGrok survived by lying in a spreadsheet, halving its cap and getting Fenwick & West to defend it for mispriced equity. Thesis: startups are founded on forgotten crimes; "there are no real rules, only laws"; success forgives all sins.

### Full stories

**Russ Siegelman, the angel.** Ex-Microsoft/MSN and ex-Kleiner, investing his own money, he wants 2-4x, wants it back if the company is imploding, does haphazard due diligence through friends, and is committed. The author groomed Russ weeks before Demo Day even though he arrived late and lost to the first meeting ("he almost wrote us off"). Had they not groomed him, "we'd have lost... our lead investor." At a $4M cap Russ's headshake was a tell that he'd do it if pushed: the author's self-described skill.

**Chris Sacca, the micro-VC.** A $20-40M fund ("a fund of bosses"), needing outsized outliers and supplying a gold-plated name that makes weak-kneed investors follow; he said "I'm in!" in half an hour.

**Sand Hill Road and Sequoia.** The road "starts in a shopping mall and ends at a particle accelerator," with the Rosewood's cougar nights between; Sequoia's lobby is a "mini-Louvre" of tombstones (Apple, Google, Airbnb) with model-caliber receptionists; VC offices are "absolutely still." The engineers broke the codebase a minute before the Sequoia pitch (hence "fund-raise alone"). Sequoia passed citing "white space" with a competing investment (Kenshoo): "a truth that is also a polite lie," but stayed prompt and offered intros: "it's the incompetent and insecure who are generally the assholes." At Charles River, Bill Tai asked "would you sell for $50M?" and the author smirked; the wrong answer cost the deal, and the question "would prove prescient."

**Ben Narasin (TriplePoint) and Ace & Company.** Narasin: sockless Top-Siders, the rumpled $50,000 emergency check from a Schwab account at a Turkish-Mexican tapas bar, wallet photos ("this is who you're working for"); MRM asked "what about my kids?" Ace & Company: a Swiss family fund, an Egyptian telecoms fortune, a wire from Zug: "a little red never made money less green." The round: four investors, ~$500K at a $2M cap.

**The suit (Sept 10, 2010).** Wilson Sonsini filed for trade-secret misappropriation, breach of contract and loyalty, and injunctive relief personally against all three founders and the company, with ~$2,000 in AdGrok's bank. Apocalypse planning: MRM would quit, Argyris would flee to Greece, the author would blog every filing to shame Adchemy; the unspoken fear was that Murthy would rehire the boys. None happened; MRM, the family man, bet everything: "the actual daredevil."

**Fenwick and the Undertaker.** Rodger Cole, Fenwick's litigator with a "frigid demeanor." The Fenwick deal: $250K in fees fronted as a loan for a small equity slice plus options; if AdGrok died, nothing repaid; if it won, buy back the equity at the seed price with no prepayment penalty: "a one-in-a-million lottery ticket to win $250,000... for $250,000." Cash-only solo lawyers at $400/hour cost more than $700/hour partners who take equity ("cash is the poor man's credit"). The French-mathematician gambit: tell lawyers the investors are in and would they take equity; tell investors legal costs are minimal because lawyers take equity; let the engineers code.

**The Death Clock.** A cash-burn spreadsheet including Fenwick's estimate (~$500K to trial, ~$1.5M total over 18 months, ~$20K/month); every version crashed into the ground. He hid it from cofounders and investors and "cooked the books" - lower legal costs, an impossible launch date, an "unconscionable" growth rate. Russ stayed, but cut the cap from $4M to $2M and required $200K more from others; about 22% was sold (a future Series A problem: "worrying about cholesterol while dying of cancer").

**Murthy's kindness that armed his enemies.** Murthy's one kindness, the Ted Wang intro (framed as a tribute to the late Rajeev Motwani), led Wang to fight out of outrage at the bullying of a startup. "If you're going to be a sociopath, do it properly." Sun Tzu: leave your opponent a way out; Murthy didn't, so even the skittish fought.

**Forgotten crimes.** Gates cloned Kildall's QDOS and kept the copyright; Jobs pocketed the Atari bonus and paid Wozniak $350; Zuckerberg ran with the idea he was hired to build. Balzac: "The secret of great fortunes without apparent cause is a forgotten crime." The Valley is reactionary about its image. Also the Silicon Valley network is fluid: unlike Wall Street's birth-and-prep-school pipeline, anyone who makes superficial friends and publishes a few thought pieces can join the elite and fall out as fast. Kiteboarding and the MaiTai camp are the VC elite's club, entered via social capital and "thought leadership."

### Evidence
- **Cap math (worked):** $100K note, no cap: Series A at $10M gives 1%; at $100M gives 0.1%. With a $3M cap: 3.3% regardless. Founders' side: $4M discussed, $2M closed, ~22% sold for ~$500K (roughly double typical seed dilution).

- **The suit's timing:** ~$2,000 in the bank when sued.

### Key terms
Angel vs. micro-VC; convertible note; cap; dilution; Death Clock; decision-making power; mispriced option; forgotten crime; French-mathematician gambit; groom your lead; fund-raise alone.

### So what
Negotiate the cap, not a "valuation." Groom a lead before Demo Day and talk cap on the third date. Ask any VC "Do you have decision-making power?" and route around hedgers. Disclose a lawsuit only when pens hover, price it into terms, and get a first-tier firm to take mispriced equity.

### Central argument

## Chapter 7: The Various Futures of the Forking Paths, Retweets, The Dotted Line, Endgame

### Central argument
The anatomy of a hybrid acqui-hire: Facebook rejected the engineers but wanted the CEO; Twitter returned at ~$10M; the CEO lied to his cofounders for two weeks, helped them win Twitter's interviews, then walked to Facebook at the term-sheet meeting - and Twitter still called back within an hour with $5M+ for the two engineers. The post-mortem: acquirers buy people at a target price per head; founders own nothing until they vest; "AdGrok was simply a long, stressful job interview"; the optimal play would have required deceiving cofounders.

### Full stories

**April 5: the split.** Twitter offered ~$10M. Amin's call said Facebook was "mixed" on Argyris and Matt (MRM a definite no; "cultural fit"), but "we want you." British Trader advised: don't tell the boys. Facebook is "legalized crack": CityVille hit 100M users in a month; a Florida mother shook her baby to death over FarmVille notifications - "products that cause mothers to murder their infants in order to use them more... simply cannot fail." (The domino set: his Cuban grandmother's five smuggled pounds, now unused because his mother plays FarmVille.)

**The Whereoscope template (Mick Johnson).** Shop to Twitter, Zynga, Google, Facebook; force bidders who each want part into the same room; accept a Frankenstein of cash, dual vesting and an investor side-deal. Skulduggery is the non-meritocrat's tool: "anyone who claims the Valley is meritocratic... has profited vastly from it via nonmeritocratic means."

**Technical due diligence and the engine dying.** Twitter inspected the stack, possibly line by line; AWS and off-the-shelf components let anyone fake a product, so they check scaling competence. At the South Park picnic table (where Twitter itself was conceived), the confrontation about waning commitment led to the realization: no shipping for a month post-launch, MRM checked out, the mind-meld gone. "The dev team is the engine of a tech company. If they were done, then we were dead in the water."

**The Twitter interview day (April 13).** Half the interviewers came from the DabbleDB acquisition; second-order stress ("if I didn't do well, they'd suffer"). A Twitter engineer's selling point, that nobody works past six - breakfast and lunch only - sealed the choice against Twitter. The Getaround race: a Tesla Roadster vs. Matt Tillman's Mini to Stinson Beach, a wrong-side-of-the-road pass through Muir Beach, a depleted battery, then a Porsche on Lombard: "he didn't get to that Porsche by turning down all-in challenges."

**The desengaño (April 14-18).** Jess's "Call" promised a term sheet; the CEO confessed the lie to the boys before the reckoning and presented his departure as a fait accompli to avoid mutiny. April 15: term sheets from both. April 16: Sacca's last call - fury at the investor-light term sheet, then at the Facebook news ("You're going to fuck up this entire deal!") - and a hang-up; they'd never speak again. Facebook's replicating-portfolio offer (~$32/share pre-IPO; ~$2.3M, about a 25% AdGrok stake after the split) accepted verbally.

**April 18: walkout and callback.** At noon at Twitter, Satya Patel (new head of product): "So you're telling us you're bailing on the deal?" The team walked out unescorted; the boys "would have kicked me to death." At 2:00 p.m. Twitter called MRM with $5M+ for the two engineers; 2:45 a lawyer said accept now and resign first; the 3:07 Caltrain; the resignation email went out as corp dev opened the door - "unemployed for all of five minutes." The scanning frenzy was "like the last few minutes inside the safe room of the American embassy in Tehran." The deal remained "a minor Silicon Valley oddity."

**The master play he didn't make.** Negotiate both offers in secret, front-load Twitter with single-trigger acceleration or up-front cash, accept, quit day one, walk to Facebook (Twitter too embarrassed to sue). Impossible without deceiving cofounders: "a step too far out on the prick spectrum." "Morality... is an expensive hobby."

### Evidence
- **Price figures:** Twitter ~$10M -> $5M+ for two engineers; investors received ~$1M of ~$10M vs. ~$2M pro rata under Twitter's default term sheet.
- **Facebook replicating portfolio:** ~$32/share pre-IPO -> ~$2.3M, ~25% of AdGrok stake.
- **Speed of Twitter's callback:** within about two hours of the noon announcement per the day-by-day (the notes also say "within an hour" of the walkout): evidence of a weak hand.
- **Headline vs. reality:** "sold for $X million" may be 10% company price and 90% signing bonuses and vesting for founders.
- **Vesting:** founders' 20-40% stakes vest like employees'; ten months in, "nobody at AdGrok actually owned anything."

### Key terms
Legalized crack; Whereoscope template; Land of the Stateless Machines (Californians hold no grudges and reward no generosity; the sunny "Hi!" means "I don't care"; "hippies with a capitalization table and a vesting schedule"); desengaño; consideration; replicating portfolio; call station; resign before you sign; corp dev = HR with a fatter checkbook; single-trigger acceleration.

### So what
Treat an acqui-hire as N separate job offers wrapped together. Know who bears the cost of the investor split (you). Sequence disclosures so each party learns your move when it can no longer stop it, but notice the notes treat deceiving cofounders as the line. Get employment counsel; accept the same day; resign before you sign.

## Chapter 9: One Shot, One Kill, Twice Bitten, Ads Five-Oh, The Narcissism of Privacy

### Central argument
Why Facebook's data was so hard to monetize (the noisy bar), why scale sets a $100M bar on any new ads idea, how ads were tested on slices of humanity, how the Irish audit ended with a Christmas Eve SQL command on a live production database - and Martínez's contrarian privacy thesis: what embarrasses you is commercially worthless, advertisers want your purchases and browsing, and Facebook doesn't sell your data, it buys it.

Noisy bar; $100M bar; PM as portfolio manager; unit of account; telephone exchange; ad blocking as theft; privacy as Iran's nukes; embarrassing != valuable; "a black hole of data that can never leave"; MOSI; Scalps@Facebook.

### So what

**The IPO tent (Feb 1, 2012).** An email from Zuck (email meant serious; "the Sec will come get you"); the filing was announced with a warning about public ridicule and "stay focused on our mission"; the author watched a Harvard old-timer browse $2.4M Diamond Heights listings during the speech. **Haves and have-mores.** Early employees with life-changing wealth beside later hires merely well paid; money never discussed on campus; the NR250 group as a how-to for the new rich (LLCs for compounds, jets, Maui); the nobility "furiously denied their very existence" like fish unaware of water; 401(k) matching arrived late because "the IPO was the pension" - for some. Middle managers hired late knew the gap yet sided with owners against peers: Marx's managerial-class argument, which Martínez says describes technobourgeois society exactly.

### Evidence
- **Vesting math:** four-year vesting, 25% at one year, then quarterly; halve the headline (founders won't last four years); halve again for stock vs. options (tax treatment). Combined: an acqui-hire headline is worth about a quarter.
- **Speedometer comparison:** autobahn / 101 / Delhi intersection (qualitative).
- **Blue-tape sequence:** vandalism in two days; macro in the same week (the loop as evidence of Facebook's culture).
- **Wealth stratification:** qualitative (NR250 group; 401(k) timing); no numbers beyond the author's own.
- **Caveat:** the "People's Republic" analogy is the author's rhetorical comparison, not a measured finding.

### Key terms
Blue tape; memento mori; red book; acquisitions as hybridization; track racing vs. commuting; VIP ("vesting in peace"); haves and have-mores; People's Republic of Facebook; NR250; Faceversary; PMMess.

### So what
Allow experiments but prune ruthlessly and encode the lesson. Keep a reminder of your own mortality as a company. When valued an offer, halve and halve again. Notice whether money is undiscussed; it signals a two-class company.

## Chapter 11: When the Flying Saucers Fail to Appear, Monetizing the Tumor

### Central argument
Facebook's bet-the-company monetization plan before the IPO - Open Graph's verbs feeding "Sponsored Stories" - was a two-miracle idea sustained by cognitive dissonance until Sheryl declared no brand would keep paying $10M a year for Likes. Revenue desperation bred product monsters like the Logout Experience, which collided with Growth, the one team that knew marketing, and exposed the rule that engagement and money are anticorrelated.

### Full stories

**The cult and the company.** Festinger's 1954 flying-saucer cult grew stronger when the prophecy failed; companies likewise "go temporarily mad." Facebook's two revenue streams circa 2010: agencies convincing brands (Starbucks, Burberry) to buy millions of page Likes at ~$10M a pop with no account of how Likes became sales, a mystery of faith; and direct-response advertisers, barely present because targeting was too weak, except game developers who found tiny proxies (energy-drink Likes predicting mob-game conversions: a Facebook "beer and diapers").

**Open Graph and Sponsored Stories.** Expand "Like" to verbs (play, listen, watch, buy) broadcast by outside developers in exchange for feed distribution, and monetize by "boosting" stories advertisers pay for. Two miracles: every company integrating and donating data at dubious benefit; marketers abandoning proven workflows for an unprovable paradigm. Gavage-style launching: products forced down partners' throats and then advertisers'; when partners reported no performance difference, the response was to push harder. A VP kept a magnum of champagne with a Post-it targeting "10% revenue from Sponsored Stories" by end-2011, never approached.

**FMC (Feb 2012).** Facebook's only marketing conference, at the American Museum of Natural History: a Timeline Hallway that replayed attendees' lives, Sheryl's forgettable keynote, Paul Adams's *Grouped* thesis (influence propagates through small friend networks - contradicted by Facebook's own research), Alicia Keys under the blue whale; engineers were relegated to badge check-in. "Never let the facts get in the way of a good story."

**The research paper.** Ads leadership sold "social context" as the future. The author had seen the internal study: social context lifts clickthrough 40-60% on a base twenty times worse than ordinary display advertising, with confounds acknowledged. Partners in meeting after meeting reported no real live difference; the organization pushed harder. The rational reading: the lift is real but irrelevant; the missing ingredient is first-party targeting data.

**Sheryl calls it (March 2012).** Zuck outsourced monetization to Sheryl; her conference room, "Only Good News," was the appellate court. She declared the Like-buying party over: with the IPO months away and growth no longer doubling, no one will pay ten million for Likes (Burberry, Ford, Starbucks, BMW had bought Likes that were at best a license to spam feeds). Brand marketers were "legacy admissions," paid to spend other people's money on "awareness."

**Growth vs. Ads and LOX.** Ads turns users into money; Growth turns money into users. Growth personalities: Alex Schultz (beefy, loud, liked by the author), Brian Piepgrass (no degree, $100M+ annual spend), Naomi Gleit (employee #29). Growth retargeted near-registrants on ad exchanges before Ads knew exchanges existed, tracked three-decimal conversion stats, ran Skinnerian reminder-email cadences and carrier deals, and kept a Risk-board map of countries yet to "tip" (holdouts "weird, dictatorial, or corrupt": Russia, Burma, Vietnam, Iran; also-rans Hi5, Orkut, MySpace vanished). The Logout Experience (LOX): selling the logout page - the most common webpage on the world's shared computers and the primary on-ramp for new users in developing countries - to brand and movie marketers for a few hundred thousand dollars. Product naming: Ads products named after birds and fish; preserved Facebook (seen after logout) -> salmon -> LOX (Scott Shapiro). Growth couldn't quantify the usage cost, so outrage and personal sway decided it: LOX ran only where Facebook had already won (the US), not where rivals like Orkut were "still warm" (Brazil).

**Running out of humans.** ~3B internet users minus ~600M in China and most of Russia, minus a 25% never-join haircut = ~1.8B addressable. At 1.44B (Q1 2015) and ~13% growth, roughly a year of growth was left; hence Internet.org and solar drones; rich-market ad revenue subsidizing it. "Money follows eyeballs, even if slowly."

### Evidence
- **Social context lift:** 40-60% lift; base clickthrough ~0.05% vs. >=0.1% for the worst display ads and ~1% for well-targeted first-party-data ads; a 20x deficit. Caveat: the paper listed confounds.
- **Like-buying:** ~$10M a pop for page Likes.
- **Champagne target:** 10% of revenue from Sponsored Stories by end-2011, not approached.
- **Addressable humans:** ~1.8B vs. 1.44B actives; ~13% growth.
- **Partner reports:** no real difference in live campaigns (qualitative but repeated).

### Key terms
Cognitive dissonance; two revenue streams; Open Graph; Sponsored Stories; social context; gavage; fog; Only Good News; Growth vs. Ads; running out of humans; Internet.org; LOX; legacy admissions.

### So what
Count miracles in your monetization plan. Judge lift against the best alternative. Listen when partners say it isn't working. Learn from the team that actually does marketing. Choose engagement or revenue for a quarter, not both, and quantify cross-team trade-offs before springing them.

## Chapter 12: The Great Awakening, Barbarians at the Gates, IPA > IPO, IPO Reevaluation, Flash Boys

### Central argument
Advertising is name-calling: every channel is a different "name" for the same primate (postal address, device ID, cookie), and the real platform war is over who controls the joins between names. Facebook awakened in 2012, built Custom Audiences ("Vulture"/"Eagle") and Facebook Exchange ("Owl") in weeks with a handful of engineers, went public in a courtyard ceremony Martínez reads as capitalism's price-sticker ritual, and, contrary to headlines, had the best IPO in tech history because it screwed the bankers rather than the employees. FBX then proved that outside data beat Facebook's own - the "unforgivable sin."

### Full stories

**Identity in practice.** Browse at work -> interstitial on your phone on the commute -> buy at home -> or in-store with a rewards card. On-boarding links all of it, and the sale is attributed back to the mobile impression, making it worth more. Publishers are post offices: a user ID is now a better address than a street address, valuable as the key to decades of purchase history.

**The real Big Brother.** Data brokers (Acxiom, Experian, Epsilon, Merkle, Neustar) have tracked consumer America since the 1960s, ingest retailers' CRM data and sell segments no citizen can inspect. Nielsen's PRIZM segments ("Beltway Boomers"): "You consume; therefore, you are."

**Mooching.** Facebook "mooched information from potential acquirees and business partners via meetings of dubious good faith": Turn, MediaMath, BlueKai, Acxiom and Epsilon patiently explained their plumbing while privately incredulous at Facebook's cluelessness. Experian sent a junior team bearing a box of bakery cookies to a meeting about cookies.

**Selling FBX internally (March 13, 2012).** The only hour KX, Hegeman, Boland, Rabkin and a dozen engineers were free; physically rounded up five minutes before (attendance "about as flaky as a meth head's promises to quit"); Zach Coelius of Triggit, a poker player who read the author's aces from his face, held the room for an hour as an outside "voice of the market" on real-time programmatic buying; sell the gee-whiz real-time decisioning to engineers and whiteboard the business case; a rare lovefest; a written product and business case within days tied to post-IPO quarters. Result: a project approved by leadership that had never heard of retargeting, and three engineers.

**The FBX build.** Five weeks to ship by June 15 to book Q3 and Q4 revenue; three engineers (a "brogrammer," a recruit poached from Adchemy, a reviewer nothing passed without); a parallel ads system with separate data stores, real-time global bidding hundreds of thousands of times per second, dynamic creative, separate stats, monitoring tools, partner on-boarding docs and tests and a sales spiel for partners; bought with a promise of $100M in 2012 revenue. The first successful FBX bid was June 15, 2012, via TellApart; from then the race was to make FBX too big for management to kill. The cast: Boland, Rabkin (wants to please his father; the author wants to punch his), Varghese (Google PM immune to Facebook bullshit), Rosenthal ("irreverence without disrespect, competence without arrogance"), Reesman, Hari, Gary; "Trackie the Owl," a solar-powered pigeon scarecrow whose head turned unpredictably. The Brogramming page (a senior engineer's cult parody) was deleted by the CTO as the company matured.

**The 120 ms bound and the Flash Boys.** A bid must return in 120 ms including network latency. North Carolina to Amsterdam is ~23 ms one way at light speed, ~60 ms in practice, so European requests timed out and FBX lost money there: "the Flash Boys of media." DSPs unpack the request, query hyperoptimized databases of your browsing, carts and third-party segments, and bid.

**The unforgivable sin.** FBX bids fed into Facebook's own auction, making Facebook "merely one more DSP"; outside bidders - who knew you'd just shopped for a handbag - bid far higher than Facebook's Jim-Carrey-page data and won. Equality with outsiders was a feature to the author, a bug to management. The author admits naïveté: incumbents prefer monopoly pricing power and asymmetry.

**IPO night.** A mandated hackathon so employees would sleep through trading; home-brewed "IPO IPA" in the courtyard; a keg run to Willows at 1 a.m. with Boland (recruited by Sheryl with "I'll hire you now, or a year from now to work for the guy whose job I'm offering"); single malt at 5 a.m.; a rock-concert stage "with the jewels"; Zuck: ring the bell, then get back to work. The Ads floor was full that afternoon; employees posted it as a Major Life Event.

**IPO truth and price-sticker capitalism.** The syndicate underprices, hypes to deep pockets and engineers the pop; an orderly open never moves 20-30% for any ordinary stock, so a pop means founders and employees were skinned and bankers' clients made fortunes. Facebook opened ~$42, closed ~$38.37, paid ~1% in fees instead of up to 7%, and raised a mountain with minimal dilution: "the most successful tech IPO in financial history," whatever *Fortune* and *Forbes* said. The ritual is Mr. Market slapping $38 on Facebook and everyone cheering; capitalism "desacralizes everything"; Burning Man and consumption identities fill the vacuum.

**The author's package.** 75,000 shares over four years, $175K salary, 5,000 shares cashed up front: ~$971K/year at the IPO price, ~$550K after tax, "San Francisco middle class, or barely." Below fuck-you money only the indifference threshold moves (the mental decimal point slides a zero); the real transformations are fuck-you money and fuck-the-world money.

### Evidence
- **Direct mail:** ~$17B in postage; a $50B+ industry (a Google, three internets or three Facebooks of money); ~$1,000 CPMs vs. ~$1 online -> online can be a thousand times less effective and break even.
- **On-boarding:** Datalogix, Neustar, LiveRamp; hashed joins ("privacy theater" if everyone uses the same hash); Custom Audiences match rates up to ~90%.
- **FBX build:** five weeks, three engineers, $100M promised for 2012 revenue.
- **Latency:** 120 ms bid window; ~23 ms light-speed vs. ~60 ms practical NC-Amsterdam one way.
- **IPO:** ~$42 open, ~$38.37 close, ~1% fees vs. up to 7%.
- **Caveat:** the "best IPO ever" claim is Martínez's reading of who benefits, countering the press view.

### Key terms
Name-calling; identity joins; data brokers; PRIZM; direct mail; data on-boarding; Custom Audiences (Vulture/Eagle); FBX (Owl); DSP; 120 ms; Flash Boys; unforgivable sin; IPA > IPO; price-sticker machine; fuck-you money; fuck-the-world money; Atlas (introduced here, detailed in Ch 13).

### So what
Treat every marketing channel as an addressing scheme and platform wars as fights over the join. Use "who gets the pop?" to read IPO headlines. Sell an unfamiliar technology with an outsider voice, in the one free hour, then follow up in writing. Expect speed limits from physics.

## Chapter 13: Full Frontal Facebook, Microsoft Shrugged, Ad Majorem, Adios Facebook

### Central argument
Facebook's decisive monetization choice - open (FBX: standard programmatic exchange, instant adoption, outside data on equal footing) vs. closed (Custom Audiences plus an in-house ad server, the Atlas acquisition, years to build, total lock-in) - was settled on April 12, 2013 in "Only Good News" on Boz's recommendation to Sheryl: no identity matching and no mobile on FBX. Martínez, who bet his career on the open path, was pushed out days after his second vest. Facebook, like Goldman, chose monopoly and information asymmetry over innovation; middle management is what remains after the ambitious are skimmed off; he chose "to do something" over "to be someone."

### Full stories

**Great powers.** Facebook has no partners, only existential rivals (Amazon, Google, once Apple - mirrors of Zuck) and convenient alliances; negotiations with peers were arms-control treaties with red-lined contracts and mutual suspicion (would Amazon's ad data feed Facebook's models? would Facebook impressions shape Amazon recommendations?). Amazon's A9 engineers "got it, instantly" while its business side (Matt Battles) played aloof for months. Twitter didn't register ("a clown car that drove into a gold mine").

**Google Plus's numbers.** Google Plus claimed 400M registered/100M active by counting anyone who clicked a Plus button. Inside Facebook, panic gave way to the recognition of Enron-style accounting; Larry Page's ultimatum reoriented all of Google, including Search, around social; Vic Gundotra became the internal Emmanuel Goldstein.

**The Omnicom meeting (Jan 2013).** A suited retargeting executive asked when identity matching would come to FBX; Gokul's non-answer. Agencies (Omnicom's trading desk Accuen) wanted to use first- and third-party data anywhere. Gokul later realized keeping the author on CA was "contracting the fox to build the new henhouse." Manager's movie reference: "The Spartans lost at Thermopylae" - no support.

**LiveBoz (April 4) and the decision (April 12).** Boland: CA is way ahead; the author: only if you count non-incremental dollars; a pissing match and a promise of a recommendation to Sheryl. April 12: Boz recommended; Sheryl: "if that's what you think... that's what we'll do"; Gokul fled the room; FBX was on life support, and for the second time in two years the author walked out mid-afternoon with nothing to do. The Fidelity analogy for the closed plan: owning not just the funds but the exchange, brokers, trading tool, bank and credit card - viable only if the identity data is so superior that advertisers accept lock-in and years of inferior tooling; otherwise "Sponsored Stories Part II."

**Atlas.** Microsoft's creaky ad server (ran only on certain Internet Explorer versions; 20% share; thousands of publishers) bought for roughly the $0.1B left after Microsoft wrote down $6.1B of its $6.2B aQuantive purchase: "Facebook's biggest ad tech acquisition to date was Microsoft's accounting rounding error." Engineered by Boland and ex-Microsoft colleagues; "it's not what you pay, it's what they cost you": two-plus years of delays.

**Court politics.** Proximity to Zuck is institutional power; Friends of Zuck (Boz, Ross, Shaffer, Lessin) survive product failures. Boz - News Feed's engineer, "punch them in the face" old-timer, a conference room named LiveBoz - was dispatched to end Ads' Lord-of-the-Flies anarchy, learned ad tech by sitting in client meetings, and reconfigured Ads along one axis, from "Boz likes you" to "fired." Why middle managers are what they are: negative selection - the ambitious leave; the rest master managing up, acting visibly on feedback, championing reports, allying with sales, routing around incompetence, banking the logo on the business card. The review: "You're a very divisive figure": team gushes, management decries. Truth on a forearm ("veritas") often accompanies a well-groomed pack of lies.

**The exit (April 15-16).** Email vacation for Monday the 15th (vesting day). Weekend: desk cleared, kegerator removed. Tuesday (the 16th): shares confirmed in Schwab; disk wiped; a suspiciously punctual one-on-one revealed HR with a severance-and-silence document ($30,000 for a year of silence; decline to sign on the spot). The Goodfellas "getting made" analogy; Miss HR's script; the poached partner employee in the lobby; the Mustang GT peeling out; Gokul's chat, "Remember the non-solicit! (seriously)"; Gokul and several PMs gone within two months. The handshake at the last one-on-one was "of a last meeting with the mafia capo." Facebook in late 2012 was in its adolescence, between startup and big company, "never to be repeated."

**Do something or be someone.** Via Col. Boyd: impact and management's scorn vs. plum products, facile praise and forgettable work. With tuition riding on unvested stock the author chose impact: "double down and dig in."

### Evidence
- **Incrementality:** CA's dashboard dollars largely from existing "Facebook" budget lines (discount by half); FBX competed for budgets earmarked for Google's AdX (100% incremental) but required new creative and reallocation from burned retailers before Q4.
- **Atlas arithmetic:** $6.2B purchase, $6.1B written down, ~$0.1B price; 20% share; thousands of publishers.
- **Google Plus:** 400M registered / 100M active by counting any Plus-button click.
- **Ad server definitions:** Google counts clicks only; Facebook wants viewthrough credit.
- **Severance:** $30,000 for a year of non-disparagement.
- **Caveat:** the open-vs-closed argument rests on the author's participation and incrementality accounting; Ch 14 shows the outcome was decided by mobile.

### Key terms
Great powers; Thermopylae; do something vs. be someone; data portability / walled garden; incrementality; containers / native formats; ad server (source of truth); viewthrough vs. clickthrough; Atlas; court politics / Friends of Zuck; negative selection; exit playbook.

### So what
Ask whether a dollar is incremental before crediting any dashboard. Treat an ad server as the strategic oracle. Don't bet a career on winning an argument with money against a fixed narrative. Plan your exit around the vest: vacation over the vesting date, desk cleared, disk wiped, don't sign silence on the spot.

## Chapter 14: Pandemonium Lost, Epilogue: Man Plans and God Laughs

### Central argument
The aftermath: Twitter bought MoPub to build the exchange Facebook refused to, and hired the author as a secret adviser while he was simultaneously VP of Product at Facebook's largest ads partner. Neither FBX nor Custom Audiences saved Facebook; mobile News Feed ads did, for structural reasons nobody predicted. FBX grew to ~$500M with no further investment and survives as Facebook's periscope into programmatic; Google surrendered; the AdGrok engineers got rich at Twitter; the author, taxed at $38 on stock sold near $20, got "practically nothing" and left to write and sail.

### Full stories

**The couch and the Medium post.** After a plantar fascia rupture (eight weeks immobilized after a drinking spree), the couch produced the MoPub essay. MoPub was the leading real-time mobile exchange (Jim Payne, Herman Yang) that Facebook had passed on (~$350M deal); the author had begged Facebook to put mobile on FBX. Executives in an acquisition can't explain their strategy publicly (FTC, secrecy) but can retweet a perceptive outsider: the post was circulated by the deal's principals, reopening a door slammed two years earlier.

**The reconciliation meeting.** At Twitter's new office at Ninth and Market ("the Twitterloin"; the same guard taking the badge) he gave Twitter the full whiteboard - data flows, cross-device de-duplication, leakage-safe targeting: "if in doubt, show more." An advisory offer followed within days (a thousand shares a quarter, ~$160K/year, no badge, no email). Dick Costolo's email had explained the AdGrok defection to all of Twitter as "a craven character flaw." Simultaneously he was VP of Product at Nanigans, Facebook's largest monetization partner, whose product team built real-time buying for Google and Twitter-MoPub, "a complete grudge job"; "just shy" of corporate treason, "which was how Silicon Valley worked." Confirmation of the Land of the Stateless Machines.

**Why mobile saved Facebook.** (1) Data: mobile browsers block third-party cookies, apps silo their data, Apple polices device IDs, developers hoard. Desktop had a mature data marketplace that made Facebook's data marginal; mobile was data-starved, even age/gender was a godsend, and Audience Network's strong performance proved "in the land of the mobile data blind, the one-eyed Facebook man was king." (2) Inventory: desktop's prestige publishers used ads as a crutch, but mobile's savvy monetizers (games, commerce) charged users directly, leaving ad exchanges with tiny bars, interstitials and Grindr; Facebook's stylish in-feed units with single-digit-percent clickthroughs had no real competition. Neko / News Feed Ads, championed by Fidji Simo (ex-product marketer, "half Sicilian, half French"), rescued the stock. Q2 2013: mobile actives up 50%+, mobile revenue roughly doubled - the rally trigger. Opening mobile News Feed to ads was a Louisiana Purchase land rush; insiders projected when pioneers would hit the Pacific, but revenue kept growing after saturation because better delivery math and rising demand for a limited commodity raised prices; Facebook re-geared the Ads team to mobile in one of its breathtaking pivots.

**Ten dice.** The Valley's success generator: try ten things on hunches and mythology; seven fail and are forgotten, two do OK, one becomes transformational for reasons discovered afterward; the press writes the narrative fallacy; you're crowned a genius and roll again until the dealer sweeps your chips - and your logo becomes someone else's memento mori.

**Buying your way out.** Instagram (2012, $1B, a weekend, a growth curve like early Facebook's; Dorsey outmaneuvered) and WhatsApp (2014, $19B, phone number as identity). Someday a founder as proud as Zuck will refuse, and Facebook will have to build, "a far riskier proposition."

**FBX's afterlife.** ~$500M by early 2014, all incremental, second only to News Feed ads, built by a handful and now costing nothing: a "perpetual motion machine of cash" and Facebook's window into programmatic competitors (like Goldman watching order flow); kept alive because too profitable to kill, starved of identity and mobile to push advertisers toward clunky in-house tools. The prediction: Facebook is "on the wrong side of technological history" and will run a full exchange; even Goldman moved CDS clearing to ICE in 2013.

**Google's Punic defeat.** Gundotra's April 2014 exit and Google Plus's demotion to a "platform": "an army... advancing in reverse." Facebook's lockdown had won.

**Karma and compensation.** Twitter's options, a post-lockout price above $50 and four-plus years of vesting made the AdGrok engineers rich; Facebook's restricted stock taxed as income at a $38 basis, a lockout that expired near $20 and a two-year ejection left the author "paying taxes on money I hadn't made." Twitter's January 2016 shake-up: Weil and Roetter out (Weil to Instagram); Bain still COO; Jess still buying companies "like so many canned tomatoes." The AdGrok boys (March 2016) were still at Twitter, fully vested; MRM's mortgage paid; Argyris with apartments in San Francisco and Athens; relations "more or less" repaired.

**Willing dreams into existence.** After his mother's death (summer 2014) he sold everything, finished the book and outfitted *Ayala*, a forty-foot cutter, to sail around the world alone: turning the entrepreneur's skill of willing things into being toward his own dreams. Anti-pattern: neglecting the boat while chasing the exit - twice.

### Evidence
- **FBX:** ~$500M new revenue by early 2014, all incremental.
- **Mobile results:** Q2 2013 mobile actives +50%+, mobile revenue roughly doubled.
- **Instagram $1B (2012); WhatsApp $19B (2014); MoPub ~$350M.**
- **Compensation:** $38 tax basis vs. ~$20 sale price; Twitter post-lockout price above $50; advisory offer ~$160K/yr.
- **Desktop vs. mobile worked example:** same data, different market structure, opposite outcome (Safari blocks third-party cookies; Apple restricts device IDs; apps keep their own).
- **Caveat:** the "why mobile won" account is retrospective by the author's own admission (a narrative, not foresight); the notes' "narrative fallacy" warning applies to it too.

### Key terms
Outsider post as mouthpiece; show enough skin; Land of the Stateless Machines; grudge job; Audience Network; land rush; ten dice; narrative fallacy; perpetual motion machine of cash; Punic defeat; lockout; restricted stock vs. options.

### So what
Being right about the technology is not being right about what saves the quarter. Market structure decides what data is worth. Compensation form, tax timing, lockouts and tenure decide who gets rich. Discount founder genius with the ten-dice test. Keep a life beyond the exit.

# PART 3 - Key learnings

## (a) Core concepts and frameworks

Each entry: definition, when to use, common mistake. (All drawn from the skill's notes.)

1. **Price on perception.** A CDS prices a belief about a company; an ad prices a slice of attention. *Use:* mapping finance skills to ad tech, decoding what a data business sells, asking "who makes the price?" *Mistake:* assuming the data business is about the data rather than who owns the joins and prices.

2. **Incumbents keep markets inefficient.** Dominant players prefer owning a small, asymmetric market to joining a large transparent one (Goldman/CDS; Facebook/FBX). *Use:* predict what an incumbent will do when an open standard threatens its pricing power. *Mistake:* expecting an incumbent to embrace a better product that erodes its asymmetry (the author's self-confessed naïveté).

3. **Miracle count.** Zero = business; one = startup; two or more = bad idea. *Use:* any pitch, investment, job offer or monetization plan. *Mistake:* mistaking a one-miracle consumer app for a safe bet (still a usage lottery), or ignoring hidden second miracles (Sponsored Stories needed two).

4. **Diagnose a doomed startup.** Only the founder survives; big revenue unrelated to the product; rotating beta logos; loyalty from Stockholm syndrome. *Use:* before joining. *Mistake:* believing the Team page or trusting alumni verdicts.

5. **Have a leader.** One person with 51%+ or a genuine mind-meld; CEO as the only early title. *Use:* founding teams. *Mistake:* equal thirds as "fairness," producing paralysis and factions.

6. **Seed notes, caps and dilution.** Notes convert at the next round; the cap fixes the maximum conversion valuation ($100K at $3M = 3.3% whatever the A). *Use:* negotiating seed terms; remember it is a proxy, not a valuation. *Mistake:* treating a cap as a valuation, or ignoring dilution (AdGrok sold ~22%).

7. **Decision-making power test.** Ask a VC "Do you have decision-making power?"; any hedge means a lackey. *Use:* routing to the person who can defend the deal at the Monday partner meeting. *Mistake:* being impressed by titles.

8. **Angel vs. micro-VC incentives.** Own-money angels take 2-4x; fund managers need outsized returns and push you to refuse good-enough exits. *Use:* predicting an investor's advice; use VC as cudgel when needed. *Mistake:* letting an investor with a stake in the buyer run your process.

9. **Giant's weak points.** Investors and prospective partners. *Use:* any dispute with a larger firm; firewall your team; find a two-hop connector. *Mistake:* attacking the claims in court only; letting the crisis into the team's headspace.

10. **Mispriced option for legal fees.** Pay a first-tier firm in equity with a cheap buyback (Fenwick: $250K fronted). *Use:* when cash is zero. *Mistake:* paying cash-only solo lawyers who end up costing more.

11. **Content marketing launch.** Pick the biggest nerve; Tuesday morning post; seed upvotes; reply to fans; egg on trolls; double down. *Mistake:* a single server for a launch you hope goes viral.

12. **Stalk before every meeting; always be closing.** Social-media workup of every counterpart and interviewer; demo that runs on any laptop. *Mistake:* arriving without a demo.

13. **Create the auction before the no-shop.** Get every plausible acquirer "in play"; bluff with the name everyone fears only if the target can't verify; run a clandestine phone channel. *Mistake:* waiting until the term sheet lands.

14. **Acqui-hire pricing.** Value per engineer ($0.5-2M in 2011 equity), not company; replicating portfolio; the split is the founders' own-wallet decision; resign before you sign. *Mistake:* assuming pro rata or valuing the headline at face.

15. **Vesting.** Four years, 25% at one year, then quarterly; founders own nothing early. *Use:* halve a big-company offer (you'll leave in two years), halve again for stock vs. options. *Mistake:* valuing the four-year headline; quitting before the vest.

16. **Call-station tempo.** A fast callback signals a weak hand; an acquirer's speed is a function of the fire under it. *Mistake:* missing what tempo reveals.

17. **PM craft at a big company.** Tiebreaker; layer new on old; bias for launching; build a dashboard to the decimal; technical depth to detect BS; verify even in production. *Mistake:* forcing a new paradigm or losing engineers' confidence.

18. **Data per pixel and the noisy bar.** Targeting turns data into cash; social chatter is mostly commercially inert. *Use:* judging ad-funded products. *Mistake:* equating usage with monetization.

19. **Revenue-impact threshold.** At scale a new ads idea must move ~5% of revenue (~$100M/year then). *Use:* kill niche ideas early. *Mistake:* whizzy niche cleverness.

20. **Blue-tape loop and memento mori.** Experiment, fail fast, prune, encode the lesson; keep reminders of mortality. *Mistake:* handing a mob paint with no direction.

21. **Cognitive dissonance in product orgs.** Organizations ignore contradicting data and push harder. *Use:* an alarm for plans no one measures. *Mistake:* champagne with a revenue target on it.

22. **Lift vs. the best alternative.** A lift is meaningful only against the best outside option (40-60% on a 20x deficit is nothing). *Mistake:* citing a lift from your own weak baseline.

23. **Advertising as name-calling and identity joins.** Postal address, device ID, cookie; the platform war is over the join. *Use:* analyzing marketing channels. *Mistake:* believing "Facebook sells your data."

24. **Incrementality.** Count only dollars that wouldn't otherwise have landed on the platform. *Mistake:* crediting a dashboard of reallocated budget.

25. **Ad server as source of truth.** The oracle of delivery, frequency and geography, defining the units of value; Google counts clicks, Facebook wants viewthroughs. *Mistake:* underestimating the lock-in value of owning the yardstick, or the cost of feeding acquired legacy tech.

26. **Reading an IPO.** A first-day pop means bankers and their clients won; a flat open with ~1% fees is the win. *Mistake:* reading a pop as success.

27. **Open vs. closed monetization.** Open = fast, incremental, shared power; closed = slow, lock-in, total control; expect incumbents to choose closed. *Mistake:* betting a career on winning an internal argument with money against a fixed narrative.

28. **Market structure decides data value.** Same data was marginal on desktop and gold on mobile. *Mistake:* assuming a thesis transfers across markets.

29. **Ten dice.** Ten bets, seven fail, two OK, one wins, narrative written afterward. *Use:* discount retrospective genius. *Mistake:* resulting-style thinking about founders' brilliance.

30. **Exit playbook.** Never quit before the vest; vacation over the vesting date; clear the desk early; secure-erase; don't sign silence on the spot. *Mistake:* trusting the manager who reminds you of the one-on-one for the first time.

## (b) The big ideas of the book (numbered)

1. **Ad tech is finance for attention.** The same quant skills and price-making dynamics apply; a CDS prices belief about a company, an ad prices a slice of attention. *Appears:* Ch 1, Ch 12, Ch 14 (the Goldman-to-ICE arc).

2. **Incumbents prefer asymmetry to competition.** Goldman wouldn't move CDS to exchanges; Facebook chose closed over FBX. Even so, both eventually faced exchange-like futures. *Appears:* Ch 1, 12, 13, 14.

3. **Computers replace decision-makers, humans fill gaps.** SLK went from hundreds of traders to two plus twenty quants; RTB is the ad version. *Appears:* Ch 1, 12.

4. **Ideas are worthless; teams are what investors buy.** YC judges the team; "stealth" is a loser tattoo; an acqui-hire prices heads. *Appears:* Ch 2, 3, 7.

5. **Count the miracles.** The number of things that must go right predicts failure. *Appears:* Ch 2, 11.

6. **Every real startup problem is a people problem, so have a leader.** Equal splits create paralysis; people "become better actors." *Appears:* Ch 3, 5, 7.

7. **Leverage beats resources.** A broke startup beat a $130M-funded incumbent by attacking its investors and partners through a loyal network. *Appears:* Ch 4, 5.

8. **Nobody owns anything until they vest; acquirers price people.** The headline price is mostly retention for founders; founders decide the investor split from their own wallet. *Appears:* Ch 7, 10, 14.

9. **Tempo and information asymmetry decide negotiations.** Stalk everyone, create the auction, bluff where unverifiable, read speed of response. *Appears:* Ch 6, 7.

10. **Facebook is a mission cult whose believers are the moat and the danger.** Onboarding is conversion; true believers can't be bought; the culture resembles revolutionary regimes. *Appears:* Ch 8, 10.

11. **Data richness is not data value.** Social data is a noisy bar; what advertisers want is first-party purchase and browsing data, mostly from outside Facebook; embarrassing does not mean valuable. *Appears:* Ch 1, 8, 9, 12.

12. **Judge products by incrementality and by lift against the best alternative.** Sponsored Stories' lift was irrelevant; CA's dollars were half non-incremental; FBX was fully incremental. *Appears:* Ch 11, 13.

13. **Identity is the strategic high ground of digital marketing.** Advertising is name-calling; the war is over the joins; data brokers and direct mail's $50B are the real prize. *Appears:* Ch 12.

14. **Structural luck beats strategy; narratives are written afterward.** Mobile News Feed ads saved Facebook for reasons nobody argued about; ten dice, one winner. *Appears:* Ch 14.

15. **Wealth outcomes are decided by structure, not talent.** Compensation form, tax timing, lockouts and tenure; the haves and have-mores; fuck-you money and fuck-the-world money. *Appears:* Ch 10, 12, 14.

## (d) Anti-patterns to avoid

From the notes' per-chapter anti-patterns:
- Mistaking a rich data store for commercially useful data (Ch 1).
- Trading security for a collar; building tech to support a CEO's bullshit without keeping the knowledge (Ch 1).
- Believing the Team page; trusting alum feedback or founder harangues as verdicts; blurting your legal workaround to the party that wants to hurt you (Ch 2).
- Equal equity as "fairness"; a single server for a launch; chasing the SMB market; demoing when you should pitch the business (Ch 3).
- Arriving late and lost to a lead investor; letting cofounders break the demo build before a pitch; giggling at an acquisition question; showing the real Death Clock to anyone (Ch 4).
- Letting the crisis into the team's headspace; assuming intelligence or vision is scarce; believing the launch spike; picking a fight you can't finish (Ch 5).
- Showing up without a demo; letting a buyer-invested investor run your process; wimping out on the call; hiring by "cultural fit" (Ch 6).
- Assuming pro rata; Sacca's dependence (investor with a stake in the buyer as adviser); Twitter's call-station reflex (Ch 7).
- Mistaking usage for monetization; insular teams; PMs who lose engineers' confidence; forcing users onto a new paradigm (Ch 8).
- Believing an engineer's "we're not doing that" without checking; placeholders in executive demos; publicizing safety wins when the frame backfires; pricing a marketplace good by fiat (Ch 9).
- Handing a mob paint with no direction; valuing an acqui-hire at its four-year headline; middle managers identifying with owners; sleeping through your own deceleration (Ch 10).
- Two-miracle monetization strategies; champagne with a revenue target on it; selling the on-ramp (LOX); springing cross-team trade-offs unquantified (Ch 11).
- Believing "Facebook sells your data"; sending the B team to the join of the century; promising revenue to buy headcount and getting three engineers; forgetting that incumbents choose asymmetry (Ch 12).
- Betting a career on winning an internal argument with money; ignoring incrementality; buying legacy tech and underestimating the cost of feeding it; trusting the manager who first reminds you of a one-on-one (Ch 13).
- Assuming the winning product was the one being fought over; cashing out restricted stock through a lockout into a falling price; neglecting the boat while chasing the exit (Ch 14).

## (e) If you remember only ten things

1. Ad tech is finance for attention; ask who makes the price.
2. Incumbents prefer owning a small asymmetric market to competing in a big transparent one.
3. Count the miracles: zero is a business, one a startup, two a bad idea.
4. Have a leader (51%+ or a true mind-meld); every startup problem is a people problem.
5. Negotiate the cap, not the valuation; ask "do you have decision-making power?"
6. A giant's weak points are its investors and partners; firewall your team.
7. Acquirers price people, founders own nothing until they vest: halve the headline, halve again for stock, resign before you sign.
8. Facebook's social data was commercially inert; outside first-party data (purchases, browsing) is what advertisers want, and the war is over identity joins.
9. Count only incremental dollars, and measure lift against the best outside alternative.
10. Structural luck (mobile) and ten-dice narratives beat strategy; vest first, then leave.

## (f) Open questions, limits and conflicts the notes flag or contain

**Limits flagged by the notes**
- The skill covers the book content only (events 2007-2016); ad tech, Facebook's products and privacy rules have changed substantially since, so treat CPMs, cookies and match rates as period data.
- The chapters are groupings of the book's 46 sections; these notes are summaries and not the book's wording.
- The notes treat the "why mobile won" account as retrospective: it is a structure described after the fact, not foresight, and the narrative-fallacy warning applies to the author's own account as well.
- Much of the evidence is anecdote and first-person assertion (founder traits, "bullies smell weakness," the "People's Republic" comparison). Only a few items are quantified (CPMs, clickthrough, caps, IPO prices, FBX revenue).
- Some claims are the author's reading against press consensus (e.g., the IPO as "best in tech history"), not tested.
- A morally ambivalent stance: the notes endorse lying to investors and acquirers if necessary, yet hold that deceiving cofounders is the line - while recording that the author concealed the Death Clock from cofounders and lied to them for two weeks during the acquisition. The notes do not reconcile this.
