# Expect to Win: Key Arguments, Stories & Evidence by Chapter
*Carla A. Harris*

Each chapter has: **Argument** (the claim), **Stories & evidence** (the anecdotes and cases the book uses), **Key numbers**, and **So what** (the lesson). Built from the full text of the chapter, glossary, patterns, and cheatsheet files in the `harris-expect-to-win` skill, so it reflects that summary of the book, not the book's own wording. The book is a practitioner's advice book: its evidence is mostly Harris's own stories and others' cases, not studies.

---

## The Big Arc in One Paragraph
Harris's premise is that no organization is a true meritocracy, because people evaluate people, so hard work and intelligence are necessary but not sufficient. Ch 1–2 lay the foundation: be your authentic self and write your own career agenda. Ch 3–4 cover how you are judged: the first ninety days and the perceptions people form of you. Ch 5–6 cover the people and the voice that move you forward: sponsors, mentors, advisers, and speaking up and asking. Ch 7–8 add the other terms of the success equation (risk) and the network that carries you. Ch 9–11 turn inward: integrating passions and giving back, expecting to win, and knowing whose you are, the faith that steadies you when timing and others' decisions are out of your control.

## Ch 1. Authenticity: The Power Is You
**Argument:** You were hired because your particular combination of skills, personality, and values beat every other candidate; that is your only durable competitive advantage. Impersonating others or staying silent to seem safe spends mental capacity, reads as lack of confidence, and costs promotions. Authenticity also means choosing an environment whose written and unwritten rules you can honestly meet.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Carla's turnaround pitch:** after repeated "flat and rote" feedback, she prepared around the client (what do they need, what would I worry about in their shoes, educate or convince?) and held a conversation with a Fortune 500 CEO, CFO, COO, and head of HR, backed by a colleague who spent political capital to get her in. The CFO called the senior banker the next day with rave reviews. "The real Carla was my best competitive weapon."
- **Marissa:** a Spanish-speaking Latina who suppressed her identity so fully that leadership never considered her for the Latin America role.
- **Peter:** charismatic outside, quiet inside; eighteen years in, no promotion in eight.
- **Anne:** 17 years writing financial marketing, good reviews, no passion; kept her job, earned a journalism master's at night over 3.5 years, networked, met an author needing a ghostwriter at an event hosted by her own firm, and became a freelance writer.
- **Carla's own early mistake:** preparing hard and then sitting silent in meetings, and losing confidence under an insecure boss.

**Key numbers:** 12–24 months for a deliberate plan if you are in the wrong seat; 3.5 years for Anne's degree.

**So what:** Know your strengths, weaknesses, and why you are in this seat (the elevator speech). Prepare for clients around their needs and aim for conversation. Bring outside interests to work as relationship bridges. If the building is wrong, build a plan rather than just a complaint.

## Ch 2. Be the Architect of Your Own Agenda
**Argument:** Write a career agenda (macro goals, micro goals, compensation and promotion targets, a timeline) and use it as the decision filter for every setback and offer. The goals are fixed; the strategy flexes. Own mistakes once and move on, stop blaming "they," and line up with your corporate-ecosystem type.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Sherry's naked short:** a largely self-taught associate guessed on share allocation, the stock rose, and covering cost the firm revenue. Her boss kept raising it for weeks. One conversation (own it, state the lesson, say it is time to move on, ask for support) ended it and earned coaching.
- **Carla's operations-officer stint:** an off-line role that produced a future president of Morgan Stanley as her sponsor.
- **Gina:** rejected a small new product judged on current revenue; waited six more years while the person who took it was promoted in three.
- **Hazel vs. Curtis:** resenting a peer's earlier promotion when he had made sacrifices (Hong Kong, uprooting family) she chose not to make.
- **Carla's demeaning manager:** she worked for him repeatedly because she did not know she could decline projects.
- **Political maniacs vs. workhorses:** people value the opposite type but reward their own.

**Key numbers:** industry norms of 3–4 years to VP and 8–12 to MD (a guide, not law).

**So what:** Measure yourself against your own agenda, not a peer's report card. Swap "they" for "What did I contribute?" Say yes to unglamorous roles that buy exposure. Ask for the pay you deserve and what the top bracket requires. Engage decision-makers when passed over to create an IOU rather than restarting political capital elsewhere.

## Ch 3. You Are the Captain of Your Career: The Ninety-Day Rule
**Argument:** You have about a quarter in any new job to create the impression of your value. Organizations assess can do, will do, and fit, and fit is the most subjective and decisive. The first-quarter impression sets a halo (or its opposite) that shapes trajectory.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Presentation-skills sprint:** two years into banking Carla realized she lacked the board-room presentation experience VP required. Quarter one: internal reps (campus recruiting presentations, shadowing strong presenters). Quarter two: external reps (finance/treasury committees at nonprofits). Presenting was no longer an impediment by the VP decision.
- **Social rhythm:** she adopted M&A's late nights and capital markets' 7 a.m. starts; she phoned her sports-fan father every Sunday to open Monday's conversation.
- **Informal hazing:** up-or-out groups may not invite the new person; the test is passed by inviting yourself.
- **Day-91 check-in template:** lead with proficiency attained, whom you enjoyed learning from, and the plan for next quarter; ask what to add or change.

**Key numbers:** 90 days to prove value; day 91 to advertise; 6 months to be on par; one year on par or ahead; 3 deliverables; perform at the next level 6–12 months before wanting the title; days 91–180 to become the go-to person.

**So what:** Calendar the three deliverables (skill, unspoken rules, key relationships). Never say "no one taught me." Request the deals and seniors you need. Ask the decision-maker what advancement requires.

## Ch 4. Perception Is the Copilot to Reality
**Argument:** People treat you as who they think you are, and because evaluation has a subjective component, perception decides assignments, pay, and promotion. Perception equals your self-presentation plus the beholder's baggage; change the first, deliberately.

**Stories & evidence**
- **The "tough" campaign:** told "I don't know if you're tough enough," Carla worked *tough* into her speech and acted congruently for about three months, until a VP was overheard warning his team to double-check assumptions because "Carla Harris is so tough."
- **The associate who wanted VP:** strong objectively, yet treated as an associate. Prescription: dress like the women officers and pre-meet her boss before group meetings. She was promoted, though Carla cautions you can't dress your way to success.
- **Perception noise** is especially dangerous in small organizations where one vocal person's view becomes everyone's.
- **Adjectives by level:** junior (eager, resourceful, analytical), mid-level (gets things done, mobilizes others, strategic potential), senior (visionary, commercial, client-focused, authority).

**Key numbers:** at least 3 adjectives; about 3 months to change a perception; 2–3 fans plus 2–3 non-fans in the audit.

**So what:** Pick adjectives true of you and valued by the firm's business model, behave consistently, audit your perception between annual reviews, meet non-fans over coffee, fix the boss's perception first, and refresh adjectives as you rise.

## Ch 5. The Mentor, the Sponsor, the Adviser
**Argument:** Three different relationships power a career and most people conflate them. Advisers answer discrete questions, mentors give tailored advice and hear everything, sponsors spend political capital to carry your paper into the room. Long-term advancement without a sponsor is nearly impossible, and finding one is your responsibility.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Carla's 1999 MD promotion:** unable to name who would argue her case, she asked a senior MD with a committee seat directly and promised him the strongest package; she added a second advocate with influence over seat-holders. Promoted.
- **Reciprocity:** a former mentee, now close to a client firm's decision-maker, gave her team post-pitch feedback and vouched for their integrity; they were named a book runner.
- **Boss capital:** a boss may have capital for one or two protégés and four or five who deserve it.
- **Women and women of color** are less often chosen for mentoring because relationships form along lines of commonality.

**Key numbers:** 8–10 mentor contacts per year (Carla: every six to eight weeks); at least 2 explicit sponsor commitments before joining or switching divisions; 4 sponsor criteria.

**So what:** Ask "Who is carrying my paper into the room?" and go recruit one if you can't answer. Test mentors against your agenda alone. Build a diverse career board of directors. Follow through and follow up, and reciprocate.

## Ch 6. Leverage Your Voice
**Argument:** Voice creates perception, sets the expectation of your contribution, and is the mechanism for stating what you expect in return. Silence and head-down hard work are not safe: in marginal decisions, those who voiced expectations win.

**Stories & evidence**
- **The eureka year:** in a tough year, Carla worked hard, said nothing, and was not promoted. Her boss said peers had been pleading their cases for months.
- **The listening turnaround:** told her boss hated giving her feedback because "she has an answer for everything," she replied only with "yes," "uh-huh," and "oh really? okay." Feedback became specific; the boss became adviser, then sponsor, and discounted false claims about her.
- **"Frequently wrong, but never in doubt"**, adapted: answer confidently with room to adjust.
- **Word-framing:** "There are three reasons…" (analytical); "This called for creative thinking" (creative).
- **Strategic silence:** once you have a voice, not speaking is noticed ("Carla, you're quiet").

**Key numbers:** 50/50 odds before you speak; evaluation one-third lessons, two-thirds aspirations; goal timelines of 3–6 months; check-in every 2 months; report card done by month 6.

**So what:** Say something in every meeting, ask for next year's promotion this year, run reviews forward-looking with explicit asks, listen without defending, and thank the giver of feedback.

## Ch 7. Have a Penchant for Taking Risks
**Argument:** Playing it safe won't get you ahead; with knowledge widely available, willingness to take risk differentiates you. Success equals performance plus political alignment plus risk. You can't prevent latent risk but can offset it by stacking forward risks.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Carrie (calculated):** two years past ready for SVP; she met her boss's boss first, then told her boss, having mapped the possible reactions and prepared to search externally. Promoted.
- **Carla's Internet IPO (studied):** oversubscribed book, thin investor feedback; she recommended waiting. The next day NASDAQ fell over 100 points and most Internet IPOs priced that day traded below issue; the client returned months later and priced successfully.
- **Risk of saying no:** in her MD year she declined her boss's request to launch a retail-distribution effort, with a rationale ready, and was promoted with his sponsorship intact.
- **Observation:** nobody who took a well-thought-out risk regretted it; almost everyone regrets a risk not taken.

**Key numbers:** 3 risk types (calculated, studied, step-out-on-faith); 3 test-the-waters questions (all yes means go); 12-month comparison horizon.

**So what:** Prefer calculated risks, take studied risks only when upside markedly dominates, keep faith risks for when you alone bear consequences. Take same-pay bigger jobs as platforms and better-paid moves as compensated risk. Title doesn't limit risk, self-imposed limits do.

## Ch 8. Power in the Network
**Argument:** No one moves up alone, and because many decisions aren't made on merit, who you know decides between equally qualified candidates. A network's power lies in relationship strength and your role, not card count.

**Stories & evidence**
- **The first CD (1999):** a chain of existing relationships (banker-musician, studio-owning colleague, Broadway musical director/producer, senior sponsor walking her to a music-label CEO, PR contacts) produced and distributed a gospel album to raise money for two schools; it also became a conversation piece with clients.
- **Closing the information gap:** surrounded by former analysts with a ready-made network, she targeted a classmate and a former analyst, invested in meals and drinks, asked for help, and offered her external network in return.
- **Recruiting as exposure:** captaining a recruiting team gave her reasons to call senior people.
- **Town-hall play:** front row, prepared question, introduce yourself afterward.

**Key numbers:** 4 relationship types; 4 roles of the lateral network; 1 face-to-face plus 4 calls a year for key contacts; 1–2 e-mails a year for others.

**So what:** Build all four rings, decide your role, honor everyone's power (especially assistants), ask peers for help, gravitate toward those ahead of you, and treat external service as investment time.

## Ch 9. Balance Is a Necessity
**Argument:** Balance is integrating your passions and giving back into your professional life. Without something outside the job that makes you feel alive, your life becomes a function of somebody else's day.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Gospel CDs and Carnegie Hall:** proceeds went to two schools (one in Harlem, one in Jacksonville). After twelve years with no press mention, the 2000 CD brought a Sunday New York Times Business feature and a 2006 Wall Street Journal half-page on her first Carnegie Hall concert. Colleagues congratulated her; clients called to thank her for speaking openly about her faith.
- **Calendarizing:** map the day, take two lunches a week for joy, and batch reading and e-mail (continuous e-mail is the biggest time leak in her experience).
- **Community service** as common ground with clients.

**Key numbers:** lunch is 5–10 hours a week; 2 lunches a week for passions; e-mail in several fixed half-hour slots; several hours a day recovered.

**So what:** Put something that brings joy in every day, give back out of gratitude (not in order to get), and talk about your passions and service at work.

## Ch 10. Expect to Win
**Argument:** Approach your career expecting to succeed and you will; approach it with "maybe" and you get mediocre results. Expectation is a daily decision that shapes body language and speech, and competitive environments exploit doubt.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Know to maybe:** at eighteen she knew she'd reach Harvard, HBS, and a top firm; after bad deals and disappointing pay her self-talk became maybe and hope.
- **The Cleveland hotel room:** the night before her birthday on a business trip, she ran the reflection she first did at thirteen, wrote who she had been and who she wanted to be, and chose the agenda and a turnaround plan over dwelling on lost money and deals.
- **Syndicate desk:** she often met a CEO and CFO only two hours before a road show she'd run over ten days; she walked in expecting to build trust and "in almost every case, it worked."

**Key numbers:** 5 principles of the winner's lens; 4 reflection sheets; age 13 (first reflection), age 18 (the "know").

**So what:** Decide each morning to be triumphant, act "as if," run the four-sheet reflection yearly, lift someone else when low, revisit the agenda when complacent, and welcome change without being wedded to specifics.

## Ch 11. My Most Precious Pearl
**Argument:** The eleventh pearl is knowing whose you are as well as who you are. Faith (for Harris, Christian; for any reader, something above the job) is the platform for managing what preparation can't control: timing, other people's decisions, a sponsor's sudden exit, illness.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Second-year associate:** working under an insecure manager uninterested in training her, she was told by a friend's mother to read Psalm 91 and Psalm 4 daily; they became mantra, armor, and comfort.
- **Scripture-to-principle mappings:** Philippians 4:13 (no challenge is insurmountable); Philippians 4:6–8 (don't worry); Isaiah 54:17 (those who wish me ill won't prevail); Romans 8:28 (it will work out for my benefit); Isaiah 41:10 (nothing to fear); Hebrews 13:5 (no battle alone).
- **Lesson or blessing:** every career event is one or the other.

**Key numbers:** none given beyond the two psalms and the verse mappings.

**So what:** Anchor identity outside the organization, expect some things to be beyond control, classify events as lesson or blessing, give your best daily, and diagnose when you can't.
