# PART 2: Chapter by Chapter

## Introduction (as covered in the notes)

The notes cover the Introduction only through SKILL.md and the glossary: Harris's premise is that **no organization is a true meritocracy**, because people evaluate people. Hard work and intelligence are necessary but not sufficient; the success equation needs strategy, relationships, politics, tenacity and faith. The book delivers "pearls," battle-tested tactics from twenty-plus years on Wall Street, aimed especially (but not only) at women and women of color. "Carla's Pearls" are the end-of-chapter takeaways. The notes contain no separate Introduction file; nothing more is claimed.

## Chapter 1: Authenticity, the Power Is You

**Central argument.** You were hired because the specific combination of your skills, personality and values beat every other candidate. That combination is your only durable competitive advantage. Suppressing it at work costs you confidence, trust, freedom to learn and promotions, because confidence is what makes clients buy, colleagues trust and bosses promote. Authenticity also means choosing an environment whose rules you can honestly satisfy.

**Full stories.**

*Carla's turnaround pitch (worked example).* Early in her career Carla repeatedly received feedback that her presentations were "flat and rote." She rebuilt her preparation around the client rather than her colleagues' opinion of her delivery, asking three questions: What information do they need? What would I worry about in their shoes? Do they need educating or convincing? She then walked into a Fortune 500 IPO meeting with the CEO, CFO, COO and head of HR. A trusted colleague had spent political capital to get her in the room. Rather than presenting *to* them she had a conversation *with* them. The next day the CFO called the senior banker with rave reviews. Lessons: conversation beats presentation, and "the real Carla was my best competitive weapon."

*Carla's early silence (anti-pattern).* The notes cite Carla's own early mistake: preparing hard, then sitting silent in meetings, which reads as "does she even have a clue?" and stalls promotion. She also lost her voice under an insecure boss (a manager preoccupied with being "found out" who kept smart subordinates on edge) and reclaimed it by treating the self-doubt as a signal to reorient and deliberately showcasing strengths.

*Marissa.* A Spanish-speaking Latina (SKILL.md calls her a consultant) who suppressed her identity so thoroughly that leadership never considered her for the Latin America role she was best qualified for. Outcome: lost assignment. Lesson: erasing distinguishing traits to fit in costs real opportunities; your background is a relationship bridge.

*Peter.* Charismatic and influential outside the office, but quiet and credit-avoiding inside; eighteen years in, no promotion in eight. Lesson: the outside/inside split. Your outside self is powerful; showing only the silent inside self stalls a career.

*Anne (wrong building).* A 17-year financial-marketing writer with good reviews but no passion. She noticed she came alive writing about nonprofits and spirituality. She kept her job, spent 3.5 years earning a journalism master's at night, networked relentlessly with publishers and editors, and at an event hosted by her own Wall Street firm met an author needing a ghostwriter. She is now a fulfilled freelance writer. Lesson: keep the paycheck, build transferable skills, network toward the new field, then jump.

*The "just to have a job" mistake.* The notes also warn against taking a job you knew was wrong; you gave away power on day one and reclaim it with a 12-24-month plan.

**Evidence.** No studies. Evidence is experiential: Carla's pitch results (CFO's next-day call), the cases above, and the observation of up-or-out environments in which colleagues "actively exploit any visible self-doubt." Caveat: all anecdotal and drawn from a 2009 Wall Street setting.

**Key terms.** Competitive advantage; submerging ("I don't know if she's smart or stupid"); up-or-out environment; insecure boss; wrong building; written and unwritten rules; multifaceted self; elevator speech; "Am I showing them?" test.

**So what.**
- Write strengths, weaknesses and why you are in this seat; lead with strengths and schedule work on weaknesses.
- Build an elevator speech from that list and rehearse it.
- Before taking a seat, ask whether the industry's expectations are credible and easy for you to meet.
- Prepare client meetings around client needs; aim for a conversation.
- Bring faith, music, sports and service to work as bridges.
- Politics are inevitable wherever two or more people form a culture; play them in a way comfortable for you, not by becoming phony.

## Chapter 2: Be the Architect of Your Own Agenda

**Central argument.** Write your own career agenda (macro goals, micro goals, compensation and promotion targets, timeline) and use it as the decision filter for every setback, offer and comparison. The agenda fixes the *goals*; the *strategy* stays flexible. Own mistakes once and move on; stop blaming "they."

**Full stories.**

*Carla's operations-officer stint.* Carla took an "off the line" role (no direct client contact or revenue). It bought exposure to senior executives, and it produced a future president of Morgan Stanley as her sponsor. Lesson: unglamorous roles can buy sponsors.

*Sherry's costly naked short (worked example).* Sherry, largely self-taught, worked on an emerging-markets deal where she did not understand share allocation and the "naked short." Afraid her peers would judge her and that her unsupportive boss would weaponize the gap at year-end, she guessed rather than asked. The stock rose after pricing, and covering the short cost the firm real revenue. For weeks her boss resurfaced the error in every internal meeting, while peers' costly mistakes were forgotten in days. Sherry took the boss aside and, in one conversation, owned the mistake without hedging; stated the lesson (never again a solo judgment when she knew she needed help); said she had noticed the repeated call-outs and that it was time to move on, "don't you agree?"; and asked for his explicit support going forward. He affirmed her ability, offered extra coaching and never mentioned the blunder in a meeting again. Lesson: mistake recovery is a single, structured conversation, not endless apology.

*Gina's rejection.* Gina turned down a small new product, judging it by current revenue instead of skill-building and first-mover upside. She waited six more years for a now-mature product, while the person who took the small product was promoted in three. Lesson: judge offers against the agenda, not short-term prestige.

*Hazel vs. Curtis.* Hazel resented Curtis's earlier promotion, though he had made sacrifices (Hong Kong, uprooting his family) that she chose not to make. Lesson: don't measure yourself against a peer's report card; their choice, their timeline. (The notes give only these details for Gina and Hazel/Curtis.)

*Carla and the demeaning manager.* Carla worked repeatedly for a demeaning manager because she did not know she could decline projects. Lesson: you may be able to decline assignments.

**Evidence.** Industry norms cited as guidance: 3-4 years to VP, 8-12 to MD (stated as examples of norms; "a guide, not law"). Pay data from HR salary-band ranges and headhunter bands for seniors. No dataset. The evidence for the ecosystem idea is Harris's observation that people value the opposite type but reward their own. Caveat: egregious inequality is the only trigger for acting on comparative pay data.

**Key terms.** Macro/micro agenda; comparative agenda; "they" syndrome; mistake recovery; political maniac vs. workhorse; corporate ecosystem; chit/IOU; off the line; political start-up costs; distraction; "every dog has their day"; "you don't ask, you don't get."

**So what.**
- Write the agenda before you walk in, answering why you joined, skills, experiences, people, timeline, how long you plan to stay.
- When passed over, return to the agenda and ask whether you are living and learning as intended; engage decision-makers for a chit rather than quitting.
- Replace "they" with "what did I contribute?" (exposure, relationships, homework, pursuing projects).
- Ask for the pay that top-notch work warrants and what the top bracket requires.
- Don't rely on "attaboys" for motivation; the agenda supplies it.
- In mergers and layoffs, look for where you can excel.

## Chapter 3: Captain of Your Career, the Ninety-Day Rule

**Central argument.** In any new job, assignment or responsibility you have roughly one quarter to create the impression of your value. Use it to prove *can do*, *will do* and above all *fit*, because the impression formed by day ninety sets your trajectory (the halo effect).

**Full stories.**

*Carla's presentation-skills sprint (worked example).* Two years into banking she realized she lacked the board-room presentation experience required for VP, because she had never attached a time frame to the goal. She applied the ninety-day rule twice. Quarter one: internal reps, such as volunteering for campus-recruiting presentations and shadowing strong presenters. Quarter two: external reps, such as taking finance/treasury committee leadership at nonprofits and practicing presenting numbers compellingly, exactly the muscle a corporate board pitch requires. By the VP decision, presenting was no longer an impediment.

*Hours and rhythms.* An early riser, Carla adopted M&A's late nights and later capital markets' 7 a.m. starts because that is where rapport is built.

*The Sunday football briefing.* When the group's conversation was sports and she was not a fan, Carla phoned her sports-fan father every Sunday to learn enough to open Monday's conversation. Lesson: engineer a conversational bridge.

*Day-91 check-in template (positive version).* "I wanted to discuss my progress. I jumped in and have attained proficiency in A, B, C. I've enjoyed working with X and Y and learned a lot from client exposure on the last two transactions. Next quarter I plan to focus on D, E, F. Is there anything you'd add or change?" It shows self-awareness, invites honest feedback and plants the seed that you progress fast. The rocky-start version acknowledges the steeper learning curve, names what you have mastered and asks how to move forward.

*Informal hazing.* Up-or-out environments may deliberately not invite the newcomer; the test is passed by inviting yourself. Often no one is invited and it is simply habit.

**Evidence.** Experiential; no data. Thresholds from the notes: ninety days to prove value; day 91 to advertise; go-to person target for days 91-180; by the six-month look be on par; at one year on par or ahead or catching up takes a long time; perform at the next level 6-12 months before wanting the title. **Note on conflicting framing:** the chapter says "six-month look" and "one-year mark"; the cheatsheet lists "6 months to be on par; 1 year on par or ahead." They are compatible; this file keeps both.

**Key terms.** Can do / will do / fit; ninety-day rule; unspoken rules of the game; points on the board; halo effect; curse of mediocrity; part of the fabric; assignments associate; informal hazing; significant value add; go-to person; skill requirements by level.

**So what.**
- Calendar three deliverables: basic skill, unspoken rules (dress, "casual Friday," how to counter an idea, which meetings matter, when to approach the boss for promotion, whether to expose teammates' mistakes) and key players.
- Take training where it exists; teach yourself where it doesn't.
- Treat lunch, coffee and the Thursday bar as relationship infrastructure.
- Request the deals and seniors you need.
- Differentiate with intellect and work; use rule-compliance to establish fit.
- Ask the decision-maker what advancement requires (women and women of color often don't know).
- Never open with "How am I doing?"

## Chapter 4: Perception Is the Copilot to Reality

**Central argument.** People treat you according to who they *think* you are. Every organization has a subjective component to evaluation, so perception, not just hard work, shapes assignments, clients, pay and promotion. You can deliberately create and change it, starting with self-presentation, and starting with the boss.

**Full stories.**

*The "tough" campaign.* Carla was told, "I don't know if you're tough enough." Rather than ignoring it, she treated it as possibly true and possibly shared (the risk of ignoring was irrelevance). For about three months she worked the word *tough* into nearly every sentence, such as "You'd better make sure it's right, because you know I'm tough!", and acted congruently, until a VP was overheard warning his team to double-check assumptions "because Carla Harris is so tough." Outcome: the perception was rewritten. Lesson: describe yourself with the target adjective and act congruently for about three months.

*The associate who wanted VP (worked example).* By every objective measure she was outstanding (resourceful, quantitative, a strong presentation builder), yet seniors treated her as an associate: not asked to present first, opinion rarely sought when seniors were present, assigned the background assumptions rather than the substance. Carla prescribed two changes: dress like the women officers in her area, and hold a pre-meeting with her direct boss before each group meeting to propose how the meeting should run and what part she would play. Her boss began to perceive that she wanted and could handle an officer role, let her run meetings, and she was promoted. Carla's caveat: you can't dress your way to success. The objective evidence was already there; the subjective evidence was the missing year.

*Bias before you arrive.* Examples of baggage in the notes: all bankers are ruthless; all Ivy Leaguers are pompous. Response: no firsthand data, don't carry it, overwrite it.

**Evidence.** Experiential only. The three-month duration, the 2-3 fans + 2-3 non-fans (4-6 people) audit, and the adjectives by level are Harris's practice, not studied results. Caveat: the cases show correlation with outcomes (promotion after changes) and Harris herself notes objective performance was already there.

**Key terms.** Marketplace; baggage of the beholder; noise; behaving upward; pre-meeting; success team; three adjectives; adjectives by level (junior: eager, resourceful, analytical; mid: gets things done, mobilizes others, strategic potential; senior: visionary, commercial, client-focused, authority); not tough.

**So what.**
- Pick at least three adjectives true of you and valued by the business model (check recruiters' words, annual report, website, "How does my company make money?").
- No intersection? Wrong seat; harvest for 12-24 months and move.
- Audit with 2-3 fans and 2-3 non-fans, coffee as equals; thank everyone.
- Fix the boss's perception first.
- Do not wait for the annual review.
- Avoid: always soliciting opinions before deciding (read as indecisive), always saying why things won't work (not commercial), belittling others (bully), running from non-fans.

## Chapter 5: The Mentor, the Sponsor, the Adviser

**Central argument.** Three distinct relationships power a career and people conflate them. The adviser answers discrete questions; the mentor gives tailored developmental advice and hears the good, bad and ugly; the sponsor spends political capital to "carry your paper into the room." Long-term success without a sponsor is nearly impossible, so recruit one deliberately.

**Full stories.**

*Carla's 1999 managing-director promotion (worked example).* In a crowded room of advocates each pushing their own candidate, Carla asked who would passionately argue *her* case, and could not answer. Diagnosis: promotion at risk, not for lack of credentials but for lack of a sponsor. She approached a senior MD with a seat on the committee with a direct, logical request: it's important I'm promoted this year; I need someone on the committee to argue for me; specifically you, because you've seen my work and heard the client feedback; I'll make sure you walk in with the strongest possible package on me. Taken aback but hard to refuse, he agreed. She also recruited a second advocate without a seat at the table but with strong influence over those who had one. She was promoted.

*Reciprocity.* Years later a former mentee, now close to the decision-maker at a client firm, gave Carla's team post-pitch feedback and vouched for their integrity; they were named a book runner on the deal.

*Mentor-as-adviser in Ch 2.* The off-line move that produced Carla's sponsor (see Ch 2) is cross-referenced here.

**Evidence.** Experiential. Harris observes that mentoring forms along lines of commonality (gender, race, school, background) so women and women of color are less often chosen. Numbers: 8-10 touches/year; Carla's own cadence every six to eight weeks; two sponsor commitments. Caveat: bosses may have capital for one or two protégés and four or five who deserve it.

**Key terms.** Adviser; mentor; sponsor; career board of directors; carry your paper into the room; political capital; moderate support (no detractors, no table-pounder); agenda of one (Y-O-U); follow-through and follow-up; assigned mentor; sponsor ROI.

**So what.**
- Test your mentors: would I tell them everything?
- Convert an adviser by thanking, stating they could be extraordinarily helpful over a couple of years, asking, and naming the expectation of frank confidential counsel.
- Sponsor criteria: seat at the table in your area, capital, willingness; ideally the boss.
- Ask the recruiting team "who will sponsor me?" and get two explicit commitments; re-ask when changing divisions.
- Answer the ROI question; reciprocate within your integrity.
- Judge advice: uncomfortable-stretch, follow; uncomfortable-backfire, trust your gut; discount advice the adviser would never take themselves.
- Don't fire outgrown mentors; don't treat these relationships as purely strategic.

## Chapter 6: Leverage Your Voice

**Central argument.** Everyone has a voice; those who succeed use it to establish relevance. Voice creates perception, creates the expectation of your contribution, and is the mechanism for stating what you expect in return. At the margin, the person who asked wins.

**Full stories.**

*The eureka year (worked example).* In a tough year on Wall Street, compensation and promotions were cut back. Carla worked hard, said nothing about wanting the promotion and assumed it would follow. It didn't. After cooling off for a few days she went to her boss, who said he had been wondering when she would come in: her peers had been in and out of his office for months pleading their cases, tough marginal choices had to be made, and she had been silent. Lesson: when decisions are made at the margin, the person who voiced expectations wins the tie. She stopped being angry at the organization and started asking.

*The listening turnaround.* A colleague told Carla her boss hated giving her feedback because "she has an answer for everything." On a mentor's advice, in her next review she responded only with "yes," "uh-huh" and "oh really? okay." The boss reported she was open and constructive; feedback became specific; the relationship evolved from manager to adviser to sponsor, and he began discounting false claims about her on her behalf.

*The convertible claim.* When someone falsely said "she doesn't know the convertible product," the model is not to argue: assert, invite the test, and make sure you can pass it.

*Intimidation games.* Smirks and furrowed brows meant to make you clam up; find a supporter to look at, or stare back and keep talking.

**Evidence.** Experiential. The 50/50 rule is a heuristic (first-time observers give even odds). Numbers: two-thirds/one-third evaluation; 3-6 month goal timelines; check-ins every two months; report card done by month six. Caveat: Harris cautions against being a windbag and against "that look" (a face that makes an evaluator uncomfortable and guarantees vague feedback).

**Key terms.** Three functions of voice; 50/50 rule; word-framing; "frequently wrong, but never in doubt"; relevance; marginal decisions; you don't ask, you don't get / ask for the order; head down; intimidation games; strategic silence; report card.

**So what.**
- Speak in every meeting; paraphrase if needed.
- Frame words to your adjectives ("three reasons...", "five key assumptions...").
- Unsure? "In my judgment X, Y, Z; I'll double-check and get back to you"; keep volume up.
- Ask for next year's promotion this year; two-thirds forward-looking evaluations with timelines.
- Check in every two months with the A-B-C-D script; ask how to make sponsoring you easier.
- In evaluations, say only acknowledgments if you tend to defend; thank the giver.
- Speak up under a bad boss; suffering in silence is the leading cause of women leaving firms.

## Chapter 7: Have a Penchant for Taking Risks

**Central argument.** Playing it safe won't get you ahead; knowledge is leveled by information and technology, so willingness to take risk differentiates. Success equation: Performing Extremely Well + Aligning with Internal Politics + Taking Risks. You can't stop latent risk, but you can offset it with forward risks.

**Full stories.**

*Carrie's calculated risk.* Carrie was two years past ready for SVP, respected by her boss but unpromoted, which meant her boss either lacked the power or was not arguing effectively in committee. Objective: make both her boss and his boss understand she deserved promotion that year. Actions: meet her boss's boss first, then tell her own boss what she did and why. Mapped consequences: boss angry about being bypassed or respectful that she kept him informed; senior boss annoyed or impressed she behaved like a senior person; and if nothing moved, she was prepared to search externally. She took it and was promoted.

*Carla's studied risk (Internet IPO).* At the end of the first tech bubble, she ran an Internet IPO with an oversubscribed book but thin investor feedback and no sign of long-term conviction. Her gut said postpone. Downside: an angry client forcing the deal or walking, internal criticism for deferring revenue, a competitor stealing the mandate. Upside: client trust and a deal that would actually trade well. She recommended waiting for the fall. The next day the NASDAQ fell over 100 points and most Internet IPOs priced that day traded below issue. The client returned months later and priced successfully.

*Carla's risk of saying no.* The year she was up for MD, her boss asked her to launch a new retail-distribution effort. She judged a long syndicate track record would carry the promotion better than six to nine months in a new role, accepted the two risks (annoyed boss, missed accelerator), declined with her rationale ready, and was promoted with his sponsorship intact.

**Evidence.** Experiential. The only external anchor is a quoted line by Meg Whitman (as the notes put it: the price of inaction exceeds the cost of a mistake) and the NASDAQ drop of over 100 points in Carla's IPO case. Harris's long-run observation: nobody who took a well-thought-out risk regretted it; almost everyone regrets a risk not taken. Caveat: a single IPO case is anecdotal.

**Key terms.** Latent risk; forward risk; calculated, studied, step-out-on-faith risk; "not smart"; test-the-waters questions; risk of saying no; lull of the status quo; predecessor rule; F-E-A-R (False Evidence Appearing Real); gift of experience; self-imposed limitations; beg forgiveness, don't ask permission; compensated risk; platform argument.

**So what.**
- Classify each risk, then write objective, actions, consequences.
- Run the three test-the-waters questions; three yeses means go.
- Take bigger jobs at the same pay (platform) and better-paid moves with political risk (compensated).
- Avoid faith risks where many interdependent players react.
- Ask continuously: how do I make my role, job and platform bigger?
- Don't wait to be senior to step out of the box.
- Treat negative people's lists as "a negative view," not a verdict.

## Chapter 8: Power in the Network

**Central argument.** No one moves up alone; many decisions are not made on merit, so who you know breaks ties between equally qualified candidates. Network power lies in relationship strength, how you manage it and the role you play, not the number of business cards.

**Full stories.**

*The first CD (1999).* Carla wanted to record and sell a gospel album to raise money for two schools but knew nothing about licensing, producers, engineers or distribution. She took a banking colleague (also a musician) to lunch; he mapped the steps and introduced her to another colleague with a recording studio, who introduced her to a Broadway musical director. After an audition between matinee and evening shows he agreed to produce, and sourced the studio, engineer, mastering, manufacturer and musicians. For distribution, a senior internal sponsor personally walked her to a music-label CEO, who connected her to the president of the label's independent distribution arm. The firm's press contacts and a PR relationship from a past deal landed a Sunday Business feature. Every link was a relationship she already had, leveraged one step at a time, and the CD became a conversation piece and differentiator with banking clients.

*Closing the information gap.* As a new associate surrounded by former analysts with a ready-made network, Carla targeted two people (a classmate with strong ties into that group and a former analyst she already knew), invested in lunches, dinners and drinks, asked directly for help accessing the information flow and offered her own external network in return. Within a short time she was invited to broader events and had the same information as peers.

*Recruiting as exposure.* Captaining a recruiting team gave Carla legitimate reasons to call senior people and walk candidates into their offices.

**Evidence.** Experiential. Cadence figures: one face-to-face and four voice-to-voice per year for key relationships; e-mail once or twice a year for others. Harris's observation: men access relationships quickly while women often don't make themselves useful to their networks or ask for help. Caveat: opinion, not measured data.

**Key terms.** Four relationship types; four roles of the lateral network; connector / information gatherer / helpful person; maintenance cadence; town-hall play; assistant as gatekeeper; series of connected relationships; everyone has power; unique network; information flow; "don't hate, gravitate"; competition with yourself; reciprocity.

**So what.**
- Build all four rings and keep them diverse.
- Choose a network role and make a relationship with you visibly worthwhile.
- Town halls: front row, prepared question, introduce yourself, seek a one-on-one, find the common thread.
- Honor assistants (career-day talks, advice for their kids), expecting no return.
- Ask peers for help; offer help first.
- Treat boards and volunteering as investment time.
- Treat the résumé as table stakes; the relationship is the tiebreaker.

## Chapter 9: Balance Is a Necessity

**Central argument.** Balance is not work vs. family but integrating passions and giving back into professional life. Without something outside the job that makes you feel alive, "your life becomes a function of somebody else's day." The time exists; you capture it by calendarizing and batching.

**Full stories.**

*Gospel CDs and Carnegie Hall.* Carla married her passion for gospel singing with giving back by directing all proceeds from her recordings and sold-out Carnegie Hall concerts to two schools (one in Harlem, one in Jacksonville). The unplanned dividend: after twelve years as a banker with no press mention, her first CD in 2000 produced a Sunday New York Times Business-section feature, and in 2006 a half-page Wall Street Journal story on her first Carnegie Hall concert. Colleagues who had never looked her way congratulated her in the hallway, and her standing with clients rose overnight. After the Journal piece, clients called to thank her for speaking openly about her faith, people who hadn't known she had a church affiliation and with whom she'd never guessed she shared it. Lesson: a new relationship layer opened purely because she was herself and honored her passion.

**Evidence.** Experiential. Numbers: lunch is 5-10 hours a week; take two for joy; e-mail only in several fixed half-hour slots; Harris says she recovers several hours a day. Caveat: self-reported.

**Key terms.** Function of somebody else's day; refuel; calendarize; congregate like actions; common point of interest; win-win of authenticity.

**So what.**
- Map a typical day; take two lunch hours a week for passions.
- Batch reading into held-call blocks; check e-mail in fixed half-hour slots (continuous e-mail is the biggest time leak).
- Serve out of gratitude, not in order to get; talk about service with clients.
- "No time" is a scheduling problem.

## Chapter 10: Expect to Win

**Central argument.** Approach your career expecting to succeed and you will; approach it with "maybe" and you get mediocre results. Expectation is a daily decision that changes body language, speech and mentality; in competitive environments people smell doubt and fear and exploit them.

**Full stories.**

*The Cleveland hotel room (worked example).* Mid-career, after deals that went badly and years of disappointing pay, Carla's confidence was low and her self-talk reduced to *maybe*. At eighteen she had *known* she would get into Harvard, HBS and a top Wall Street firm; now her thoughts were "mediocre thoughts, mediocre results." The night before her birthday, on a business trip in Cleveland, she ran the reflection she had first done at thirteen (when she decided to stop being the girl who knew everyone's gossip and became the trustworthy friend she still is). She wrote who she had been that year (good friend, trustworthy, accessible) and who she wanted to be professionally (known in the industry as a professional and an expert at her craft). Reading her own list she recognized she had the power to choose focus: the money not made and deals not won, or the agenda and a plan to turn things around. She chose the plan and to "knock the cover off the ball" so she'd be ready when the next opportunity arrived.

*The syndicate-desk habit.* Often meeting a company's CEO and CFO only two hours before a road show she would run and price over the next ten days, she walked in expecting to build trust, "and in almost every case, it worked."

**Evidence.** Experiential: the reflection and the syndicate observation. Caveat: "in almost every case" is Harris's own assessment, not a measured rate.

**Key terms.** Know vs. maybe vs. hope; act "as if"; winner's lens; selectively motivated; not wedded to specifics; complacency; change as guarantee; four-sheet reflection; lift-someone-up reset.

**So what.**
- Decide every morning to be triumphant.
- Apply the five principles: best-outcome strategy; fearlessness (achieve or learn); expectation of good; interdependence; mastery (be the positive outlier).
- Do the four sheets yearly or when self-talk slips to maybe; write in third person if stuck.
- When low, do something for someone else.
- When complacent, return to the agenda.

## Chapter 11: My Most Precious Pearl

**Central argument.** Know not only who you are but *whose* you are. For Harris it is Christian faith ("I am a child of God"), the platform that lets her manage what the other pearls cannot control. For any reader: have something above the job to turn to for strength, guidance and comfort.

**Full stories.**

*The second-year associate.* Working for an insecure manager neither capable of nor interested in training her, and worried about her job, Carla shared the worry with a close friend's mother, who told her to read and pray two psalms daily (Psalm 91 and Psalm 4). They became a mantra, then armor, then comfort and motor, and marked the start of using faith deliberately as a tool for success rather than a Sunday habit. From there she built verse-to-principle mappings (Philippians 4:13: no challenge is insurmountable; Philippians 4:6-8: don't worry; Isaiah 54:17: those who wish me ill won't prevail; "The will of God will not lead me where the grace of God won't protect me": I'll get what I need in uncharted waters; Romans 8:28: it works out for my benefit; Isaiah 41:10: nothing to fear; Hebrews 13:5: no battle alone) and treated everything as lesson or blessing.

**Evidence.** Personal testimony; no external data. Caveat: it is faith-specific; the generalized version in the notes is "God, Buddha, Allah, or the power of the Universe."

**Key terms.** Whose I am; faith as steady constant; lesson or blessing; give your best then diagnose; scripture-to-principle mapping; platform for success.

**So what.**
- Separate what preparation controls from what it doesn't, and have a resource for the second.
- Classify events as lesson or blessing.
- Give your best daily; if you can't, diagnose and act.
- Have a consistent decision ritual for high-stakes calls.

## (b) The big ideas of the book

1. **No organization is a true meritocracy.** People evaluate people, so performance alone isn't enough; you need strategy, relationships, politics, tenacity and faith. *(Introduction, Ch 7 equation.)*
2. **The authentic you is your competitive advantage.** It is why you were hired, and suppressing it costs confidence, trust and opportunities. Choose a firm whose rules you can honestly meet. *(Ch 1.)*
3. **Write your agenda and let it filter decisions.** The agenda takes emotion out of decisions and lets you self-motivate; goals fixed, strategy flexible. *(Ch 2, Ch 10.)*
4. **Take ownership: "they" is the language of passivity.** Ask what you contributed; own mistakes once and move on. *(Ch 2.)*
5. **Your first ninety days set the halo.** Master the skill, the unspoken rules and the relationships; fit is decisive; advertise on day 91. *(Ch 3.)*
6. **Perception is reality's copilot; manage it deliberately.** Three adjectives, congruent behavior, audit with fans and non-fans, fix the boss first. *(Ch 4.)*
7. **A sponsor is non-negotiable.** Adviser, mentor and sponsor differ; recruit sponsors with a seat at the table in your area before joining and ask directly. *(Ch 5.)*
8. **Use your voice, including to ask.** Speak in every meeting, frame words, answer with confident room to adjust, ask for next year's promotion this year. *(Ch 6.)*
9. **Risk is part of the success equation.** Offset latent risk with forward risks; match the risk type to the decision rule; saying no is also a risk. *(Ch 7.)*
10. **Networks are relationships, not card counts.** Four rings including assistants; cadence; offer help first. *(Ch 8.)*
11. **Balance means integrating passions and service.** Without it your life is a function of someone else's day; calendarize and batch. *(Ch 9.)*
12. **Expect to win: know, don't hope.** Act "as if," use the winner's lens, run the reflection, lift someone up. *(Ch 10.)*
13. **Know whose you are.** Something above the job steadies you against what you can't control; every event is a lesson or blessing. *(Ch 11.)*
14. **Fit and relationships are as decisive as skill.** Fit (Ch 3), network ties (Ch 8) and sponsors (Ch 5) decide marginal calls.
15. **Failure and mistakes are experience, not verdicts.** F-E-A-R, mistake recovery and fearlessness all say the same. *(Ch 2, 7, 10.)*

- Erasing distinguishing traits (Marissa) or splitting inside/outside selves (Peter) (Ch 1).
- Taking a job you knew was wrong "just to have a job" (Ch 1).
- Copying an admired path; rage-quitting after a missed promotion; resenting peers (Hazel/Curtis); rejecting small new roles (Gina) (Ch 2).
- Ruminating and re-apologizing for mistakes (Ch 2).
- Accepting assignments you never had to accept; relying on "attaboys" (Ch 2).

- Assuming hard work is noticed; always soliciting opinions; always saying why things won't work; belittling others; running from non-fans; waiting for the annual review (Ch 4).
