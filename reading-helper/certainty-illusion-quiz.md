# The Certainty Illusion Quiz Book
*The Certainty Illusion: What You Don't Know and Why It Matters* by Timothy Caulfield

**How to use:** Cover the answers, answer from memory (out loud or in writing), then check. Anything you miss is a section to reread. Each chapter has short-answer (SA), true/false (T/F), and multiple-choice (MC) questions. A cumulative final exam is at the end. Chapter numbering follows the study notes (Intro = Chapter 0, then Chapters 1-13, then the Conclusion as Chapter 14). Part I (Ch 1-5) is the Science Illusion, Part II (Ch 6-10) is the Goodness Illusion, Part III (Ch 11-13) is the Opinion Illusion.

## Chapter 0: Introduction: Adrift in a Storm

**Q1 (SA).** State the book's thesis, "the certainty illusion."
**A:** The more we seek certainty, the harder it becomes to find, because the information economy is incentivized to spin exactly the signals we rely on. Distortion follows value: the signals worth faking are the ones people trust most.

**Q2 (SA).** Name the Three Illusions and the guidepost each corrupts.
**A:** Science Illusion (the appearance of science replaces science), Goodness Illusion (a messaging strategy that makes a choice feel like the right thing without close examination), and Opinion Illusion (reviews, rankings, and polls are faked, biased, or manufactured).

**Q3 (MC).** Caulfield calls the problem a "knowledge production crisis" rather than just misinformation because:
a) misinformation is not real  b) it is about the degraded methods and institutions that make knowledge, not only downstream lies  c) only scientists are affected  d) the internet did not exist before 2000
**A:** b. Upstream incentives (publish-or-perish, hype, predatory journals) keep manufacturing the raw material.

**Q4 (T/F).** Trust level should be used as a safety indicator: the more trusted a signal, the safer it is.
**A:** False. Trust level is a risk indicator, not a safety indicator, because the most trusted guideposts are the most worth counterfeiting.

**Q5 (SA).** How did Caulfield answer the hostile questioner who said "Science is full of shit," and why?
**A:** He conceded: "Yeah, it is." The man was partly right (predatory journals, hype incentives, salami science, fraud), and more citations would not answer an incentive critique. The productive move is to grant the corruption and argue the repair is better science, not no science.

**Q6 (T/F).** Because science has problems, the right response is to become a cynical disbeliever of everything.
**A:** False. That is an anti-pattern: skepticism is a filter, not an off switch, and cynicism destroys your ability to use good evidence. "If not science, what?" The alternative is astrology, tea leaves, and hunches.

**Q7 (SA).** What does the stat that 81% of Americans rely "a lot" on their own research for big decisions (96% of them online) suggest?
**A:** Doing your own research does not escape the problem, because it happens inside the same rigged information environment. Related self-check: "I Google, therefore I'm certain."

## Chapter 1: The Science-y Veneer

**Q1 (SA).** What is scienceploitation, and what is its cycle?
**A:** Using scientific verbiage and hot research topics to sell unproven products and agendas. Cycle: an exciting scientific development makes pop-culture noise, hucksters spot a market, the market floods with products riffing on the science, people get hurt, and public discourse is confused.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the quantum test?
**A:** If the word *quantum* is used to explain a health product or procedure, assume pseudoscience and a fraudster nearby, and stop evaluating. Physicist Mark Freeman has never seen it used accurately with a health product.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the science-y / scary dyad, and how do you defuse it?
**A:** Science-sounding jargon plus a fear message, the most durable misinformation engine. Strip the jargon and ask what the claimed harm is; strip the fear and ask what supports the mechanism. Neither usually survives alone.

**Q4 (MC).** In the stem-cell clinic study (about 250 alternative-medicine clinic websites), what share mentioned any limitation of the evidence?
a) 19%  b) 50%  c) 75%  d) 95%
**A:** a. Absence of stated risks and limitations is diagnostic of scienceploitation.

**Q5 (T/F).** The 2004 study on science-y language found that science majors and graduate students were immune to its persuasive effect.
**A:** False. Science-y language raised persuasiveness regardless of the audience's education level, so build procedural defences, not confidence.

**Q6 (SA).** What is "science washing"?
**A:** Michelle Wong's term for using "science" as a generic legitimizing badge (graphs, numbers, technical language) instead of showing relevant research. Check whether there is relevant research applying to this specific claim.

**Q7 (SA).** Why is "immune boosting" a near-reliable scam marker?
**A:** You cannot "boost" the immune system as promised, and you would not want to; an overactive immune system is how autoimmune disease behaves.

## Chapter 2: Certainty, Dunning-Kruger, and the Humility Fix

**Q1 (SA).** What is the Galileo gambit and how is it dismantled?
**A:** Answering evidence by casting yourself as the persecuted truth-teller ("they laughed at Galileo"). Galileo faced a religious orthodoxy, not a scientific consensus; Sagan noted they also laughed at Bozo the Clown; real revolutionaries (Einstein, Darwin, Curie) produced data tested against their era's conventions. Treat it as a last-resort defence and a tell, not an argument.

**Q2 (SA).** State the Sagan standard and how to apply it.
**A:** Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. Assess plausibility against existing knowledge, assess the claimant (expertise, what they sell, what thousands of researchers supposedly missed), scale the proof to the improbability, and keep the onus on the claimant.

**Q3 (MC).** The Dunning-Kruger effect, as used here, is:
a) experts underrating themselves  b) overestimating knowledge because lacking knowledge impedes recognizing the lack  c) fear of public speaking  d) trusting only famous people
**A:** b. A 2019 anti-GMO study found extreme opponents know the least but think they know the most; a large 2022 study replicated this across climate change, nuclear power, vaccination, evolution, and GMOs.

**Q4 (SA).** Describe the humility fix and Caulfield's exercise.
**A:** Make intellectual humility ("recognition of one's fallibility as a knower") the default posture. Exercise: list areas where evidence evolved and you changed position, plus grey areas where you are still seeking information. An empty list is itself the finding.

**Q5 (SA).** What was the finding of "On the Reception and Detection of Pseudo-Profound Bullshit" (Pennycook, 2015 Ig Nobel)?
**A:** Participants rated syntactically coherent sentences built from random vague buzzwords as at least somewhat profound. A 2019 follow-up found the labelling effect: identical nonsense rated more profound when attributed to a famous author.

**Q6 (T/F).** Contrarian theories like plate tectonics and H. pylori ulcers justify treating any fringe claim as possibly right.
**A:** False. Those won because data eventually supported them, raised in scientific venues, not because the proponents proclaimed themselves persecuted.

**Q7 (SA).** What rule of thumb applies to coaching or wellness offerings full of "transformational," "blind spots," and "optimal levels"?
**A:** If it sounds like pretentious tripe, it most likely is. Demand a concrete description of what you will do and how it works. Pennycook: "Science that can't be understood is bad science."

## Chapter 3: The Hype Machine, Catastrophizing, and Science Investment

**Q1 (SA).** What is the hype pipeline?
**A:** The Caulfield & Condit (2012) idea that exaggerated claims of benefit and social impact are generated throughout knowledge production: initial excitement, grant writing, publication, and media representation.

**Q2 (MC).** Roughly what share of biomedical abstracts contain at least some spin (2017 study)?
a) 5%  b) 15%  c) nearly 40%  d) 90%
**A:** c. The abstract is often the only thing read, even by academics.

**Q3 (SA).** What did Sumner et al. (2014) find about health press releases?
**A:** Of 462 releases, 40% contained exaggerated health advice, 33% exaggerated causation, and 36% exaggerated inferences from animal studies, and the hype was transcribed into the popular press.

**Q4 (SA).** What did the University of Tsukuba analysis of NIH grant abstracts find?
**A:** In nearly a million successful NIH abstracts (1985-2020), hype rose roughly 1,400 percent. The team also found a comparably massive increase in hype in NIH's own funding announcements, showing a funder-applicant loop.

**Q5 (T/F).** Heavy media attention is a sign that a finding is more likely to replicate.
**A:** False. A 2023 study of 14,000 papers found media attention was negatively associated with replication.

**Q6 (SA).** What is the "timeline test" for therapy claims?
**A:** Clinical trials alone take 10-15 years and roughly 90 percent fail, so a "five to ten years or sooner" promise (found in almost 70 percent of the stem-cell newspaper articles Caulfield analyzed) is impossible by construction.

**Q7 (SA).** What is funding by lottery and why is it proposed?
**A:** Reviewers decide only basic fundability (meets reasonable standards), then grants are allocated by lottery. The current process is already near-random and biased (a 2018 PNAS study found no agreement among reviewers); a lottery is cheaper, faster, more equitable, and removes the incentive to hype. The Swiss National Science Foundation and the Health Research Council of New Zealand have tried it.

## Chapter 4: The Predator Problem

**Q1 (SA).** Distinguish predatory, hijacked, and vanity/agenda-driven journals.
**A:** Predatory: charge authors fees with lax or no peer review. Hijacked: criminal clones of a legitimate journal's name and domain to harvest fees ("a fake Gucci handbag"). Vanity/agenda: purpose-built to legitimize a predetermined position (anti-vaccine, climate denial, etc.), typically publishing their own editorial board.

**Q2 (SA).** List the checks in the red-flag audit for a single supporting study.
**A:** Publisher (known predatory operator?), author affiliation (vague or mimicking a real institution?), submission-to-acceptance interval, ethics review, methods (control group, blinding, standardization, sample size), and the author's regulatory history. Later chapters add retraction status and citation pattern.

**Q3 (MC).** Biomedical papers typically take about how long to reach acceptance, and what does two weeks imply?
a) 100 days; no real review  b) 2 days; fast review  c) 1 year; fraud  d) 30 days; normal
**A:** a. A 2021 study found 91-639 days, averaging around 100.

**Q4 (SA).** In the O-Shot case, what did the audit of Runels's paper reveal?
**A:** Publisher OMICS International (later fined $50 million by the FTC in 2019); an ambiguous affiliation ("Medical School Birmingham") that was a modest building far from the real medical school; submitted June 12, accepted June 25; no ethics review by an author previously sanctioned by the FDA; eleven self-reporting women, no control, no blinding.

**Q5 (SA).** What is the citation-pattern test?
**A:** Check who cites the work. If the broader academic community does not pick it up, even to contest it, be suspicious. The *International Journal of Vaccine Theory, Practice, and Research* was cited almost entirely by itself, its own editorial board, and known anti-vaccine advocates.

**Q6 (T/F).** Appearing in PubMed, Scopus, or a WHO library guarantees a paper is legitimate.
**A:** False. About 400 publications from hijacked journals were found in the WHO COVID-19 library, and a 2023 study found Scopus carrying work from 67+ hijacked journals.

**Q7 (SA).** What policy fix does Caulfield propose, and what incentive drives the industry?
**A:** Prohibit public research funds from paying predatory fees. The incentive is publish-or-perish, with rewards for quantity (in South Africa, quantity-based pay produced up to a 140-fold rise in predatory publications).

## Chapter 5: AI and Zombie Science

**Q1 (SA).** What is the single study syndrome rule?
**A:** From Ivan Oransky: if a claim is based on one study, be much more suspicious. Always consider the body of evidence. Caulfield calls it the most important takeaway of Part I, because it neutralizes predatory papers, AI fakes, and zombie retractions without detecting each fraud.

**Q2 (SA).** What is zombie science?
**A:** Discredited or retracted findings that keep circulating and influencing literature, public perception, and health debates after formal correction.

**Q3 (MC).** A 2021 analysis of 13,000+ retracted papers found what share of post-retraction references acknowledged the retraction?
a) 5.4%  b) 25%  c) 50%  d) 95%
**A:** a. Assume retraction status will not reach you passively; check it.

**Q4 (T/F).** Retracted papers receive less attention than never-retracted papers over time.
**A:** False. A 2022 University of Michigan analysis of nearly 4,000 retracted papers found they received more attention over ten years, perhaps because they are more exciting and extreme.

**Q5 (SA).** What is Holden Thorp's two-stage retraction?
**A:** Stage one decides whether the paper is valid, with no blame, so it can be removed (even provisionally) quickly. Stage two asks who erred, which is slow because careers are at stake. Retractions currently average two to three years (Wakefield took over ten).

**Q6 (SA).** Why was Wakefield's 1998 *Lancet* paper structurally unable to show causation, and what happened afterward?
**A:** It described twelve children, with no randomization and no control group. Brian Deer found it was funded by lawyers acting for parents suing vaccine makers. Wakefield lost his licence in 2010, the paper was retracted in 2010 after twelve years, yet in 2015 21 percent of Americans under thirty believed vaccines cause autism.

**Q7 (SA).** How does motivated reasoning keep zombies alive?
**A:** For the motivated reasoner the retraction becomes proof of suppression ("Big Pharma can't have those conclusions floating around"), a self-sealing narrative that makes the bogus study seem more valid.

## Chapter 6: Health Halos and the Devious Dozen

**Q1 (SA).** What is a health halo, and what is its real function?
**A:** A word or phrase that creates an immediate impression of health to influence thinking and behaviour. Its function is to deliver a shortcut to certainty, short-circuiting critical thinking by exploiting our desire to do good.

**Q2 (SA).** List the Devious Dozen.
**A:** Natural, holistic, healthy, organic, non-GMO, gluten-free, chemical-free, toxin-free, locally grown, the colour green, immune-boosting, personalized.

**Q3 (SA).** How do you deconstruct a halo word?
**A:** Ask what it means (does a regulator define it?), what it implies (usually healthier/safer), and whether the implied claim is supported. The gap between the meaning and the implication is the halo.

**Q4 (T/F).** Caulfield's point is that every Devious Dozen item is definitively bad.
**A:** False. His point is narrower: the science is uncertain or mixed for several, and the implied certainty is the deception, not the underlying goal.

**Q5 (SA).** What did the BMJ Open "immune boosting" study find?
**A:** Using a "naive computer" across 225+ websites, about 86% portrayed the concept inaccurately (including claims of protection against COVID-19) and fewer than 10% offered any critique. Supplement sales had spiked by as much as 60% in some countries.

**Q6 (MC).** Pew's 20-country survey on GM foods found what share saying they are safe?
a) 13%  b) 40%  c) 70%  d) 95%
**A:** a. Nearly half think GM foods are unsafe, despite an unusually complete scientific consensus; this is the largest gap between consensus and public perception of any issue discussed.

**Q7 (SA).** What did the two JAMA RCTs find on "personalized" diets?
**A:** The 2018 genetic-diet and 2022 glycemic-response-diet trials found no weight-loss advantage over non-personalized approaches. The best diet is the healthy one you can sustain.

## Chapter 7: Pets, Clean Beauty, and "Safe and Sustainable"

**Q1 (SA).** Explain motte-and-bailey with the clean beauty example.
**A:** The bailey is the contested claim (clean beauty is definitively safer). When challenged, the defender retreats to the motte, an unarguable truism (we all want safe cosmetics), then treats it as vindicating the original claim. Name the retreat and return to the original claim.

**Q2 (SA).** Why is "clean" beauty definitionally problematic?
**A:** There is no definition at Health Canada, the FDA, or the industry, and it is circular (clean means non-toxic, non-toxic means clean). It is, in cosmetic chemist Perry Romanowski's framing, "telling new stories about the same old products."

**Q3 (SA).** What did Darbre's 2004 paraben study actually show, and what happened to it?
**A:** It detected parabens in 20 breast tumours with no comparison group, testing whether they could be measured, not whether they cause cancer. Darbre clarified that the paper never claimed causation, but the correction did not reach the public; a 2007 *Daily Mail* headline helped make paraben-free the founding premise of clean beauty.

**Q4 (MC).** What do the FDA (2022) and Cancer Research UK (2020) say about parabens in cosmetics?
a) They cause cancer  b) The FDA has no information showing an effect on human health as used; CRUK says they do not cause cancer  c) They are banned  d) They are untested
**A:** b.

**Q5 (T/F).** Replacing a demonized ingredient with a "natural" alternative is a safety gain.
**A:** False. It is substitution risk, an information loss. A 2022 study of ~1,500 "clean" products found most contain a potential allergen, and "clean" toothpaste often drops fluoride (a 2018 review found fluoride-free toothpaste failed to reduce caries).

**Q6 (SA).** What is the pet-halo transfer?
**A:** Owners who adopt grain-free, keto, gluten-free, or non-GMO eating are more likely to impose it on pets (2021 study). Grain-free has no evidentiary basis in pets and is linked to canine heart problems under FDA investigation; nearly half of pet food is now labelled grain-free versus 15% a decade ago.

**Q7 (SA).** Why are successful lawsuit settlements not evidence of safety risk?
**A:** They are legal outcomes, not scientific findings; courts are notoriously poor with science.

## Chapter 8: Ancient Aliens, Wishful Thinking, and the Goodness Bias

**Q1 (SA).** Define white hat bias.
**A:** Coined by Allison and Cope (2010): distortion of research-based information in the service of perceived righteous ends. Research on noble topics is more likely to get funded, published, hyped, and believed.

**Q2 (SA).** How was hydroxychloroquine a white hat bias case?
**A:** The urgent, righteous goal of a COVID therapy led to publication bias favouring positive results, with positive findings from lower-quality studies carrying more influence than higher-quality RCTs showing no benefit and possible harm.

**Q3 (MC).** The 2019 University of Southern Mississippi study of 100+ NIH-funded investigators found scientists rated unethical research practices as:
a) equally defensible regardless of cause  b) less defensible when the cause was noble  c) more defensible when the research was for noble reasons  d) irrelevant
**A:** c.

**Q4 (SA).** Describe the CBD placebo loop.
**A:** Placebo responses produce positive trials; positive trials get media coverage; coverage raises expectations and strengthens the placebo response in the next trial (2022 Karolinska study on cannabinoids for pain).

**Q5 (T/F).** Lower-quality observational studies are more prone to white hat bias than randomized trials.
**A:** True. Selective analysis and reporting are easier with weaker data.

**Q6 (SA).** What is the pareidolia check, and what is the problem with ancient-alien claims beyond silliness?
**A:** Before treating a pattern as evidence, ask whether you wanted to see it. Ancient-alien claims are aimed almost exclusively at non-white civilizations (Giza, Machu Picchu, Rapa Nui, etc.) and never at the Colosseum, reinforcing racist ideas; pseudoarchaeology has been linked to antisemitism, white supremacism, and QAnon.

**Q7 (SA).** What counterfactual test guards against the goodness bias?
**A:** Would I accept this method, sample size, or press release if the finding cut against a goal I like? Also: "who would want this to be true?"

## Chapter 9: Manly Men and Manly Ideology

**Q1 (SA).** How is masculinity a "health halo"?
**A:** "Manly" works like "natural" or "clean": an unargued proxy for good that lets sellers attach unproven interventions. Mallory DeMille: influencers present the pursuit of maximum masculinity as the pursuit of maximum health.

**Q2 (SA).** What makes masculinity different from safer food or safer cosmetics as a halo?
**A:** The goal itself is contested, and conformity to traditional masculine norms is consistently associated with worse health outcomes (less help-seeking, more heart disease, risky behaviour, worse mental health, etc.).

**Q3 (SA).** State Starbird's emotional-signal rule.
**A:** Whenever content makes you feel politically self-righteous, like about to spike a political football, be extra careful about sharing. Fear, anger, or moral charge is the cue to slow down.

**Q4 (MC).** Testicle tanning is best summarized as:
a) well supported by animal studies  b) no clinical trials and not even animal studies, and possibly harmful  c) approved by urologists  d) proven to double testosterone
**A:** b. Urologist Rena Malik says it does not work; Ranjith Ramasamy notes it may decrease testosterone and sperm counts.

**Q5 (T/F).** Testosterone decline is entirely a myth.
**A:** False. It is partially real (2021 Miami study, 1999-2016; most experts estimate 20-25% over two decades, not the documentary's claimed 50%), with complex likely causes; none supports a conspiracy to de-masculinize men.

**Q6 (SA).** What did the Marin County case show about ideology and misinformation?
**A:** Once anti-vaccine sentiment read as conservative, a wealthy, very Democratic county once known for it became one of the most vaccinated places in America (80% of 5-to-11-year-olds double-vaccinated, over double state and national rates). Ideology amplifies misinformation rather than being its source.

**Q7 (SA).** What is the 2022 Spanish study's "misinformation fingerprint"?
**A:** Across ~100,000 pieces of social media misinformation, versus accurate content, it was easier to process, more emotional, scarier, and focused on morality.

## Chapter 10: A Good Story and the Certainty Grift

**Q1 (SA).** Define the certainty grift.
**A:** Most people want to do good but rarely convert that into behaviour. The goodness illusion bridges the gap with false certainty about both the goal and the path (halos, wishful thinking, scary anecdotes) and sells it for profit, politics, or evidence-free agendas.

**Q2 (MC).** What share of Americans live a healthy lifestyle (2016 Mayo Clinic Proceedings)?
a) 2.7%  b) 27%  c) 50%  d) 75%
**A:** a. This intention-behaviour gap is the market map for the grift.

**Q3 (SA).** List the documentary evidence audit steps.
**A:** Does it rely on shocking anecdotes? Does it place claims in the broader body of evidence? Separate your emotional response to the production from your assessment of the data. Check whether an uncertain literature has been rendered as a definitive story.

**Q4 (SA).** What happened in the *What the Health* study of 160 health science students?
**A:** After viewing, their nutrition knowledge decreased, agreement with incorrect health claims increased, and many said they would change their diets. Subject-matter training did not protect them.

**Q5 (SA).** What does *Toxic Beauty* get wrong about talcum powder?
**A:** It leaves viewers believing the product-cancer link is clear, supported by billion-dollar settlements. But a large 2020 JAMA study found no statistically significant association between genital powder use and ovarian cancer, and a 2021 review found at most a weak association. Caulfield does not claim there is no connection, only that the literature is uncertain and underwhelming.

**Q6 (T/F).** Caulfield concludes that stories should be avoided in science communication.
**A:** False. Stories can be near-essential (Dahlstrom: narrative creates meaning from scattered facts); the problem is anecdote doing the work of data. Narrative is a delivery mechanism, not a truth signal.

**Q7 (SA).** What is "backlash" (2022 Rutgers study)?
**A:** People exposed to conflicting health information made more errors, were slower, reported more workload and nutritional confusion, and rejected emerging recommendations outright: "it's all so confusing, so I give up."

## Chapter 11: That Earthy Taste! The Opinion Economy

**Q1 (SA).** What is the opinion economy?
**A:** The vast, lightly regulated industry that monetizes our belief in strangers' authentic opinions and, in doing so, destroys the authenticity that made them valuable. A trillion-dollar enterprise per the chapter.

**Q2 (SA).** Why does the wisdom of the crowd fail for online reviews?
**A:** It requires independent judgments (the ox-weight example); reviewers see each other, so herding (social influence bias: +32% likelihood of a positive rating, +25% in final ratings) and anchoring corrupt the mechanism.

**Q3 (MC).** The "Goldilocks zone" of ratings that sophisticated fakers target is:
a) 5.0 stars  b) 4.2-4.5 stars with small complaints  c) 3.0 stars  d) 1.0 stars
**A:** b. All-five-star profiles actually reduce sales, so fakers add minor gripes (the stained blanket).

**Q4 (SA).** Describe the fake review cycle.
**A:** A business buys fake reviews to sell more, spends the extra revenue on ads with the platform hosting the fakes, so the platform, business, and fake reviewer profit; only the customer loses.

**Q5 (SA).** How did Oobah Butler make The Shed at Dulwich the top-ranked London restaurant?
**A:** A $13 burner phone for TripAdvisor verification, an appointment-only site with a menu of moods, styled fake food photos, and fake reviews (praising the "earthy taste" with a stained-blanket complaint). It rose from 18,149th of 18,149 to number one, with no food ever served; he refused all bookings, adding exclusivity.

**Q6 (T/F).** Having thousands of reviews averages out bias.
**A:** False. Garbage in, garbage out scales with the garbage: roughly 25-30% may be unreliable, and only 1.5% of one apparel company's customers wrote reviews (an extreme-value sample).

**Q7 (SA).** What are incentivized reviews and how much do they inflate ratings?
**A:** Reviews paid or discounted into existence; not technically fake, but they inflate ratings 40-70% and require FTC disclosure.

## Chapter 12: Reviews Are Often Wrong

**Q1 (SA).** What did the 2016 Amazon vs. Consumer Reports study find?
**A:** Across 1,200+ products, higher Amazon ratings scored higher with Consumer Reports only 57% of the time. Consumer Reports scores predicted used-product resale prices; star ratings had essentially no relationship to them.

**Q2 (SA).** Give the two mechanisms Donald Lichtenstein offered for consumer review inaccuracy.
**A:** Non-comparativeness (consumers evaluate one product in isolation) and timing (they review right after purchase, before living with the product).

**Q3 (SA).** State the knowledge asymmetry rule.
**A:** The more expertise separates you from the provider, the less your review tracks quality. Use reviews only for experience (wait times, courtesy), not clinical quality, which patients cannot judge.

**Q4 (MC).** What do studies find about online physician ratings?
a) They closely predict clinical outcomes  b) No clear relationship with clinical outcomes or peer-assessed performance  c) They predict mortality  d) They are all fake
**A:** b. Yet 84% of surveyed Americans would not see a physician rated under four stars.

**Q5 (T/F).** Tech-savvy users can reliably spot machine-generated fake reviews.
**A:** False. They detected them at rates indistinguishable from random guessing. Assume-you-can't-detect rule: treat your ability as near zero.

**Q6 (SA).** What did Marco Zenone's alternative cancer clinic analysis find?
**A:** 1,000+ reviews across 39 clinics averaged 4.4 stars (the believably good Goldilocks range), dominated by stage-four and terminal patients, for treatments with no supporting evidence; patients using alternative therapies for cancer have worse outcomes.

**Q7 (T/F).** Knowing that fakes exist protects you from them.
**A:** False. 52% of Australians suspect they have fallen for fakes and 86% of Germans still call reviews credible. Three in four Americans overestimate their ability to sort credible from bunk, and the most overconfident spread more falsehoods.

## Chapter 13: An Escape Plan: Polls, Taste Makers, and Liking What You Like

**Q1 (SA).** List the main steps of the escape plan for reviews.
**A:** Be very skeptical of online reviews; seek side-by-side comparisons in standardized environments; use independent expert outlets (Consumer Reports, Wirecutter, video reviews); use multiple comparative sources; ignore star ratings and aggregates; check your own motivated reading.

**Q2 (SA).** What are the four poll questions (Frank Graves)?
**A:** How big is the sample (~1,000 reasonable nationally)? Is it representative (random, not self-selecting)? Who did the research (survey experts or an influencer)? Any obvious biases, such as a sponsor with a vested interest? Also check question wording.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the spiral of silence?
**A:** People withhold opinions they believe are in the minority, so visible consensus silences dissent. A 2019 study found likes directly changed personal opinion as consensus cues.

**Q4 (MC).** FiveThirtyEight recomputed IMDb rankings using only women's ratings. Only what share of 4,000+ films had more ratings from women than men?
a) 2.4%  b) 25%  c) 50%  d) 80%
**A:** a (97 films). *The Godfather* fell from second to tenth. Lesson: an aggregate is a population; change the population and the "objective" answer changes.

**Q5 (SA).** What is the deliberate taste-widening experiment?
**A:** For a set period, engage attentively with things you claim to loathe, with iconography bracketed off. Caulfield's year of yacht rock, Fleetwood Mac, AC/DC, and disco expanded his "this is great" list by 30-40%, though some dislikes never budged.

**Q6 (T/F).** Caulfield recommends sharpening your skill at filtering real reviews from fake ones.
**A:** False. Experts say individual consumers cannot do it; substitute sources rather than sharpen your filter.

**Q7 (SA).** What are incidental similarities?
**A:** Non-political common ground that increases felt closeness and thereby openness to opposing political views; a route into contested conversations.

## Chapter 14: Conclusion: A Path Forward?

**Q1 (SA).** List the Certainty Six.
**A:** (1) Pause and recognize the ecosystem isn't rigged for accuracy, asking "is this accurate?" before sharing. (2) Watch for scienceploitation. (3) Resist the allure of science hype. (4) Take a closer look when goodness is used as marketing. (5) Always consider the existing body of evidence. (6) Like what you like.

**Q2 (SA).** What are the three sub-tests of Certainty Six #2?
**A:** Is the claim riding a hot topic (electricity, radiation, now stem cells, genomics, nanotech, microbiome, quantum)? Does actual research support it or just buzzwords (word salad)? Do scientific trappings appear in a field already associated with pseudoscience?

**Q3 (SA).** What are the steps of the body-of-evidence test?
**A:** Refuse single study syndrome; check your personal biases on the topic first; check retraction status and venue (search the title and see whether a breadth of academics cite it); ask what the scientific consensus is and find it via responsible aggregators (science communicators, journalists, university and government resources, credible professional bodies).

**Q4 (T/F).** The public tends to overestimate how strong scientific consensus is.
**A:** False. It underestimates it, because consensus "often isn't sexy" and contrarian views are overrepresented.

**Q5 (SA).** What is the Fundamental Fix?
**A:** Fix how science is funded, done, and communicated: reward quality over quantity; shut down fake journals; retract bad science faster and make retractions unmistakable; fund replication and negative results; open data; enforce integrity; and have publicly funded researchers correct the record when their field is spun.

**Q6 (MC).** What discriminates valid contrarianism (plate tectonics, bacterial ulcers) from grift?
a) the charisma of the speaker  b) venue: raised and tested in scientific venues versus ranting on podcasts and cable news  c) popularity  d) age of the claim
**A:** b.

**Q7 (SA).** How did Caulfield apply the test to his own book?
**A:** He found studies he planned to cite skewed toward predatory venues and cut them ("Punt."). He admitted his own work sits inside the same distorting incentives, and answered by running the body-of-evidence test on his sources rather than abandoning citation.

## Cumulative Final Exam

1. **State the thesis of the book in two sentences and explain why trust level is a risk indicator.**
   *A:* The more we seek certainty, the harder it is to find, because the guideposts we rely on (science, goodness, opinion) attract distortion in proportion to their value. The signals worth faking are the ones people trust most.

2. **Name the Three Illusions and, for each, one countermeasure from the book.**
   *A:* Science Illusion (body-of-evidence test / single study rule / red-flag audit); Goodness Illusion (health halo deconstruction / separate the goal from the intervention); Opinion Illusion (review escape plan / four poll questions).

3. **A clinic's website cites one study as proof its therapy works. Describe what you would check.**
   *A:* Single study syndrome rule; red-flag audit (publisher, affiliation, acceptance interval, ethics review, methods, author history), retraction status, citation pattern, then the scientific consensus via aggregators. Also check whether risks and limitations are stated and whether a legitimate governing body (e.g., ISSCR) says it is clinic-ready.

4. **What do the Galileo gambit, the pseudo-profound bullshit finding, and the science-y language studies have in common?**
   *A:* All manufacture the illusion of certainty: persecution framing converts missing evidence into evidence of suppression; vagueness is mistaken for profundity; opacity is rated as credibility regardless of education.

5. **Explain how hype and predatory publishing both trace back to the same incentive, and what fix addresses it.**
   *A:* Publish-or-perish and quantity-over-quality rewards (and funding that rewards exaggerated promises). Fixes: reward quality, block public funds paying predatory fees, funding by lottery, understatement as default.

6. **Why does a retracted paper like Wakefield's keep circulating?**
   *A:* Post-retraction citations rarely note the retraction (5.4%), retracted papers attract more attention, slow retraction lets misinformation entrench, and motivated reasoners fold the retraction into a persecution story.

7. **A product says it is "toxin-free" and "natural." Walk through the deconstruction.**
   *A:* Ask what the words mean (regulator definition?), what they imply, whether supported. Dose makes the poison; appeal to nature fallacy (arsenic, venom, lightning); try "evil-spirits-free"; read the ingredient list; ask what replaced removed ingredients (substitution risk).

8. **Someone responds to your evidence with "surely you want safe cosmetics?" Name the move and your reply.**
   *A:* Motte-and-bailey. Restate the original contested claim (that this product is definitively safer than alternatives) and return the discussion to it.

9. **How can a noble cause bias research and a documentary? Give two examples.**
   *A:* White hat bias (hydroxychloroquine and COVID; CBD placebo loop) and the certainty grift via a good story (*Toxic Beauty* talc; *What the Health*). Noble goals lower the evidence bar; production quality and anecdote make weak data feel definitive.

10. **You are choosing a washing machine, a physician, and a poll to believe. Apply the book's guidance to each.**
    *A:* Washing machine: independent comparative expert testing (Consumer Reports, Wirecutter), multiple sources, ignore star ratings (57% agreement, no link to resale prices). Physician: knowledge asymmetry means reviews track experience not clinical quality; use with extreme caution. Poll: four questions (size ~1,000, representative, who did it, sponsor bias). Throughout, assume you cannot detect fakes, pause, and "like what you like."
