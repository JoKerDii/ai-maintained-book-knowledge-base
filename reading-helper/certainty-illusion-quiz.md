# The Certainty Illusion: Quiz Book

**Source note:** This quiz reflects the *certainty-illusion* skill's chapter notes (summaries of Timothy Caulfield's "The Certainty Illusion: What You Don't Know and Why It Matters"), not the book's own wording. Every fact, number and story below comes from those notes; where the notes are silent, nothing is claimed.

## How to use this quiz
- The book has 15 chapters (Introduction + 13 + Conclusion). They are grouped into 8 sections of 1-2 chapters. Each section has 10 questions.
- Cover the answers (scroll only to the question), write or say your answer, then check. Mark each question Got it / Partly / Missed.
- Question types: short answer (SA), true/false (T/F), multiple choice (MC), explain in your own words (EXPLAIN), and scenario/application (SCENARIO: "a manager faces X, which concept applies and why?"). Every section has at least 2 scenarios.
- Re-do every "Missed" question after 24 hours, then try the 20-question cumulative final exam at the end.
- Chapter map: Part I Science Illusion = Ch 1-5; Part II Goodness Illusion = Ch 6-10; Part III Opinion Illusion = Ch 11-13; Ch 0 = Introduction; Ch 14 = Conclusion.

## Section 1: Introduction and The Science-y Veneer (Ch 0-1)

**Q1 (SA).** State the book's thesis, "the certainty illusion," in one sentence, and say what it implies about how to treat the level of trust we place in a source.

**A1.** The more we seek certainty, the harder it becomes to find, because the guideposts we rely on become more valuable precisely as they become more distorted. Implication: trust level is a *risk indicator*, not a safety indicator, since "distortion follows value" and the signals worth faking are the ones people trust most. (Ch 0)

**Q2 (T/F).** The book's main diagnosis is that the problem is downstream misinformation, i.e., individual lies spreading online.

**A2.** False. Caulfield calls it a **knowledge production crisis**: degradation of the methods and institutions that make knowledge (publish-or-perish, hype incentives, predatory journals), not just the downstream misinformation that gets the attention. The upstream system keeps manufacturing raw material. (Ch 0)

**Q3 (MC).** Which pairing of illusion and guidepost is correct?
(a) Science Illusion: reviews, rankings and polls are faked; (b) Goodness Illusion: the appearance of science replaces science; (c) Opinion Illusion: reviews, rankings and polls are faked, biased or manufactured; (d) Opinion Illusion: a messaging strategy that makes a choice feel like "the right thing."

**A3.** (c). Science Illusion = the appearance of science replaces science (Part I); Goodness Illusion = a messaging strategy making a choice feel right without close examination (Part II); Opinion Illusion = faked/biased/manufactured reviews, rankings, polls (Part III). Use them as a triage layer: identify which guidepost a claim leans on, then apply that Part's countermeasures. (Ch 0)

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** In your own words, what is the "great flattening of knowledge," and how is it different from "equal access to knowledge" or postmodern relativism?

**A4.** It is a race to the bottom in which real science, worthy goals and valued opinion are replaced by *illusions* of each, so everything becomes equally bad and meaningless. It is not equal access to knowledge and not relativism ("all views are equally valid"); it is the degradation of the signals themselves. (Ch 0)

**Q5 (SA).** At the opening lecture, a hostile questioner said, "Why should we trust you?" and "Science is full of s***." What did Caulfield do, and why was that the book's method in miniature?

**A5.** After his reflex (more citations plus the "science is a process, not an institution" speech) he conceded: "Yeah, it is." The man was partly right (the literature is polluted by predatory journals, hype incentives, salami science and fraud), and the objection is an *incentive* critique, not a factual gap, so more studies would not answer it. The method: grant the corruption of the guidepost, then argue the repair is better science, not no science. (Ch 0)

**Q6 (SA, numbers).** Give the following figures from Chapter 0: (a) share of Americans who say they rely "a lot" on their own research for big decisions, and what share of those do so online; (b) share who say they trust science; (c) share of clinical trials that reach clinical application.

**A6.** (a) 81%, and 96% of those research online, inside the same rigged environment. (b) About 90% say they trust science while also embracing astrology and conspiracies, so the fight is over "who gets to wear the costume." (c) Roughly 10%, usually with less impressive efficacy than promised, so calibrate "breakthrough" headlines. (Ch 0)

**Q7 (SA).** Describe the scienceploitation cycle and give two of the "hot" topics the notes list.

**A7.** An exciting scientific development generates pop-culture noise; hucksters spot a market opening; the market floods with products riffing on the new science; people get hurt; public discourse is confused. Hot topics named include stem cells, regenerative medicine, microbiome, nanotech, genomics, neuroscience, precision medicine, cannabis, psychedelics, quantum, AI. Historical prototype: Mesmer's 1779 "animal magnetism." (Ch 1)

**Q8 (MC).** Physicist Mark Freeman's observation underpins the "quantum test." What is it?
(a) Quantum physics is never relevant to health; (b) he has never seen *quantum* used accurately in association with a health product; (c) quantum products are safe but useless; (d) quantum claims need two studies.

**A8.** (b). The rule: if *quantum* explains a health product or procedure, stop evaluating, assume pseudoscience and a fraudster nearby. It is a blunt consumer-health heuristic and is not to be exported to physics contexts. (Ch 1)

**Q9 (SA).** Summarise the stem-cell clinic study from Chapter 1: what was examined, what the ISSCR says, and the key number.

**A9.** Caulfield, Zarzeczny and Murdoch examined roughly 250 alternative-medicine clinic websites advertising stem-cell therapies (pain, lung disease, sexual enhancement, anti-aging; cells from bone marrow, cord blood, even apples). The ISSCR says proven stem-cell treatments are extremely few (some blood/immune disorders via bone-marrow transplant, certain tissue transplants such as skin and cornea); everything else is experimental. Only **19%** of clinic sites mentioned any limitation of the evidence. A separate 2015 skincare analysis (with Rachul and Percec) found most products presented as ready for use, few substantiated, fewer mentioning risk. (Ch 1)

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A wellness brand sells a "quantum-balanced, immune-boosting" cleanse "for the microbiome," says it is "scientifically proven," names no risks, and offers no explanation of how it works. Which concepts apply, and what do you do?

**A10.** Several at once: the **quantum test** (stop evaluating); **scienceploitation** (hot topics: quantum, microbiome; no risks stated, which is diagnostic); **immune boosting** as a near-reliable scam marker (you cannot "boost" the immune system as promised, and an overactive one is what autoimmune disease is); **science washing** ("scientifically proven" without *relevant* research); and the **explanation demand** (no plain-language mechanism means the language is there to impress). The incoherent stew of "natural" plus cutting-edge science is itself a tell. Do not debate the jargon; ask for the body of evidence. (Ch 1, Ch 6)

**Q10b (SCENARIO).** A relative reads a scary post combining technical-sounding jargon with a fear message about a food additive. How do you apply the "science-y/scary dyad"?

**A10b.** Separate the two channels: strip the jargon and ask what the claimed harm actually is; strip the fear and ask what evidence supports the mechanism. Neither usually survives alone. Expect that merely reciting accurate-but-boring facts will lose to fear (negativity bias: a 2019 17-country experiment found people everywhere are more physiologically activated by negative news), so lead with procedure not just facts. (Ch 1)

> Bonus recall (Ch 1): Name three research findings on why science-y language works. *Answer:* a 2013 study (trappings of science create an illusion of authority even for pseudoscientific topics); a 2023 study (people who hear vague scientific claims later misremember them as definitive); a 2004 study (science-y language raised persuasiveness regardless of attribution to a real study or audience education; science majors and grad students were equally swayed). Also the 2020 Ohio State jargon disengagement finding: dense verbiage reduces non-expert engagement even with lay definitions supplied.

## Section 2: Certainty, Dunning-Kruger and The Hype Machine (Ch 2-3)

**Q1 (SA).** Who tends to be most confident about contested science, according to the 2019 anti-GMO study and its 2022 replication?

**A1.** "Extreme opponents know the least, but think they know the most." A large 2022 study replicated the pattern across climate change, nuclear power, vaccination, evolution and GMOs. This is the **Dunning-Kruger effect**: lacking knowledge impedes recognizing the lack ("first rule of the Dunning-Kruger club: you don't know you're in it"). (Ch 2)

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** Explain the Galileo gambit and give two of the four ways to dismantle it.

**A2.** The claimant answers evidence by casting themself as the silenced visionary: "they laughed at Galileo." Dismantling: (1) Galileo faced a religious orthodoxy, not a scientific consensus produced by the process he inspired; (2) Sagan's rebuttal: they also laughed at Bozo the Clown; (3) real revolutionaries (Einstein, Darwin, Curie) produced data tested against their era's conventions rather than campaigning to be "the next Galileo"; (4) classify it as a defence of last resort, not an argument. It converts absence of evidence into evidence of suppression. (Ch 2)

**Q3 (MC).** The Sagan standard says:
(a) Dismiss all fringe claims; (b) extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, scaled to improbability, with the onus on the claimant; (c) consensus is always right; (d) new claims need three replications.

**A3.** (b). Method: assess plausibility against the existing knowledge base, assess the claimant (relevant expertise? selling something? claiming to know what thousands of researchers missed?), scale the proof to the improbability. It keeps a cautiously open mind without letting a fringe assertion destabilize a body of evidence. (Ch 2)

**Q4 (SA).** What is the "humility fix," and what is Caulfield's exercise?

**A4.** Make intellectual humility ("recognition of one's fallibility as a knower") the default posture. Exercise: list areas in your life where evidence evolved and you changed position, and list your live grey areas. An empty list is itself the finding (revisit the narcissism material). Studies link humility to resistance to political and health misinformation, better decisions, empathy, tolerance and gratitude. (Ch 2)

**Q5 (T/F).** Gwyneth Paltrow's response to criticism (e.g., after being fined for false marketing on jade vagina eggs) is a soft, non-explicit Galileo gambit ("we were just early").

**A5.** True, per the notes: the "we were just early" defence is a soft version of the gambit. (Ch 2)

**Q6 (SA).** What did Pennycook's Ig Nobel-winning study (2015) do, and what was the 2019 follow-up finding?

**A6.** "On the Reception and Detection of Pseudo-Profound Bullshit" built syntactically coherent sentences from random vague buzzwords (using Deepak Chopra's output to map BS characteristics); participants rated them at least somewhat profound. The 2019 follow-up added the **labelling effect**: identical BS is rated more profound when a famous author's name is attached. It illustrates people confusing vagueness for profundity. (Ch 2)

**Q7 (SA, numbers).** Give five hype numbers from Chapter 3: the Sumner press-release figures, the abstract-spin figure, the Tsukuba NIH finding, and the media-attention study.

**A7.** Sumner et al. (2014), 462 press releases: 40% exaggerated health advice, 33% exaggerated causation, 36% exaggerated inferences from animal studies. About 40% of biomedical abstracts contain spin (2017). Tsukuba team, nearly a million successful NIH abstracts 1985-2020: hype up roughly 1,400%, with NIH's own funding announcements also showing a massive increase. A 2023 study of 14,000 papers: media attention was *negatively* associated with replication. (Ch 3)

**Q8 (EXPLAIN).** Why does Caulfield call hype "an emission, not a lie," and what fix does he propose at the funder level?

**A8.** Hype is the predictable byproduct of a funding system that rewards exaggerated promises ("impact merchants" who describe "marketizing" research as survival; "hot stuff bias"), so fix incentives rather than blame individuals. Funding by lottery (Page & Barnett): peer review decides basic fundability and the rest is allocated by lottery; cheaper, faster, more equitable, and it removes the incentive to hype (tried by the Swiss National Science Foundation and Health Research Council of New Zealand). Context: Canadian success rates ~15%; one large Australian competition wasted 600+ years of researcher time. (Ch 3)

**Q9 (SA).** Give the Chapter 3 evidence that grant ranking is not a quality signal and the examples of failed economic-impact projections.

**A9.** A 2018 PNAS study of 40+ reviewers on the same grants found no agreement among reviewers; a 2016 analysis of 100,000+ successful NIH grants found no relationship between ranking and later productivity. Projections: Texas (2009) 230,000 jobs and $88 billion; New Jersey 20,000 jobs and $71.9 billion; California's 2004 analysis promised $6.4-12.6 billion health-care savings from cures for stroke, Parkinson's, spinal cord injury and Alzheimer's and persuaded voters to commit $3 billion; two decades on none of those therapies exist. Also, a "five to ten years or sooner" promise appeared in almost 70% of stem-cell newspaper articles Caulfield analysed (with Kamenova), impossible when trials take 10-15 years and ~90% fail. (Ch 3)

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A university press office wants to describe a mouse study as a "breakthrough that could cure" a disease within five to ten years. As the communications adviser, which concepts apply and what do you recommend?

**A10.** The **hype pipeline** (press-release stage), Sumner's finding that animal-to-human inference is among the most common exaggerations, and the **timeline test** (10-15 years, ~90% failure). Recommend **understatement as default**: strip breakthrough/revolution language, state limitations and honest timelines, still make it engaging (collaborate with artists/writers). Caulfield's rule: if genuine, you will hear it from a range of reputable sources with a supporting body of evidence. (Ch 3, Ch 14)

**Q10b (SCENARIO).** A colleague says a charismatic expert defies the consensus, "just like Galileo, and plate tectonics proved the contrarians right." How do you respond using this section's concepts?

**A10b.** Apply the **Sagan standard** (plausibility, claimant's expertise and stake, scale the proof) and the Galileo-gambit dismantling. Plate tectonics and H. pylori ulcers won because data eventually supported them, not because proponents were disbelieved or proclaimed on podcasts; consensus produced by evidence is not groupthink. Venue is the discriminator (Ch 14). Also note the overconfidence data: conspiracy believers overestimate how many others agree with them. (Ch 2, Ch 14)

> Bonus recall (Ch 3): Catastrophizing is the mirror image of hype (overstated harms, "academic cottage industry"); utopian and dystopian narratives are the same machine running in two directions. Walkable neighbourhoods, food and exercise policy, smoking cessation and community mental health are unglamorous high-impact areas crowded out by Big Science hype. At a 2013 whole-genome-screening workshop a senior genetics researcher told Caulfield to stop criticizing genomics' clinical value or the money would go to stem cells.

## Section 3: The Predator Problem and AI and Zombie Science (Ch 4-5)

**Q1 (SA).** Define predatory, hijacked and vanity/agenda-driven journals, and give the Chapter 4 market estimates.

**A1.** Predatory: charge authors fees with lax or no peer review (est. 11,000-16,000 journals; likely 750,000+ articles a year; ~$400 million annually, some estimates in the billions). Hijacked: criminal clones of a legitimate journal's name and domain ("fake Gucci handbag"); fraud, distinct from predatory publishing. Vanity/agenda: purpose-built pretend journals to legitimize a predetermined position (anti-vaccine, climate denial, alternative medicine, the defunct Holocaust-denier *Journal of Historical Review*), often publishing their own editorial board. (Ch 4)

**Q2 (SA).** List the red-flag audit items for a single study.

**A2.** (1) Publisher: known predatory operator? (2) Author affiliation: vague or mimicking a real institution? (3) Submission-to-acceptance interval: biomedical papers take 91-639 days, ~100 average (2021); two weeks means no real review. (4) Ethics review done? (5) Methods: control group, blinding, standardization, sample size. (6) The author's regulatory history. Add retraction status and the citation pattern. (Ch 4, patterns)

**Q3 (EXPLAIN).** Walk through the O-Shot case and what each audit item revealed.

**A3.** Dr. Jen Gunter called Dr. Charles Runels's trademarked platelet-rich-plasma "O-Shot" potentially very dangerous; Runels posted "just some of the research": one small study, Runels as lead author, eleven self-reporting women, no control, no blinding, no standardization. Audit: publisher OMICS International (fined $50 million by the FTC in 2019 for deceiving authors about peer review); affiliation "Medical School Birmingham" (a modest two-storey building with a dumpster, 250 miles from the real University of Alabama at Birmingham school); submitted June 12, accepted June 25; no ethics review, from an author already sanctioned by the FDA for an ethics violation. Result: clinics worldwide sell it for thousands of dollars and outlets (*Cosmopolitan*, *Harper's Bazaar*, *Daily Mail*, *The Sun*) covered it uncritically although no good trial supports it. One predatory paper manufactured an industry's certainty. (Ch 4)

**Q4 (SA).** What was the citation-pattern finding about the *International Journal of Vaccine Theory, Practice, and Research*?

**A4.** Nearly all citations came from the same journal, from its own editorial board (often in predatory venues) or from known anti-vaccine advocates. Echo-chambering is diagnostic: if the broader academic community does not pick a paper up, even to contest it, be suspicious. (Ch 4)

**Q5 (T/F).** Presence in PubMed, Scopus, MEDLINE or a WHO library validates a paper.

**A5.** False. Abalkina found ~400 publications from hijacked journals in the WHO COVID-19 library; a 2023 study found Scopus carrying work from 67+ hijacked journals; Moher and Lalu found 800+ policies citing papers from a single predatory publisher. Publication is "an address, not a credential." (Ch 4)

**Q6 (SA).** What is the Ch 4 policy fix and the incentive evidence behind it?

**A6.** Prohibit public research funds from paying predatory fees (no funds, no industry; most academics would not pay out of pocket). Incentive evidence: in South Africa, pay tied to publication quantity produced up to a 140-fold increase in predatory publications; a 2020 review of 20 Canadian universities' tenure policies found no language discouraging predatory publishing, and a small Canadian study linked it to greater financial reward. Yeo-Teh and Tang argue willful submission is misconduct. (Ch 4)

**Q7 (SA).** State the single study syndrome rule and who it comes from. Why does it work against many frauds at once?

**A7.** Ivan Oransky (Retraction Watch): if a claim rests on one study, be "much more suspicious." Always consider the body of evidence. It neutralizes predatory papers, hijacked papers, agenda journals, AI-fabricated abstracts and zombie retractions without detecting each fraud individually. Caulfield calls it the most important takeaway of Part I. (Ch 5)

**Q8 (EXPLAIN).** Tell the Wakefield zombie story: what was the paper, what went wrong, and why it still lives.

**A8.** In 1998 Andrew Wakefield led a *Lancet* paper linking the measles vaccine to autism: a case study of twelve children, no randomization, no control, biased sample, unable to show causation; he asserted a link at a press conference anyway. Media (*Daily Mail*, *NYT*, CNN, *60 Minutes*) and Jenny McCarthy amplified it; vaccination fell, outbreaks rose, children died. Brian Deer found it was funded by lawyers acting for parents suing manufacturers and that "no case was free of misreporting or alteration." Wakefield lost his licence (2010, GMC: "dishonestly and irresponsibly"); *The Lancet* retracted in 2010, twelve years on. Yet in 2015, 21% of Americans under thirty (and 21% in an Alberta survey of ~3,000) believed vaccines cause autism; post-retraction citations rose (337 references 2011-2017, ~62% not noting the retraction). Activists reanimate it and fold the retraction into a persecution narrative. (Ch 5)

**Q9 (SA, numbers).** Give the retraction-citation numbers: the 5.4% study, the *Economist* analysis, the Michigan attention paradox, and the "89% unaware" study.

**A9.** 13,000+ papers retracted 1960-2020: only 5.4% of post-retraction references acknowledged it. *Economist*, 20,000 retracted articles: 84% got at least one post-retraction citation, 95,000 in total, cited in a further 1.65 million papers. Michigan 2022, ~4,000 retracted papers: *more* attention over ten years than never-retracted ones (retracted content is often more exciting/extreme). A 2022 study: most authors say they check references yet 89% were unaware a cited article was retracted. (Ch 5)

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A journal editor asks you how to redesign retraction. Another asks how to cope with AI-generated submissions. Which concepts apply?

**A10.** Retraction: Holden Thorp's **two-stage retraction** (stage one decides validity with no blame so a defective paper can be removed, even provisionally; stage two investigates misconduct, which is slow); the UK parliament's April 2023 report proposed no longer than two months (retractions currently average 2-3 years; Wakefield took over ten); and make retraction unmissable (red banner, in databases, Google/Google Scholar). AI: ChatGPT writes believable abstracts experts could not identify (Northwestern/Chicago); Eurasia Group called AI a "weapon of mass disruption"; Wachter warns of losing the "middleman" experts. Shift defence from detecting fakes to structural habits (body of evidence, venue, citation pattern). Also paper mills (multi-million-dollar authorship-selling industry). (Ch 5)

**Q10b (SCENARIO).** A friend forwards a "peer-reviewed" study in an unfamiliar journal proving a controversial wellness claim. Walk through your steps.

**A10b.** Apply the **body-of-evidence test** and red-flag audit: refuse single study syndrome, check the publisher, affiliation, acceptance interval, ethics review, methods, retraction status and who cites it (search PubMed/Google Scholar). The claim being counter-consensus *and* convenient for an agenda means verify the venue first. Do not accept "peer-reviewed" because the journal says so (an anti-vaccine correspondent made that claim about a journal that isn't). (Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 14)

## Section 4: Health Halos, the Devious Dozen, Clean Beauty and Pets (Ch 6-7)

**Q1 (SA).** What is a health halo and what is its real function?

**A1.** A word or phrase creating "a vibe or an immediate impression of health" unsupported by evidence; its real function is delivering a shortcut to *certainty* by exploiting our desire to do good, not a health claim. Supporting finding: a 2018 University of Chicago study showed people judge good/bad much faster than expected on little information. (Ch 6)

**Q2 (SA).** List the twelve terms of the Devious Dozen.

**A2.** Natural, holistic, healthy, organic, non-GMO, gluten-free, chemical-free, toxin-free, locally grown, the colour green, immune-boosting, personalized. Caulfield's point is not that each is definitively wrong but that each projects implied certainty it hasn't earned. (Ch 6)

**Q3 (T/F).** "Clean" is one of the Devious Dozen.

**A3.** False; "clean" is deliberately held out because it dominates a different market (beauty), covered in Chapter 7. (Ch 6-7)

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** Trace "immune boosting" end to end, including the BMJ Open study's numbers.

**A4.** The goal is appealing, but the immune system is a balanced network, not a muscle; an overactive one produces autoimmune disease and anaphylaxis. During COVID supplement sales rose as much as 60% in some countries. A "naive computer" search across 225+ websites found ~86% portrayed the concept inaccurately (including COVID protection) and fewer than 10% offered critique. Trusted outlets (NYT, Harvard Health, Mayo Clinic) ran "boost your immune system" headlines; a Columbia study found 60% of shared links are never opened, so the headline is the message. Harm: people feel protected and displace vaccination and sleep. (Ch 6)

**Q5 (SA).** Non-GMO as the contrast case: what are the consensus and the public perception numbers?

**A5.** Consensus is unusually complete (2016 NAS, Purdue, Health Canada, FDA, FAO, WHO): no association between GE foods and disease, plus higher yields and reduced pesticide use. Yet GMO safety has the largest gap between scientific consensus and public perception of any issue, larger than climate change, vaccines or evolution. Pew, 20 countries: nearly half think GM food unsafe; only 13% say safe. (Ch 6)

**Q6 (SA).** Give the counter-facts for "organic/local," "personalized," and "toxin-free."

**A6.** Organic means something about production, nothing about nutrition/safety; organic farms are generally less efficient with lower yields; transport is only ~5-10% of food's footprint versus >80% for production. Personalized: two JAMA RCTs (2018 genetic diet; 2022 glycemic-response diet) found no weight-loss advantage; it also shifts responsibility from society to the individual. Toxin-free: the dose makes the poison, so it is content-free; test with "evil-spirits-free." (Ch 6)

**Q7 (EXPLAIN).** What is the motte-and-bailey fallacy and how do you answer it?

**A7.** Advance a controversial weak claim (bailey: clean beauty is definitively safer); when challenged, retreat to an unarguable truism (motte: we all want safe cosmetics) and treat it as vindication. Answer: write down the original claim, ask whether the truism is what the debate is actually about, return to the bailey, and notice the framing trap that casts you as the villain. Politicians use it too (examples in notes: urban tanks behind "safer streets," fluoride removal behind "toxin-free water"). (Ch 7)

**Q8 (EXPLAIN).** Trace the parabens story from the 2004 Darbre study to the current evidence.

**A8.** Darbre's 2004 study detected parabens in 20 breast tumours with no comparison group; it tested whether they could be *measured*, not whether they cause cancer, and she later clarified that, but the correction never reached the public. A 2007 *Daily Mail* headline ("5 lbs of damaging chemicals a year") helped make paraben-free the founding premise of clean beauty. Later: FDA (2022) has no information showing health effects as used; Cancer Research UK (2020): parabens do not cause cancer; Schwarcz: extremely weak estrogenic effect, less allergenic than alternatives. Parabens (since the 1930s) are chemically identical to those in blueberries and carrots. Avoidance does lower measured urine paraben (2020) but with no demonstrated health benefit. (Ch 7)

**Q9 (SA).** What is substitution risk, and what evidence illustrates it?

**A9.** Replacing a well-studied ingredient with a poorly studied "natural" one is information loss, not safety gain. A 2022 study of ~1,500 "clean" products at Target and Walgreens found most contain a potential allergen (mainly fragrances, botanicals); dermatologist Liszewski said the paraben panic pushed preservatives that are more allergenic; "clean" toothpaste often drops fluoride and a 2018 review found fluoride-free toothpaste failed to reduce caries; Schwarcz's "preservative-free becomes bacteria-filled"; some clean companies recalled products for mold, yeast, bacteria. (Ch 7)

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A small-business owner is pitching "human-grade, grain-free, holistic" pet food because "that's what health-conscious owners eat." A veterinary nutrition adviser pushes back. Which concepts apply, and what evidence is available?

**A10.** **Pet humanization** and the **pet-halo transfer test** (a 2021 study: owners who adopt grain-free/keto/gluten-free/non-GMO eating impose it on pets). Evidence: grain-free pet food is now nearly half of products (up from 15% a decade ago) and is linked to canine heart problems under FDA investigation; 88% of veterinary professionals with dogs and 73% with cats said grain-free is not healthy; 2018 study: "grain-free" cat food often has no less carbohydrate; 45% of pet products contain ingredients not on the label; food allergies are rare and mostly protein-related, with top allergens (beef, dairy, chicken) the very ingredients in homemade diets; UC Davis (2013): 67 home-prepared diets showed major nutritional imbalances; FDA warns raw pet food is more likely to carry *Salmonella*. (Ch 7)

**Q10b (SCENARIO).** A marketing director defends a "clean" skincare line: "Parabens are bad, we removed them because consumers don't want them. Who could oppose safer products?" Identify the concepts and the right response.

**A10b.** **Clean beauty deconstruction** (no regulator or industry definition; circular: clean = non-toxic = clean), **fear marketing / create the need, sell the fix** (Romanowski: the industry is left "telling new stories about the same old products"), and **motte-and-bailey** ("who could oppose safer products?"). Note the Drunk Elephant founder Tiffany Masterson's quote: "I don't think they are bad for you," removed because "consumers don't want them." Distinguish "we should research more" from "unsafe." Lawsuit outcomes are not scientific findings. (Ch 7)

> Bonus recall (Ch 6-7): Halo behavioural effects: perceived healthiness increases portions and calories (removes guilt). "Holistic" implies science-informed clinicians do not treat the whole person; "zero plus zero is still zero." Health halo supergroup = stacking halos; halo plus scienceploitation merge (non-GMO stem cell injections; microbiome-boosting natural colonics). Ted Kyle: ask "what is the pattern?" not "is this item good or bad?" Rubin and Brod, *JAMA Dermatology* 2019: misinformation may drive contact dermatitis and unnecessary avoidance of safe ingredients.

## Section 5: The Goodness Bias and Manly Ideology (Ch 8-9)

**Q1 (SA).** Define white hat bias, who coined it, and why noble topics are riskier.

**A1.** Allison and Cope (2010): "Distortion of research-based information in the service of what may be perceived as righteous ends." Research on noble topics is more likely to get funded, published, hyped and believed; people accept lower-quality data as conclusive when it fits righteous ends. Observational work is especially susceptible because selective analysis and reporting are easier with weaker data. (Ch 8)

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** How does hydroxychloroquine illustrate white hat bias?

**A2.** The goal (an urgent COVID therapy) was unarguable. A 2021 study found publication bias favouring positive results, with positive findings from lower-quality studies carrying more influence than higher-quality RCTs showing no benefit and possible harm; the drug was ultimately ineffective. The paper's conclusion: quality "may be negatively affected by white hat bias, which comes from personal beliefs and the urgent need of an effective intervention." Nonrandomized studies were the most susceptible. (Ch 8)

**Q3 (SA).** Describe the CBD loop (Karolinska 2022).

**A3.** A study of cannabinoids for pain found placebo responses contribute significantly to pain reduction in cannabinoid RCTs, and the placebo response was itself bolstered by media coverage of earlier placebo-driven positive studies. Placebo produces positive trials, which produce coverage, which produces more placebo. Also Caulfield, Snyder and Zenone (2020): CBD cancer-therapy claims on crowdfunding sites were riddled with misinformation. (Ch 8)

**Q4 (T/F).** A 2015 analysis found that methodological rigour boosted press coverage, e.g., large RCTs got more press releases.

**A4.** False. Rigour had no effect on press coverage, large RCTs were no more likely to get a press release, and news media are *more* likely to cover low-quality observational studies. Hence media coverage is not a quality proxy. (Ch 8)

**Q5 (SA).** What do the 2019 Southern Mississippi NIH-investigator study and the ancient-aliens material show about the goodness bias?

**A5.** In 100+ NIH-funded PIs, scientists rated unethical research practices as more defensible when the research was for noble reasons. Ancient aliens: pseudoarchaeology (Stephanie Halmhofer links it to antisemitism, white supremacism, occult fascism and QAnon) is aimed almost entirely at non-white civilizations (Giza, Machu Picchu, Sacsayhuamán, Pumapunku, Teotihuacán, Rapa Nui, never the Colosseum); "harmless fun" can carry an ideological payload. A Finnish 2013 study: paranormal believers are more prone to pareidolia. (Ch 8)

**Q6 (EXPLAIN).** What is the "masculinity as a health halo" idea, and how is the goal different from safer food?

**A6.** "Manly" works like "natural" or "clean" as a no-brainer proxy for good that lets sellers attach it to unproven interventions (Mallory DeMille: the sell is "masculinity optimization, testosterone boosting, anti-woke diets"). Unlike safe food or cosmetics, the goal itself is contested, and evidence runs the other way: conformity to traditional masculine norms is associated with worse outcomes (less help-seeking, more heart and infectious disease, poorer cancer outcomes, smoking, risky drinking, less sleep, loneliness, conspiracy belief, worse mental health per a 2017 review, a 2021 depression study, 2020 suicidal-ideation work). Much of it is observational, but the direction is consistent. (Ch 9)

**Q7 (SA).** Trace testicle tanning: claim, premise, evidence, and the correction on testosterone numbers.

**A7.** Claim (Tucker Carlson's *The End of Men*, guest "Raw Egg Nationalist"): red light on the testicles raises testosterone to counter a globalist-engineered crisis. Premise: testosterone decline is partly real (2021 Miami study, 1999-2016; experts estimate 20-25% over two decades, not the documentary's 50%) with complex causes (obesity, diabetes, diet, sleep, chemicals, COVID). Evidence: urologist Rena Malik says no scientific evidence supports it; Caulfield found no clinical trials, not even animal studies; Ranjith Ramasamy says it may decrease testosterone and sperm counts. Sellers (e.g., "Tantric Academy") fuse manly and natural halos. (Ch 9)

**Q8 (SA).** State Kate Starbird's rule and the 2022 Spanish study finding.

**A8.** "Whenever some piece of content makes me feel politically self-righteous, like I'm about to spike a political football, that's when I know I need to be extra careful about sharing." The Spanish study of ~100,000 pieces of misinformation found it is easier to process, more emotional, scarier and more moralized than accurate content. Emotion first, evidence second. (Ch 9)

**Q9 (SA).** What do the Liver King, raw eggs and the Marin County flip illustrate?

**A9.** Liver King: built a multi-million-dollar supplement business on raw organ meat and "ancestral living" while spending $11,000 a month on steroids and growth hormone (archaeology shows early humans ate diverse diets). Raw eggs carry *Salmonella* risk (thousands hospitalized, hundreds killed annually in the US). Marin County: famously New Age and anti-vaccine (2015 measles outbreak) became among the most vaccinated places (80% of 5-11-year-olds double-vaccinated, over double state/national rates) once anti-vaccine sentiment read as conservative: ideology is an amplifier, not a source. (Ch 9)

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A health-podcast host with a large following tells male listeners that "real men" optimize testosterone with a supplement stack and an extreme raw diet. A listener asks you whether to follow it. Which frameworks apply?

**A10.** **Separate goal from intervention** (is the goal argued or assumed? what is the evidence for *this* intervention?), **check whether the pitch manufactured the insecurity** (insecurity is the product; manhood-threatening contexts elicit aggression, per Duke 2021), the **Starbird emotional-signal check**, and **white hat/goodness bias** (a strong goal paired with weak evidence is a red-flag pairing). Evidence-based alternative: exercise, sleep, real food, no smoking, alcohol moderate or none; see a physician if genuinely concerned. Do not tell people how to be a man; the debunk targets the health claim. (Ch 9)

**Q10b (SCENARIO).** A public-health officer wants to recruit reluctant men to screening by leaning into masculine norms. What tension do the notes flag?

**A10b.** Leaning into masculine norms may get reluctant men to seek help but risks reifying the norms that harm them (2014 *AJPH* commentary); APA 2018 guidelines favour helping men "create their own concepts of what it means to be male." Also, men can explicitly reject outdated views and still respond to threat ("they order the steak," Sarah DiMuccio), and a 2022 NYU-team study found masculinity-driven political effects especially strong among liberal men. (Ch 9)

> Bonus recall (Ch 8): Pareidolia check: Caulfield saw a rocket on a Mayan sarcophagus lid that depicts a king's descent into the underworld, and was pulled back by the face on Mars after rejecting the theory. Calibration: "be open to the wonder, but not closed to the facts." Counterfactual test: would I accept this method if it cut against a goal I like? Placebo relief is real relief but reporting it as pharmacological damages the evidence base.

## Section 6: A Good Story and the Certainty Grift (Ch 10)

**Q1 (SA).** What is the certainty grift?

**A1.** Most people genuinely want to do good but rarely convert that into behaviour; the goodness illusion bridges the gap with deceptive halos, wishful thinking and scary anecdotes that supply false certainty about both the goal *and* the path, sold for profit, politics and evidence-free agendas. (Ch 10)

**Q2 (SA, numbers).** What does the 2016 *Mayo Clinic Proceedings* study say, and why does it matter?

**A2.** Only **2.7% of Americans** live a healthy lifestyle: the intention-behaviour gap. It is the "market map": wherever people badly want an outcome they cannot reach, someone sells certainty into the gap. (Ch 10)

**Q3 (MC).** Jonathan Haidt's line used in the chapter is that the human mind is:
(a) a logic processor; (b) a story processor, not a logic processor; (c) a pattern matcher only; (d) a social calculator.

**A3.** (b). Supporting: fMRI work shows stories activate language, sound, movement, social cognition and future-prediction regions; a 2020 University of Florida analysis of the anti-vaccine film *Vaccines Revealed* describes **transportation** into a story and para-social relationships that persist after viewing. (Ch 10)

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** Explain the anecdote/data distinction using Dahlstrom's and Jarry's points.

**A4.** Dahlstrom: science seeks broad patterns; storytelling celebrates the particulars of one experience regardless of representativeness. Jarry: "the salience of an anecdote can turn a molehill into a mountain" and post hoc reasoning "can act like gasoline on fire embers." Ask what the population data say and how much would survive if the anecdote were unrepresentative. But narrative is not the enemy: it can raise recall, comprehension and acceptance (one grieving family lands harder than seven million abstract COVID deaths). (Ch 10)

**Q5 (SA).** What happened to health science students who watched *What the Health*?

**A5.** A 2020 Sam Houston State study showed the 2017 Netflix film (e.g., an egg a day as bad as five cigarettes) to 160 health science students; afterwards nutrition knowledge *decreased*, agreement with incorrect claims *increased*, and many said they'd change their diets. Subject-matter training does not protect you; form over substance. (Ch 10)

**Q6 (EXPLAIN).** Run the documentary evidence audit on *Toxic Beauty* (2019) and talcum powder.

**A6.** The film follows people who used products including talc and developed ovarian cancer; successful lawsuits worth billions reinforce a sense of proven risk. But settlements are legal outcomes, not scientific findings (courts are poor with science). A large 2020 *JAMA* study found no statistically significant association between genital-area powder use and ovarian cancer; a 2021 *Gynecologic Oncology* review found at most a weak association. Caulfield does not claim no connection; the point is an uncertain literature rendered as definitive. Audit: shocking anecdotes yes; broader body of evidence no. Verdict: emotionally true, evidentially unsupported. (Ch 10)

**Q7 (SA).** What is "backlash" and what does the 2022 Rutgers study report?

**A7.** The "ugh, it's all so confusing, I give up" effect. Participants exposed to conflicting health information made more errors, were slower, reported greater workload and nutritional confusion, and rejected emerging recommendations outright. The grift both causes and profits from this. (Ch 10)

**Q8 (T/F).** Documentaries cannot change behaviour for good.

**A8.** False. A 2016 study found an environmental documentary significantly shifted perceptions and donations; University of Antwerp (2021) found *Blue Planet* produced lasting increases in journalists' and politicians' mentions of ocean issues and related searches. Narrative is a delivery mechanism, not a truth signal. (Ch 10)

**Q9 (SA).** What does the 2020 Ohio State study say about kindness, and why does it matter for the grift?

**A9.** Motives for kindness are robust and varied (paying it forward, being seen as good, reciprocating); believing you are kind fosters happiness and some longitudinal work links kindness to longevity. Implication: the audience (and opponents) are usually genuinely motivated to do good; the grift exploits that and does not require cynicism from the audience. (Ch 10)

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A school board member shows a moving documentary to staff about a food additive and wants to ban it from lunches. Which concepts apply and what questions do you ask?

**A10.** **Documentary evidence audit** (anecdote-driven? contextualized against the body of evidence?), **anecdote vs data** (population-level evidence), **transportation** and **form over substance** (production adds persuasive force independent of merit), **backlash** risk, and **certainty grift** (naming the good intention exploited). Treat your emotional conviction as data about the production, not about the world. Do not abandon storytelling; use it to make real data relatable and represent uncertainty honestly. (Ch 10)

**Q10b (SCENARIO).** A lawyer says: "Billions in settlements prove the product causes cancer." How do you reply?

**A10b.** Settlements are narrative/legal outcomes, not scientific validation ("billions awarded over talc mean next to nothing scientifically"). Check the evidence directly: for talc, the 2020 JAMA and 2021 review above show no significant/weak association, with the honest caveat that better research would help and any risk is likely small in absolute terms. (Ch 7, Ch 10)

## Section 7: The Opinion Economy and Reviews Are Often Wrong (Ch 11-12)

**Q1 (SA).** Define the opinion economy and give the three questions to ask before trusting an aggregate rating.

**A1.** The vast, lightly regulated industry monetizing our belief in strangers' authentic opinions, destroying the authenticity that made them valuable. Ask: (1) Who profits if this rating moves up half a star? (2) Is it plausibly manipulated, incentivized or herded? (3) Would an independent comparative expert agree? (Ch 11)

**Q2 (SA).** List the five reasons we over-trust reviews.

**A2.** (1) "A person like me" trust; (2) the persuasive force of anecdote/testimonial; (3) perceived authenticity ("feels real"); (4) wisdom-of-the-crowd consensus; (5) desire for quick, easy, certain answers. Discount hardest when (1) or (2) is working on you: they are the most exploitable. (Ch 11)

**Q3 (EXPLAIN).** Why does the wisdom of the crowd fail for online reviews?

**A3.** It works only if judgments are independent (the ox-weight test). Reviewers see each other: **anchoring** (an inflated average pulls ratings up) and **social influence bias** (prior positive reviews raised the likelihood of a positive rating by 32% and final ratings by 25% via positive herding). Reviewers are an extreme-value sample (an MIT Sloan study: only 1.5% of an apparel company's customers wrote reviews). **Choice supportive bias** applies to verified buyers. (Ch 11)

**Q4 (SA, numbers).** Give the Chapter 11 money and scale numbers.

**A4.** One extra Yelp star moves restaurant revenue 5-9%; fake reviews influence roughly $791 billion in annual US online spending, directly affecting about $152 billion (WEF estimate); ~25-30% of reviews may be unreliable; incentivized reviews inflate ratings 40-70% (FTC disclosure required); ~70% of shoppers filter by rating, usually at four stars; 88% trust reviews more than experts or friends (the notes' anti-pattern figure), despite reviewers being non-random, non-independent and often paid. (Ch 11)

**Q5 (EXPLAIN).** Tell the story of The Shed at Dulwich and its lesson.

**A5.** Oobah Butler bought a $13 burner phone to get TripAdvisor-verified, built an appointment-only website with a menu of moods, photographed food styled from shaving cream and toilet-bowl bleach cakes, and he and friends posted florid fake reviews ("earthy taste," plus a small complaint about a stained blanket). From rank 18,149 of 18,149 London restaurants it reached 156 within months and eventually number one, with 89,000 page views in a day; he refused booking requests, which amplified exclusivity. No food was ever served. Lesson: rank came from narrative and scarcity signals with zero underlying product. (Ch 11)

**Q6 (SA).** What is the Goldilocks zone and the fake review cycle?

**A6.** Goldilocks: 4.2-4.5 stars with minor complaints; all-five-star profiles depress sales and sophisticated fakers add small gripes, so it is a manipulation target, not a credibility signal. Cycle (Curtis Boyd): business buys fakes, sells more, spends the gain on ads with the hosting platform, so platform, advertiser, business and faker profit and only the customer loses; "we removed millions of fakes" shows scale, not cleanliness. (Ch 11-12)

**Q7 (SA).** Describe the 2016 accuracy study (Amazon vs Consumer Reports vs resale).

**A7.** Researchers compared Amazon star ratings with Consumer Reports scores for over 1,200 products. Higher Amazon rating predicted a higher Consumer Reports score only 57% of the time. Against used-product resale prices, Consumer Reports scores predicted value while star ratings had essentially no relationship. Donald Lichtenstein's mechanisms: consumers rate one product in isolation (not comparatively) and review right after purchase, before living with it. (Ch 12)

**Q8 (EXPLAIN).** State the knowledge asymmetry rule and the physician numbers.

**A8.** The more expertise separates you from the provider, the less the review tracks quality. Patients can judge experience (waits, courtesy, communication) but not clinical quality. Physicians are 64% more likely than other businesses to get five stars and 194% as likely to get one star; 84% of Americans surveyed would not see a physician under four stars, yet studies show no clear relationship between ratings and clinical outcomes or peer-assessed performance. A doctor who withholds an unwelcome truth may score higher. (Ch 12)

**Q9 (SA).** What did Marco Zenone's cancer-clinic analysis find, and what does it show about stakes?

**A9.** 1,000+ reviews across 39 alternative-medicine cancer clinics averaged 4.4 stars, dominated by stage-four/terminal patients, for treatments without supporting evidence (patients using alternative therapies for cancer have worse outcomes). The higher the stakes and the more desperate the search, the stronger the incentive to manipulate, so trust ratings least where you want them most. (Ch 12)

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A buyer is choosing a laptop and a family doctor, and has read the star ratings for both. For each, which concepts apply and what should they do? Also: a restaurant owner finds 50 one-star reviews appearing overnight.

**A10.** Laptop (objective, testable): **accuracy test** and the 57% finding; use comparative expert testing (Consumer Reports, Wirecutter, video reviews), more than one source, ignore stars. Doctor: **knowledge asymmetry rule**; use reviews at most for experience signals with caution. Overnight one-stars: **fake negative reviews / one-starring** (about 50 fake negatives can be enough to overtake a competing hotel in visibility); ideology can drive them and kill a small business. Also remember the **assume-you-can't-detect rule**: three in four Americans overestimate their ability to sort credible from bunk and the most overconfident spread the most falsehoods; machine-generated fakes defeated tech-savvy users at chance. (Ch 12)

**Q10b (SCENARIO).** A manager says, "We have thousands of reviews, so the bias averages out, and I can usually spot the fake ones." Respond.

**A10b.** Volume does not fix it (garbage in, garbage out scales; 25-30% may be unreliable). Illusory superiority and Dunning-Kruger apply to lie detection; confidence is the bias, not a defence. Awareness doesn't immunize: 52% of Australians suspect they've fallen for fakes while 86% of German consumers still call reviews credible. Context effects also move ratings (rainy-day reviews lower; NYC hotel ratings: couples 8.19 highest, business travellers 7.61 lowest). (Ch 11-12)

> Bonus recall (Ch 11): Marketing placebo effect (expensive wine tastes better; discounted energy drink works less well); precarious manhood principle ("Real Men Don't Buy 'Mrs. Clean'"); super contributors; illusory consensus.

## Section 8: The Escape Plan and the Conclusion (Ch 13-14)

**Q1 (SA).** List the steps of the review escape plan.

**A1.** Be very skeptical of online reviews (Proserpio); seek side-by-side comparisons in standardised environments (de Langhe); use independent expert outlets (Consumer Reports, Wirecutter, video reviews; Khalifah); use multiple comparative sources since experts can be incentivized; ignore star ratings and aggregates; audit your own motivated reading (wanting the product weights positives); for subjective goods find critics whose taste matches yours; talk to humans face to face; stay open and try things. (Ch 13, patterns)

**Q2 (SA).** Give the four questions for any poll (with Frank Graves) and his standard.

**A2.** (1) How big? ~1,000 respondents is reasonable nationally. (2) Representative? Random beats self-selecting internet polls. (3) Who did it? Survey experts or an influencer? (4) Obvious biases, e.g., sponsor with a stake? Plus check for leading question wording. Standard: sound survey research is "a mirror measuring public opinion, not a force shaping it." "Faux research" is Graves' term for flawed or fabricated polling. (Ch 13)

**Q3 (EXPLAIN).** Explain the spiral of silence and why likes matter.

**A3.** People withhold opinions they believe are in the minority, so visible consensus signals silence dissent. A 2019 study found likes directly changed personal opinion because they read as societal consensus. So visible consensus (star averages, likes, poll toplines) is an active force, and "illusory public opinion" can produce real consensus. (Ch 13)

**Q4 (SA, numbers).** Give the FiveThirtyEight IMDb numbers.

**A4.** Of 4,000+ films only 97 (2.4%) had more ratings from women than men; for 90% male ratings outnumbered female by at least 2:1, and for 51% by more than 5:1. Recomputed with women's ratings only, *The Godfather* fell from 2nd to 10th, *Godfather II* from 3rd to 22nd, *Empire Strikes Back* from 13th to 41st. Also: 64% of reviews of the 100 highest-grossing films were by white men; 89% of "top critic" reviews were by white critics. Lesson: an aggregate is a population. (Ch 13)

**Q5 (SA).** Summarise Caulfield's taste-widening experiment and the music findings.

**A5.** A lifelong punk-rock snob spent a year listening to yacht rock, Fleetwood Mac, AC/DC and '70s disco with the iconography bracketed off, expanding his "this is great" list by 30-40% (some dislikes never budged). Rough split: ~70% iconography, 25% quality, 5% random exposure. Stated vs measured taste: 60% of liberals in one study said they liked R&B but tested as liking it less than conservatives. Higher education and early musical exposure correlate with liking fewer artists; exposure to a critic's review influenced liking more than listening did. Resolution: "if you are a fan, you are a fan." (Ch 13)

**Q6 (SA).** What are incidental similarities and what experiment backs bridge-building?

**A6.** Non-political common ground (shared interests, tastes) increases felt closeness and thus openness to opposing political views. Forced exposure to other perspectives moved even hardcore partisans in the Berkeley/Yale Fox-to-CNN study. Slow and individual-scale, but it works where argument does not. (Ch 13)

**Q7 (SA).** Name the Certainty Six in order.

**A7.** (1) Pause: recognize the ecosystem isn't rigged for accuracy; ask "is this accurate?" before sharing. (2) Watch for scienceploitation. (3) Resist science hype. (4) Take a closer look when goodness is a marketing strategy. (5) Always consider the body of evidence. (6) Like what you like. Deliberately low-effort: a filter for everyday claims, not an audit. (Ch 14)

**Q8 (EXPLAIN).** Explain the body-of-evidence test and why we underestimate consensus.

**A8.** (1) Don't fall for single study syndrome; (2) check your personal biases about the topic first; (3) for extraordinary claims check retraction and venue (search PubMed/Google Scholar for a breadth of citations); (4) find the scientific consensus through responsible aggregators (science communicators, journalists, university and government resources, credible professional bodies, mainstream media). Consensus "often isn't sexy" (Jarry), so contrarians are overrepresented and the public underestimates consensus. Caulfield admits his own bias for exercise, sleep and social-connection studies. (Ch 14)

**Q9 (SA).** List the "Fundamental Fix" reforms and the supporting numbers in Chapter 14.

**A9.** Shift from quantity to quality: shut down fake journals; retract faster and visibly; fund replication and negative results; open data; enforce integrity including treating predatory/vanity publishing as a problem; make publicly funded researchers correct the record when work is spun. Numbers: 20% of researchers admit sacrificing quality for quantity (2023); a 2023 *Nature* analysis of 45M papers and 3.9M patents found disruptive research has declined sharply since 1945 (>90% by one measure); José Manuel Lorenzo Rodriguez reportedly published 176 papers in 2022, one every two days. Accuracy nudge: Pennycook and Rand showed it improves shared-news quality. (Ch 14)

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** Your department head wants to evaluate researchers by paper count; a junior colleague has found a dramatic study supporting a favoured hypothesis; and a friend wants to share a political post that made them furious. Which Chapter 14 and earlier concepts apply to each?

**A10.** Paper count: **publish or perish / salami science** and the Fundamental Fix (reward quality; early-career staff under pressure invite questionable practices; Spain example). Dramatic study: **single study syndrome** and the body-of-evidence test, plus name your own bias; ask if it is in a marginal/predatory venue. Friend: **accuracy pause** plus **Starbird's emotional-signal check**. (Ch 9, Ch 14)

**Q10b (SCENARIO).** Caulfield removed some studies from his own book. What happened and what is the point?

**A10b.** While writing, he checked venues of studies he planned to cite and found some skewed toward predatory, so he cut them ("Punt."). He names the paradox: his own work sits inside the same distorting incentives he critiques (he cites hundreds of studies, applies for grants, talks to media), and the literature on polluted literature may itself be polluted. The answer is to run the body-of-evidence test on your own sources (venue, retraction, citations, consensus), not to abandon citation. (Ch 14)
