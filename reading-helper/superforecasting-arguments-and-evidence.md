## Chapter 1: An Optimistic Skeptic

**Central argument.** Forecasting is a measurable, learnable skill, neither a gift nor a fraud. The "dart-throwing chimp" finding concerned average experts on multi-year questions; on shorter horizons some people have real, testable foresight, and what makes them good is what they do rather than who they are. The sensible stance is optimistic skepticism: reality is partly predictable and partly not, and the only way to tell which part is which is to measure.

**Full stories.**

*Bill Flack.* Bill Flack is a retired USDA employee in Kearney, Nebraska, who dropped out of a math PhD after "having my nose rubbed in my limitations." He answered about 300 forecasting questions in the Good Judgment Project, such as "Will Russia annex additional Ukrainian territory in the next three months?" and "Will NATO invite new countries to the Membership Action Plan in the next nine months?" He often had no idea what a question meant at first sight, so he did his homework, weighed clashing arguments, and committed to a number. Every prediction was dated, recorded and scored by independent observers, and his record is excellent. The notes then contrast him with Tom Friedman, who advises presidents and speaks at Davos, whose forecasting accuracy has never been tested (so opinions about it are opinions about opinions). Bill Flack has never been asked to Davos. The difference is not intelligence; it is that Bill's forecasts were scored. The premise of the book follows: relying on Bill is reasonable, and any intelligent, open-minded, hard-working person can learn what he does.

*Lorenz and the Arab Spring (skeptic half).* The notes use Lorenz's butterfly and the Arab Spring, sparked by one street vendor, as illustrations of nonlinear systems that impose hard limits on prediction.

*Weather forecasting (optimist half).* Weather is reliable at 1-2 days and chimp-level at about 8, which shows predictability is a spectrum depending on what, how far out and under what circumstances. Meteorologists have run the forecast-measure-revise loop and improved.

*The forecasting-goals jumble.* The notes list entertainment (Cramer's "booyah"), galvanizing action (activist warnings), impressing (bank pundits on 2050), and comfort (partisan warm baths). Examples of non-accuracy goals explain why measurement rarely occurs.

*Poker (Aaron Brown).* The difference between 60/40 and 40/60 separates poker heavyweights from amateurs, "the difference between a consistent winner and the guy going broke."

*Eyesight analogy.* A 60% improvement is like moving from 20/100 to 20/40: not hawk-eyed, but life-changing.

**Evidence.**
- **Expert Political Judgment (EPJ), 1984-2004:** the average expert was roughly random ("dart-throwing chimp") on many questions and worst on long horizons. Caveat: this is the average expert, not all, and not short horizons.
- **Good Judgment Project (GJP):** 20,000+ volunteers; about 500 IARPA questions over four years; more than 1 million judgments; mostly 1-12 months out. GJP beat the control group by 60% (year 1) and 78% (year 2), beat university rivals by 30-70%, and beat intelligence analysts with classified access. About 2% of forecasters were "super."
- **Training:** a one-hour tutorial on basic concepts (the Ten Commandments) improved accuracy about 10% across a tournament year.
- **Predictor analysis:** commitment to self-improvement was the strongest predictor of GJP performance.
- **Meehl's rule:** when a validated statistical algorithm exists, use it; but such algorithms rarely exist for the question at hand. Expect "freestyle chess" blends of human and machine.

**Key terms.** Dart-throwing chimp; Goldilocks zone; clocks vs. clouds (Laplace's demon, Lorenz's cloud); forecast-measure-revise; optimistic skeptic; GJP; IARPA; superforecaster.

**So what.** Before accepting a forecast ask three scoping questions: what exactly, how far out, under what conditions. Ask for scored accuracy; treat unscored pundits' records as unknown. When someone says either "experts are useless" or "we're 90% right," note that both are unmeasured assertions. Use validated algorithms when they exist and train humans where they don't.

## Chapter 2: Illusions of Knowledge

**Central argument.** Medicine stagnated for millennia because physicians trusted the view from the tip of their nose; progress began only when they accepted doubt and ran controlled trials. Forecasting today resembles nineteenth-century medicine, and the cure is the same: a tablespoon of doubt.

**Full stories.**

*Archie Cochrane's lump.* Cochrane, a founder of evidence-based medicine and a man who berated German guards as a POW physician, found a lump in his armpit in 1956. An eminent specialist removed it, and before the pathologist reported, cut out Cochrane's pectoralis minor and told him he was dying. Cochrane sobbed, planned his remaining time and made peace with it. The pathology report showed no cancer. He later wrote that he "never doubted the surgeon's words." His skeptic's defenses folded because the story fit (the hand carcinoma, prior X-ray exposure, the lump) and because he substituted the easy question ("is this the sort of man who would know?") for the hard one ("do I have cancer?"). The lesson is not that Cochrane was foolish but that no one is immune; the only defense is a habit: pause, ask what else could explain this, wait for the pathologist.

*The Cochrane trick.* He told cardiologists that preliminary results favored the cardiac units; they demanded he stop the "unethical" trial. He then revealed that he had reversed the numbers and home care was ahead. Their reaction exposed their certainty (the "God complex": physicians who "just knew").

*Galen.* The treatment cures all except the incurable, who die: the model of unfalsifiable confirmation.

*Oslo bombing, 2011.* It was reasonably suspected to be Islamist; the error was treating a plausible hypothesis as settled.

*Thatcher's "short, sharp shock."* An uncontrolled policy rollout yields only the illusion of insight.

*Peggy Noonan on Romney, 2012.* "The vibrations are right": an example of forecasting by feel.

*Split-brain patients and journalists.* Split-brain patients confabulate reasons for actions they cannot explain; journalists explain market moves with news that came out after the move.

*Fireground commanders, chess and Carlsen.* Intuition works where valid cues and practice exist; even Magnus Carlsen "double-checks." Stock-picking lacks cues.

**Evidence.**
- **Randomized controlled trials** (Bradford Hill, 1921 template): random assignment balances unknown differences so outcomes can be attributed to the treatment. This is the engine of medical progress in the notes' account.
- **Bat-and-ball "ten cents" example:** System 1 answers, System 2 may or may not engage.
- **Kahneman-Klein reconciliation:** intuition is valid where the environment offers valid cues and enough practice to learn them (fireground, chess) and fails where cues are absent (stock picking). Caveat: even in good settings, double-check.
- Not a numerical chapter; its evidence is historical and experiential.

**Key terms.** Tip-of-your-nose perspective; System 1 / System 2; WYSIATI; bait and switch (attribute substitution); confirmation bias / motivated skepticism; availability heuristic; confabulation; cargo cult science (Feynman); God complex; randomized controlled trial.

**So what.** You cannot switch System 1 off, so build checks: ask "what would convince me I'm wrong?"; ask "has this been tested or does it just feel right?"; confidence signals a coherent story, not a true one; trust intuition only in cue-rich, feedback-rich settings and still double-check; beware explaining outcomes after the fact with whatever is at hand; watch for bait and switch.

## Chapter 3: Keeping Score

**Central argument.** You cannot improve what you cannot score, and most forecasts cannot be scored because they lack timelines, defined terms and numbers. Fix those, collect many forecasts, and score them with Brier. You then discover that foxes beat hedgehogs and that aggregation (the "dragonfly eye") is the mechanism.

**Full stories.**

*Sherman Kent and the language of probability.* Sherman Kent of the CIA proposed a numeric scale for estimative words. The problem he fought was concrete: "serious possibility" in National Intelligence Estimate 29-51 meant 80/20 to one analyst and 20/80 to another. Kent: "I'd rather be a bookie than a goddamn poet." In the Bay of Pigs memo, "fair chance" was meant as 3-to-1 against, but Kennedy heard optimism.

*Ballmer's iPhone forecast.* In full context ("2% or 3%" of 1.3 billion global mobile phones; "they may make a lot of money") the forecast was ambiguous, not laughably wrong; the iPhone was about 6% of global mobile sales in Q3 2013. The lesson: hall-of-shame quoting out of context is unfair, and ambiguity blocks scoring.

*The Bernanke letter (2010).* A letter warned that quantitative easing "risk[s] currency debasement and inflation": "risk" plus no time frame equals unfalsifiable. Rewritten scorably: "If the Fed continues large-scale asset purchases through 2011, the probability that US CPI inflation exceeds 4% year-over-year in any month before 31 December 2013 is 65%; the probability that the trade-weighted dollar index falls more than 15% from its November 2010 level by the same date is 40%." Now there is a time frame, a defined measure and numbers. When 2013 arrives it can be scored, and "note the word risk, note the absence of a date" (Ferguson, 2014) is no longer an escape. The exercise shows how much thinking the original avoided: choosing 4% vs. 3%, CPI vs. "Wall Street inflation," 2013 vs. "someday" is where the work lives.

*Galton's ox.* The crowd's average guess was 1,197 lb against an actual 1,198 lb. It works because valid information points one way and errors cancel.

*Guess two-thirds of the average (FT/Thaler, 1997).* Pure logic says 0; the winning answer was 13 because contestants are humans, not Vulcans. The Spock, McCoy and Captain Kirk synthesis: model other actors as a mix and ask how many Kirks are in the room.

*Annie Duke's raise.* Students assume an opponent's raise means strength, yet admit they would never raise with a strong hand; they never took the opponent's perspective.

*Leonhardt and Obamacare.* A prediction market's 75% outcome did not occur and the market was called "wrong": the wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy.

*Springfield and Phoenix.* A Brier of 0.2 is excellent in Springfield and poor in Phoenix ("always sunny" scores near 0).

**Evidence.**
- **Kent's scale:** certain 100%; almost certain 93% ±6; probable 75% ±12; chances about even 50% ±10; probably not 30% ±10; almost certainly not 7% ±5; impossible 0%.
- **Brier score** (Glenn Brier, 1950): 0 perfect; 0.5 random or hedged 50-50; 2.0 maximally wrong with 100% confidence every time. It punishes hubris like a bet at 19-to-1 odds. Caveat: difficulty varies, so compare against "no change" and "recent rate of change" rules and on the same questions over the same periods.
- **EPJ:** 284 experts, about 28,000 predictions. Hedgehogs (one Big Idea) were worse than foxes on calibration and resolution, even in their own specialty, and worse than chance on long horizons. Fame correlated inversely with accuracy. What predicts accuracy is how people think, not what they believe.
- **Galton's ox** as aggregation evidence.

**Key terms.** Scorable forecast; Kent's scale; wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy; calibration; resolution; Brier score; benchmark; fox vs. hedgehog; green-tinted glasses; dragonfly eye; wisdom of crowds; metacognition.

**So what.** Before accepting a forecast run the four-question scorability check (time frame? defined terms? number? enough instances?). Read every probability as two numbers: 70% yes and 30% no. When "furthermore, moreover" wells up, look for the "on the other hand." Aggregate perspectives, from others and from yourself. Do not reward hedgehogs just because simple confident stories calm nerves. Translating "serious possibility" into a number forces you to think about your thinking.

## Chapter 4: Superforecasters

**Central argument.** The Iraq WMD "slam dunk" was a reasonable judgment expressed with unreasonable certainty. The shock it produced led IARPA to run a forecasting tournament, which found that ordinary volunteers with the right habits beat professionals, and that their edge was skill, not luck.

**Full stories.**

*The Iraq WMD estimate (NIE 2002-16HC).* The national intelligence estimate asserted "Iraq has... Baghdad has...": statements admitting no surprise. There were no red teams, no devil's advocates, and no paper proposing the view now believed true. The conclusion was reasonable given what was knowable and wrong; the sin was certainty ("slam dunk"). A 60-70% estimate instead of "Baghdad has..." might have stopped the invasion. The notes warn that replacing "was it a good decision?" with "was it a good outcome?" is a popular, pernicious bait and switch (the poker novice's blunder).

*Doug Lorch.* A retired IBM programmer. In year 1 his Brier was 0.22, fifth among 2,800. In year 2 it was 0.14, the best; he beat a prediction market by 40% and the control crowd by 60%, alone exceeding IARPA's fourth-year target.

*Langer's rigged coins.* Yale students shown early lucky streaks on rigged coin flips believed they had skill (the illusion of prediction).

*The regression test as a worked example.* Imagine 2,800 people each calling 104 coin flips: a bell curve, with a few "stars" at 70%, pure luck, collapsing to 50% next round. Define Frank (bottom 1%) and Nancy (top 1%) in year 1. If all luck, both regress to 50% in year 2; if half skill, Frank rises to about 25% and Nancy falls to about 75%; if all skill, no regression. Observed: superforecasters as a group *increased* their lead in years 2 and 3, which is suspicious since questions like the East China Sea stabbing were pure chance. Resolution: being named "super" and placed on elite teams raised performance enough to mask the regression luck would produce. Individually, 0.65 year-to-year correlation and 30% annual turnover are exactly what skill-plus-luck predicts. Conclusion: mostly skill, no one infallible.

*"She called the crash of 2008."* Without knowing how many people were calling crashes, this tells you nothing (survivorship reasoning). Skill and luck are not either/or (Mauboussin's *Success Equation*).

**Evidence.**
- **IARPA tournament:** five teams, daily forecasts at 9 a.m. from Sept 2011 to June 2015; about 500 Goldilocks-zone questions; teams had to beat the control crowd by 20% in year 1 rising to 50% by year 4; internal randomized experiments on training, teaming and markets.
- **Aggregation recipe:** identify the top ~2% by accuracy and update frequency, weight them, and extremize (push 70% toward 85%, 30% toward 15%) to simulate full information sharing.
- **First cohort:** 58 superforecasters with collective Brier 0.25 vs. 0.37 for everyone else; by year 4 they beat regulars by more than 60%; at 300 days out they were as accurate as regulars at 100 days. About 30% better than intelligence analysts with classified access (Ignatius, *Washington Post*, 2013).
- **Skill vs. luck:** father-son height correlation about 0.5; super year-to-year correlation about 0.65; about 30% drop out of the top 2% each year and 70% remain; the chance of that consistency among coin-flippers is under 1 in 100 million.
- **Caveat:** accountability for process (checklists) is not accountability for accuracy; the notes liken it to hand-washing without checking whether treatment works.

**Key terms.** Reasonable vs. correct; extremizing; regression to the mean; illusion of prediction; survivorship reasoning; superforecaster; red team; devil's advocate.

**So what.** After any outcome ask "reasonable at the time?" before "right?" Treat unanimity as a warning. When someone cites a dramatic hit, ask "how many were throwing darts?" Expect stars to have bad years. Use regression to separate skill from luck before believing any track record. Simple aggregation plus extremizing can beat a multi-billion-dollar analytic apparatus.

## Chapter 5: Supersmart?

**Central argument.** Superforecasters are smart and knowledgeable but not off the charts (about top 20%, not top 1%). What separates them is method: Fermi-ize the question, start from the outside view, then investigate the inside view, then synthesize many perspectives; and a disposition: beliefs are hypotheses to be tested, not treasures to be guarded.

**Full stories.**

*David Rogg's Europe-attack forecast.* Early in 2015, just after *Charlie Hebdo*, the question asked whether Islamist militants would attack in one of eight Western European countries between 21 January and 31 March 2015 (69 days). Outside view: Rogg counted attacks in those countries over the previous five years on a Wikipedia list: six, a base rate of 1.2 per year. Inside view, step 1: ISIS's rise and European recruitment made pre-2010 data stale, so he dropped it, raising the base rate to 1.5 per year, "which I suspect is still low." Step 2: post-*Hebdo* security surges cut the other way. Net adjustment: raise by about one-fifth to 1.8 per year. Scaling to the window: 69/365 × 1.8 ≈ 0.34, so **34%**. He then posted the entire chain on the team forum to solicit disagreement. The tip-of-the-nose alternatives ("Europe is awash with ISIS talk, 80%!" or "security is tight, 10%!") are neither anchored, nor scaled to 69 days, nor checkable.

*McNamara's "best and brightest."* They escalated Vietnam without analyzing their assumptions: intelligence alone is not method.

*The exhumed famous dead man.* When no base rate exists, Fermi-ize the outside view: the probability must be well above 0 and well below 100, say 20-80%, midpoint 50%.

*Pet ownership.* Refining the reference class: all US households own pets 62%; single-family detached homes about 73%.

*Peggy Noonan on Bush's approval.* She told a story for a rebounding approval rating while the base rate (all ex-presidents' approval rises) was available.

*The Arafat question.* "Did Israel poison Arafat?" is not the same as "Will Swiss or French labs find elevated polonium?": a bait and switch.

*Doug Lorch's reading program.* Lorch built a program that selects his reading for ideological and geographic diversity: AOM operationalized.

*Ghana's election.* A super described "Who will win Ghana's election?" as an opportunity to learn about Ghana: openness to experience.

*Summers.* The planning-fallacy correction: double the estimate and move up one time unit (an hour becomes two days).

**Evidence.**
- **Intelligence threshold:** regular forecasters scored above about 70% of the population; supers above about 80%. The big jump is public to forecaster, not forecaster to super.
- **Personality measures:** supers score high on need for cognition, openness to experience, and the Cognitive Reflection Test (they "think thrice, not twice").
- **Crowd within:** assume your first answer is wrong and produce a second; nearly as good as a second person.
- **Super forum posts** routinely juggle three or more competing considerations ("on the third hand") before netting to a number ("feels no-ish, 80%").

**Key terms.** Fermi-izing; outside view / base rate / reference class; inside view; anchoring; active open-mindedness (Baron); crowd within; dragonfly eye; need for cognition; bait and switch; planning fallacy; thesis-antithesis-synthesis.

**So what.** Run the standard sequence on every question: Fermi-ize, outside view, inside view, synthesize, seek other views, state a precise number. Base rate is the anchor; the story is the adjustment. Write reasoning down and read it as a stranger. Flip the question's wording. Avoid six months of aimless reading and avoid answering the emotionally salient question. Do not treat a brilliant puzzle-solver as a good forecaster without checking appetite for self-criticism.

## Chapter 6: Superquants?

**Central argument.** Superforecasters are numerate but rarely use math. What numeracy gives them is probabilistic thinking: a mental dial with many settings of "maybe" instead of the primal yes/no/maybe, plus the granularity to use it (single percentage points), which measurably predicts accuracy.

**Full stories.**

*The bin Laden meeting (as dramatized and as a probabilistic exercise).* In *Zero Dark Thirty* the character Maya says "100%" and is right, but unreasonable. The fictional Panetta wanted unanimity; the real Panetta wanted honest, diverse estimates (30% to 90%+) and treated them as gift-wrapped wisdom of the crowd. The notes rerun the meeting: estimates of 95% (team lead), 95% (second officer), about 80% (most DNI reviewers), 60%, and some at 40% or 30%. Step 1: do not demand consensus; disagreement means people are thinking independently. Step 2: aggregate; the median is roughly 70%, and a weighted average favoring analysts with the best track records might land higher. Step 3: extremize modestly if advisers hold different scraps of information (OPSEC patterns, courier tracking, the compound's design), nudging toward 80-85%. Step 4: apply the decision threshold; if the president would act above, say, 30%, the exact number matters less than being well above it, but that is a decision rule, not a forecast. What should not happen: declaring "fifty-fifty" and discarding the room's information. In hindsight bin Laden was there, Maya's 100% was still unreasonable, and the ~70-85% synthesis was the honest estimate.

*Obama's "coin flip."* When the median estimate was about 70%, saying "this is a coin flip" may have been executive triage, or a retreat to the ignorance prior that discarded information. (Commandment 6 notes Obama applies granularity to March Madness.)

*Maya vs. the IC.* Right but unreasonable vs. wrong but reasonable: outcomes don't fix or break reasoning.

*Rubin.* "Absolutely" is unacceptable; say 60% and argue whether it's 59. He had to pound the table so an 80% forecast wasn't heard as "it will happen."

*Bill Flack's Monte Carlo on exchange rates.* The rare case in which a super builds an explicit model.

*Tversky's joke / the lion.* The three-setting dial is evolutionarily sensible (run, stay alert, relax) but fatally flawed for forecasting.

**Evidence.**
- **Fifty-fifty tell:** people who say 50% usually mean "I don't know"; frequent users of 50% are measurably less accurate.
- **Granularity (Mellers):** forecasters using tens < fives < ones in accuracy. Rounding supers' forecasts to the nearest 0.05 hurt their scores; rounding regulars to 0.2 barely mattered, so the precision is real.
- **Certainty premium:** people pay 2-3x more to go from 5% to 0% risk than from 10% to 5%.
- **Confidence-competence conflation:** people prefer confident advisers even with identical records; middling probabilities read as laziness.
- **Fate and Brier:** "everything happens for a reason" thinking correlates with well-being but negatively with Brier scores.
- **NIC scale:** a 5-7 point scale (remote to almost certainly) is better than three settings but coarser than supers.
- **Uncertainty types:** on cloudlike (aleatory) questions supers stay within 35-65%.
- **Munger:** without elementary probability you go through life "like a one-legged man in an ass-kicking contest."

**Key terms.** Three-setting dial; probabilistic thinking; granularity; epistemic vs. aleatory uncertainty; ignorance prior; certainty premium; calibration; "how, not why."

**So what.** Replace yes/no with "how many degrees of maybe can I honestly distinguish here?" When you hear 50% ask "literal or 'I don't know'?" Separate "is the probability high enough to act?" from "what is the probability?" Ask how it could happen, not why it must. Avoid granularity theater (e.g. "73% Apple finishes up 24%") without a scored track record, and avoid "nothing is 100%" followed by "slam dunk."

## Chapter 7: Supernewsjunkies?

**Central argument.** Superforecasters win partly by updating, but their initial forecasts are already about 50% more accurate, and good updating is as hard as forecasting. Steer between underreaction (Scylla) and overreaction (Charybdis) with many small updates, while keeping the judgment to jump hard when the old basis has collapsed.

**Full stories.**

*The Swiss lab and Flack (Arafat).* Everyone updates on Obama announcing strikes in Syria; the edge is catching subtly diagnostic signals earlier. On the Arafat-polonium question, the Swiss lab's unexplained delay, then reports that they found polonium and were ruling out lead decay, moved Flack from 60% to 65%.

*General DeWitt (1942).* "The fact that no sabotage has occurred is a disturbing and confirming indication that it will": belief perseverance in its purest form.

*Robert and David.* Irrelevant details (Robert plays tennis; David skis) sway judgments that should ignore them (the dilution effect).

*Doug Lorch's collapse.* Lorch had a 95% forecast resting on a month-old report that was clearly failing. The right move was not gradual retreat but a fresh forecast: 95% to 55% to 15%, then resume small steps (Orwell's sixth rule).

*Flack and Yasukuni.* Flack answered "would I visit Yasukuni if I were PM?" and so discounted the insider tip that Abe would. Abe went. A bait-and-switch updating failure.

*Ulfelder on Hagel.* Base rate: 23 of 24 nominees confirmed (96%). Even granting the critic generous likelihoods (1 in 5 successful nominees botch hearings; 19 in 20 doomed ones do), the posterior fell only to 83%. Hagel was confirmed two weeks later.

*Tim Minto.* Year-3 champion with a Brier of 0.15; on a Syrian-refugee question he updated 34 times with an average move of 3.5 points; his final Brier was 0.07.

*Nate Silver, Colorado and the Senate (worked example).* Silver gives Republicans 60% to win the Senate in early September. The next day a Colorado poll moves the GOP candidate from 45% to 55%. How much do you update? Lower bound: more than zero. Upper bound: one race among many needed, so no more than about 10 points. Narrow: how many winnable races beyond the minimum? Are Colorado's drivers present elsewhere? How predictive are September polls historically? The bracket tightens to 2-9, then 3-7, and settles at +4: 64%. Boring, but it works.

*Devyn Duffy's alerts and Frankel's stale forecast.* Duffy set alerts on key terms: 2,271 forecasts on 140 questions in one season. Frankel's 82% that should have been 99% shows the cost of not sweeping stale forecasts when swamped.

*Keynes.* "When the facts change, I change my mind" apparently was never said by Keynes; the slogan must be a practice, not a quote.

**Evidence.**
- Supers update more often *and* in smaller increments than everyone else (tournament data).
- **Traders:** highest-turnover households earned 11.4% vs. 17.9% for the market.
- **Minto:** 34 updates, average 3.5 points, Brier 0.07.
- **Hagel:** 96% base rate to 83% after generous critic likelihoods.
- **Initial forecasts:** supers' were already about 50% more accurate.

**Key terms.** Belief perseverance; Jenga tower; dilution effect; likelihood ratio / diagnostic value; Bayes' theorem (posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds); units of doubt; underreaction (Scylla) and overreaction (Charybdis); public commitment.

**So what.** Update often, in small steps proportional to diagnostic weight. Know which failure you are prone to on this question (frozen beliefs vs. gust-driven swings). Ego investment drives underreaction; write forecasts so you can discard them cheaply. Ask whether a new fact really bears on the question. When the foundation fails, re-forecast. Picture updating as locating a billiard ball blindfolded: many scraps narrow the range but uncertainty never hits zero. Avoid swinging 40 points on one report.

## Chapter 8: Perpetual Beta

**Central argument.** The strongest predictor of becoming a superforecaster is not IQ but perpetual beta: growth mindset plus grit, expressed as try, fail, analyze, adjust, try again. The cycle works only with informed practice and clear, timely feedback, which vague language and hindsight bias normally destroy.

**Full stories.**

*Mary Simpson.* An economics PhD who missed 2008 and lost her savings, she joined GJP because "I should be better at this": growth mindset in action.

*Keynes.* He lost nearly everything in 1920 and again in 1929, subjected his assumptions to withering scrutiny and invented something close to value investing. His line: "there is no harm in being sometimes wrong, especially if one is promptly found out."

*Polanyi's bicycle.* Tacit knowledge requires doing; no manual substitutes for practice.

*Devyn Duffy's Arafat-polonium postmortem (template).* What we forecast: low probability of detection. What we assumed: polonium's short half-life would make detection "virtually impossible." Where the assumption came from: our own intuition about physics; no one checked. What we didn't do: consider whether decay products offered a detection route; ask anyone with radiochemistry expertise (a teammate on another team had read the Swiss report). Luck or process? Process. Adjustments: don't assume expertise you lack, find an expert when one exists, schedule re-examination of foundational assumptions. The contrast is the typical expert response to the Gorbachev surprise: "I saw it coming all along." One produces learning; the other a story. Duffy also used "we lucked out" on Guinea to postmortem a success.

*Abe and Yasukuni.* A Brier of 1.46: unambiguous failure and therefore a learning opportunity ("lights on").

*Beugoms.* His sparse notes made postmortems impossible, so he now documents reasoning at forecast time ("comments for your future self").

*Sloane and Kilkenny.* Elizabeth Sloane forecast through brain-cancer treatment. Anne Kilkenny e-mailed the UN agency (in English, after a French reply) to learn how refugee data were compiled. Kilkenny also described partisan reactions to her Palin e-mail as "feelings masquerading as knowledge."

**Evidence.**
- **Perpetual beta** predicts success about 3x as strongly as intelligence ("75% perspiration, 25% inspiration").
- **Training booklet** gave about 10% improvement and interacted positively with practice ("fortune favors the prepared mind").
- **Forer effect:** astrology boilerplate rated 4.2/5 as a personal profile; vague statements feel accurate, so self-judged vague forecasts mislead.
- **Hindsight bias:** experts recalled 1988 Soviet-collapse estimates 31 points too high; one moved 20% to 70%.
- **Domain-specific calibration:** meteorologists and bridge players are well calibrated because feedback is prompt and unambiguous; police lie-detection confidence grows with experience while accuracy doesn't. Bridge skill doesn't transfer to politics.
- **Counterfactual asymmetry:** experts embrace "I was almost right" and reject "I was almost wrong."
- **Composite superforecaster:** philosophic outlook (cautious, humble, nondeterministic); thinking style (actively open-minded, intelligent, need for cognition, reflective, numerate); method (pragmatic, analytical, dragonfly-eyed, probabilistic, thoughtful updaters, good intuitive psychologists); work ethic (growth mindset, grit).

**Key terms.** Perpetual beta; growth vs. fixed mindset (Dweck); grit (Duckworth); informed practice; Forer effect; hindsight bias; postmortem; tacit knowledge; composite superforecaster.

**So what.** Treat every forecast as a bet you will be scored on and write it so you can be. Prepare the postmortem at forecast time. After a win ask "what had to go right that I didn't control?" Distinguish experience (years) from feedback (scored outcomes). Avoid "I'm bad at forecasting" as identity; avoid judging your own vague forecasts; don't over-learn from a minor technical slip on a sound approach or under-learn from a flawed assumption; don't assume domain skill transfers.

## Chapter 9: Superteams

**Central argument.** Groups can be mad (Bay of Pigs) or wise (Cuban missile crisis) with the same people; what differs is process. Teams beat individuals by 23%, superteams beat prediction markets by 15-30%, and the mechanism is a culture that preserves independent judgment while sharing information: constructive confrontation, precision questioning, psychological safety and givers.

**Full stories.**

*Bay of Pigs vs. the Cuban missile crisis (process audit).* Bay of Pigs (April 1961): the plan was on the *New York Times* front page three months earlier; the contingency escape route came from an earlier landing site 80 miles away across swamp; the cabinet discussed none of it because cohesion substituted for scrutiny. The CIA presented, advisers nodded, no one asked "how far?" Cuban missile crisis (October 1962): same people, new rules. Kennedy withheld his initial preference for air strikes, left the room, appointed watchdogs (Bobby Kennedy, Sorensen) to bark rude questions, suspended rank, imported outsiders and forced ten alternatives onto the table on day one. Outcome: a blockade and negotiated withdrawal instead of invasion. Kennedy later estimated a one-third to one-half chance the crisis could have gone nuclear. Lesson: personnel changes aren't required; process changes are.

*Tulips and 2005 housing.* Crowd failures where errors piled up rather than canceled.

*Matthies and Worline's precision questions.* Instead of "you're silly," ask "what do you mean by 'pastime'?" and "what evidence, over what time frame?" The Bay of Pigs would have died at "eighty miles of swamp and jungle?"

*Edmondson's nurse.* Psychological safety: the nurse tells the surgeon about the sponge.

*Superteam culture.* Early excessive politeness ("dancing around") suppressed critique; fixed by explicit invitations ("I want push-back") and thanks for criticism. Givers (Marty Rosenthal's "leading from behind," Doug Lorch's tools, Tim Minto's analyses) lifted everyone. Paul Theron's deep dig on Honduras found a country specialist and an obscure analyst by e-mail and shared everything. One top forecaster refused to comment to avoid groupthink; some non-supportive teams did well anyway, which argues against copy-paste recipes.

**Evidence.**
- **Team results:** teams +23% vs. individuals in year 1; a newly minted super placed on a superteam became 50% more accurate; ordinary teams beat the crowd by about 10%; prediction markets beat ordinary teams by about 20%; superteams beat markets by 15-30%. Caveat: markets lacked liquidity and real money.
- **Extremizing** barely helped information-sharing superteams and greatly helped regular teams: it works only to the extent members hold information others lack.
- **Team AOM is emergent:** a property of communication patterns, not the sum of members' traits.
- **Scott Page:** aggregation gains come from different perspectives; the real choice is fine-tuning the ability/diversity mix.
- **Adam Grant:** givers who deliberately model sharing come out on top.

**Key terms.** Groupthink (Janis); wisdom of crowds / independence; constructive confrontation (Grove); precision questioning; premortem; cognitive loafing; psychological safety; "our" vs. "my"; CEO disease; givers/matchers/takers; extremizing; team AOM.

**So what.** Treat unanimity as a symptom. Form judgments independently, then share, and keep the leader's view out until the end. Ask precision questions rather than expressing disagreement. Extremize in proportion to private information. Avoid leaders announcing preferences, hierarchy governing who may challenge, and confusing cohesion with effectiveness.

## Chapter 10: The Leader's Dilemma

**Central argument.** Leaders must be confident, decisive and visionary; superforecasters must be humble, deliberate and willing to change their minds. The contradiction dissolves in mission command (Auftragstaktik): deliberate with doubt, then act with resolve, and push decisions down to those who see the ground, combining intellectual humility (about the problem) with confidence rather than self-doubt (about oneself).

**Full stories.**

*Moltke and the Prussian academies.* Moltke's axioms: "In war, everything is uncertain"; no plan extends with certainty beyond the first encounter with the enemy's main strength; "it is impossible to lay down binding rules." Prussian war academies debated scenarios collectively, juniors critiqued generals, instructors acted as comrades; officers were trained to disobey with good reason (Seydlitz: "after the battle my head is at his disposal, but meanwhile I will make use of it"). By contrast, the US Army of that era used Sears-catalogue orders and Eisenhower was threatened with court-martial for publishing tank ideas.

*Eben Emael, 1940.* The commander's glider landed 100 km off; a sergeant took charge and knocked out the guns; another stray sergeant improvised a ground attack and took 121 prisoners.

*Hitler at Normandy.* Reserves could move only on his personal order, and no one would wake him.

*Eisenhower at D-Day.* He wrote the note taking responsibility for failure before the landing, then projected calm assurance: morale from composure, not claims of certainty. He also said "plans are useless, but planning is indispensable" (Ch 11).

*Petraeus.* Pushed people out of their intellectual comfort zone with unscripted live-fire exercises and graduate school to meet "seriously bright people with very different basic assumptions." "The bold move is the right move except when it's the wrong move."

*Business echoes.* 3M's Coyne ("we do not tell them how"); Bezos's "have backbone; disagree and commit"; Walmart's leadership academy built by a former Royal Marine who finds companies more command-and-control than militaries. Patton: "Tell them what to do, and they will surprise you with their ingenuity." Mission command became US doctrine in 1982.

*Command-and-control vs. mission-command orders (worked example).* C&C: "Approach from the southwest, skirt the factory, seize the canal bridge, occupy the town hall." The captain who finds the bridge already blown, or a second intact bridge HQ didn't know about, has no basis to act and radios for new orders while his men lose confidence. Mission command: "Take the town. Your battalion's purpose is to stop enemy reinforcements reaching the region beyond it; the town severs the key road." Now the captain knows why, sees the unexpected intact bridge on another road would also carry reinforcements, and destroys it on his own initiative.

*Lincoln and the two humilities.* Lincoln: "with firmness in the right, as God gives us to see the right." Annie Duke's two humilities: humility in the face of the game, confidence in the face of opponents.

*The Wehrmacht, Lorch's Aleppo, Frankel's North Korea.* The Wehrmacht was both evil and, man for man, highly effective. Lorch's wishful Aleppo forecast and Frankel's hope-tinted North Korea forecast show values leaking into probabilities.

*Hamlet vs. Henry V.* Ralph Peters's attack: a false dichotomy; leaders must be both.

**Evidence.** The chapter's evidence is historical and doctrinal rather than statistical: Moltke's axioms, the 1982 US doctrine, the wartime cases above, and business echoes. No numbers beyond the 121 prisoners and the 100 km. Caveat: these are illustrations, not controlled results.

**Key terms.** Mission command / Auftragstaktik; deliberation vs. implementation; intellectual humility; two humilities; facts vs. values; green table (micromanagement); confidence vs. certainty; Hamlet vs. Henry V.

**So what.** Run every decision in two modes with a visible switch: doubt-heavy deliberation, doubt-free execution, scheduled review of whether a new decision is needed. Write orders as goal and intent. Keep two humility ledgers ("I may be wrong about this problem," always; "I am not up to this," rarely and separately). Before assessing an adversary ask whether contempt is lowering your estimate of their competence. Avoid choreographed training that never surprises.

## Chapter 11: Are They Really So Super?

**Central argument.** The two strongest critiques, Kahneman's (System 2 vigilance is exhausting and illusions can't be switched off) and Taleb's (only unforecastable black swans matter), are partly right. Yet superforecasters showed real resistance to scope insensitivity, black swans are often gray and their consequences unfold through forecastable steps, and even planning for surprise requires probability judgments.

**Full stories.**

*General Flynn's "unprecedented" claim.* He said societal conflict was "pretty unprecedented," contradicting every dataset on declining battle deaths since the 1950s, yet the reports on his desk felt like all there was (WYSIATI). Like the Müller-Lyer illusion, knowing does not un-see; you check with a ruler.

*The Lin Wells memo (April 2001).* Every decade since 1900 looked nothing like the previous decade's expectations, "so we should plan accordingly." Five months later, 9/11.

*9/11 and WWI as gray swans.* 9/11 was war-gamed in 1998; WWI was feared for a decade. The consequences of 9/11 (the ultimatum to the Taliban, their refusal, the invasion) are each a scorable short-range question.

*The 1914 Foreign Office official (worked example).* He believes war deaths cluster around 100,000 with a bell-curve tail, so a ten-million-death war looks like a one-in-millions freak and the forecaster predicting it (and a sixty-million-death sequel) a crank. Swap in a wealth-like distribution, where billionaires occur at one in 700,000 rather than one in trillions, and the same catastrophe becomes thousands of times more probable: still unlikely, but like a lottery ticket paying one in 500 rather than one in five million. A rational official who knew the true distribution would have bought insurance (diplomacy, restraint, contingency) more aggressively. Procedure: identify whether the variable is height-like or wealth-like.

*Tokyo vs. Kansas earthquake codes; the two-war doctrine.* Resilience is costly; Tokyo's codes make sense, Kansas's would not; the two-war doctrine rests on a probability judgment.

*Investing styles.* Khosla: "a 90% chance of failing people don't like, but a 10% chance of changing the world people love" vs. poker-style edges (68% when others say 60%).

*Hitler, Stalin and Mao.* Kahneman: a 12.5% chance all three were born male; history is one draw from many once-possible worlds.

*Japan in the 1980s; China today.* Hindsight bias makes the past look predictable and the future more predictable than it is.

**Evidence.**
- **Scope insensitivity:** people would pay about $10 to clean lakes in one region or in all 250,000 lakes; about $80 to save 2,000, 20,000 or 200,000 birds. In forecasting the time frame is the scope: regulars gave Assad's fall 40% at three months and 41% at six; supers gave 15% and 24% (no one saw both versions). Caveat: supers only partly resist.
- **Adversarial collaboration:** Kahneman-Klein, then Kahneman-Mellers on scope insensitivity.
- **System 2 to System 1 habit:** outside-view reflexes can become automatic like a golf swing; software tools help (Lorch's diverse-source selector).
- **History:** 1-2% annual growth compounding across centuries mattered as much as any jump.
- **Black swan limits:** tournaments of months-long questions cannot test skill at 0.1% events.

**Key terms.** WYSIATI; scope insensitivity; black swan (strict vs. modest); gray swan; fat tails; antifragility / resilience; hindsight bias; adversarial collaboration; Quadrennial Defense Review.

**So what.** Run the scope stress-test (vary the deadline and threshold; if your number doesn't move, you are scope-insensitive). Before calling something unprecedented, Google the base rate. Decompose an "unforecastable" shock into its chain of consequences. Ask of every resilience investment "against what probability?" and say the number. Assume fat tails for conflict, markets and history. Don't dismiss forecasting because black swans exist, and don't dismiss black swans because most are gray.

## Chapter 12: What's Next?

**Central argument.** An evidence-based forecasting revolution is possible, as evidence-based medicine was, but faces the kto-kogo reality that forecasts serve tribal and career interests more than accuracy. The way forward: keep score, cluster small scorable questions to approach big unscorable ones, pair superquestioners with superforecasters, and use adversarial collaboration to depolarize debates.

**Full stories.**

*Kto-kogo.* Lenin's "who, whom": argument and evidence as adornments on a power struggle. Nate Silver went from prophet to stumblebum among Democrats when his 2014 Senate forecast turned unwelcome. A Santander analyst was fired for an accurate but politically unwelcome forecast. Dick Morris defended his Romney-landslide call as duty, not belief.

*Codman.* His End Result System (record ailment, treatment, outcome; publish) got him expelled from Mass General and Harvard, and was then adopted by the profession. Evidence-based medicine, policy, philanthropy (Gates Foundation evaluations) and sports analytics show a broad shift from authority and intuition to measurement. IARPA funding the tournament despite the wrong-side-of-maybe risk is the surprising proof.

*Keynesians vs. Austerians.* Four years passed and no one budged; the 2010 letter's signatories said in 2014 they'd change nothing ("note the word risk, note the absence of a date"). The alternative: identify disagreements, agree on precise forecast questions (how much inflation, by what benchmark, over what period), run many, and accept split decisions as information. Krugman and Ferguson are the notes' example of brute-force hammering of opponents' failures without owning your own.

*Friedman's question.* His 2002 "Is Iraq the way it is because Saddam is, or vice versa?" passes the smack-the-forehead test. His vague petro-state column should be read as a question, not a forecast. Aim for a Tom-Bill symbiosis.

*Korea question cluster (worked example, 2013).* Recent escalations: a rocket launch, a nuclear test, renunciation of the armistice, a cyberattack, the severed hotline, nuclear threats. Cluster: (1) Will North Korea attempt a multistage rocket launch between 7 Jan and 1 Sep 2013? (2) Another nuclear test in the same window? (3) Rebuff six-party talks? (4) Fire artillery at the South? (5) Will a North Korean vessel fire on a South Korean vessel? Each has a date, a definition and a number; each yes nudges "this ends badly" up, each no down. After a year the cluster's Brier scores show which forecasters and mental models deserve weight on the big question no one could score directly.

*Drezner's pledge.* After the Scottish referendum: "make a clear prediction and offer a confidence interval... I want to keep score."

*The hospital with 100% survival.* A hospital can hit 100% survival by refusing sick patients: metric capture, illustrating Wieseltier's partly valid objection ("where wisdom once was, quantification will now be").

**Evidence.**
- The chapter is argument and example rather than data: the Codman, Gates Foundation and sports-analytics shifts; the IARPA tournament itself; the Keynesian/Austerian standoff.
- **Brier limits:** it treats misses and false alarms equally; asymmetric costs can be built in by telling forecasters the rules in advance. Imperfect scores still beat titles, confidence, storytelling, book sales and Davos invitations, as imperfect credit scores beat whimsical loan officers.
- **Blame-game ping-pong:** missed 9/11 leads to over-alarm; WMD false alarm leads to under-alarm.

**Key terms.** Kto-kogo; End Result System; Bayesian question clustering; smack-the-forehead test; superquestioner; adversarial collaboration; "choose your own analysis"; attentive public; good judgment beyond foresight (moral judgment and question-asking).

**So what.** Expect resistance; accuracy competes with power, tribe and career. When a pundit offers a big narrative ask which scorable small questions would test it. Treat a refusal to be scored as information about incentives. Use "I was wrong" headlines as a metric of a field's health. When two camps clash, propose a joint list of precise bets. Improve scores; don't abandon them. Don't believe big questions must stay vague because they are big.

## Chapter 13 (Appendix): Ten Commandments for Aspiring Superforecasters

**Central argument.** Eleven guidelines distilled from the book and from training experimentally shown to raise accuracy about 10%. Each is a balance between opposing errors, and the last is a warning not to treat any of them as binding.

**Full stories.**

*Peter Backus's women in London (Commandment 2).* Backus Fermi-ized the number of compatible women in London, arriving at about 26: a demonstration of daring to be wrong with explicit guesses.

*Summers's doubling (Commandment 3).* Summers doubled estimates and moved up a time unit, then adjusted for exceptions.

*The migration project (worked example).* "When will the migration project finish?" (1) Triage: forecastable, in the Goldilocks zone. (2) Fermi-ize: remaining modules × hours per module ÷ available hours, with each factor guessed explicitly. (3) Outside view: how late were the last five projects of this kind? If typically 2x over, anchor there (Summers's rule) before adjusting for this team. (4) Set lead indicators (first-module completion date, defect rate) and update in small steps. (5) List clashing forces: new hires ramping up vs. holiday season. (6) State "70% by 30 June, 90% by 31 August," not "should be done by summer." (7) Commit to the number; don't waffle at 50%. (8) Schedule the postmortem now and record the reasoning. (9) Ask the team to attack the estimate with precision questions. (10) Do this every project. (11) When this project is genuinely unlike the last five, say so and adjust the outside view; don't apply the 2x rule blindly.

*Other illustrations.* Obama applies granularity to March Madness but said "fifty-fifty" on bin Laden; "slam dunk" means offering infinite odds; Lasorda's dove (too tight kills, too loose loses); "Who wins in 2028?" is impossible but narrowing 2016 to plausible contenders is not; comparison classes for Kony or Greece.

**Evidence.** The training experiment: roughly 10% accuracy improvement from the guidelines (also noted in Ch 1 and 8). The notes give no further statistics for this chapter.

**Key terms.** Goldilocks zone; clocklike/cloudlike; Fermi-izing; comparison class; lead indicators; dove-hawk hybrid; granularity; calibration and resolution; postmortem; perspective taking; precision questioning; constructive confrontation; deliberate practice.

**The eleven guidelines.**
1. **Triage:** work the Goldilocks zone; skip clocklike questions (rules of thumb) and cloudlike ones (even models can't beat the chimp).
2. **Break intractable problems into tractable sub-problems:** Fermi-ize; expose assumptions.
3. **Balance inside and outside views:** nothing is 100% unique.
4. **Balance under- and overreacting to evidence:** belief updating is dental hygiene; hunt lead indicators; usually 0.4 to 0.35, jump on diagnostic signals.
5. **Look for clashing causal forces:** doves and hawks list in advance what would move them; synthesize into a hybrid.
6. **Distinguish as many degrees of doubt as the problem permits, but no more.**
7. **Balance under- and overconfidence, prudence and decisiveness:** calibration and resolution; don't overcorrect the latest miss.
8. **Look for the errors behind your mistakes, but beware rearview-mirror hindsight.**
9. **Bring out the best in others and let others bring out the best in you.**
10. **Master the error-balancing bicycle:** practice with feedback.
11. **Don't treat commandments as commandments** ("two cases will never be exactly the same," Moltke).

Mapping to chapters: 1 to Ch 1, 11; 2-3 to Ch 5; 4 to Ch 7; 5 to Ch 3, 5; 6 to Ch 6; 7 to Ch 3; 8 to Ch 8; 9 to Ch 9; 10 to Ch 8; 11 to Ch 10.

**So what.** Treat the list as a pre-flight checklist, not a law code. Every commandment is a dial between two errors; locate yourself on each dial for the question at hand. Avoid spending effort on the unforecastable or trivially forecastable, vague verbiage where a number was available, avoiding only the most recent mistake, and reading about forecasting instead of forecasting.

# PART 3: Key learnings

## 3(a) Core concepts and frameworks

**1. Scorability.** *Definition:* a forecast needs a date, defined terms, a numeric probability and many instances. *Use when:* before making or accepting any prediction. *Common mistake:* accepting "risk" or "serious possibility" language; or expecting a single 70% forecast to be judged right or wrong (wrong-side-of-maybe).

**2. Calibration and resolution (Brier).** *Definition:* calibration is whether 70% calls happen 70% of the time; resolution is decisiveness; Brier captures both (0 perfect, 0.5 random, 2.0 worst). *Use when:* evaluating any forecaster against benchmarks on the same questions. *Mistake:* reading a Brier of 0.2 without knowing question difficulty (Springfield vs. Phoenix); being "calibrated but cowardly."

**3. Fox vs. hedgehog.** *Definition:* eclectic probabilistic thinkers vs. Big-Idea thinkers; foxes forecast better. *Use when:* judging commentators or checking your own style. *Mistake:* rewarding hedgehogs because they sound confident and are on TV.

**4. Dragonfly eye / aggregation.** *Definition:* synthesize many perspectives; the crowd within is nearly as good as another person. *Use when:* after forming your own view. *Mistake:* aggregating non-independent views, or letting loud voices dominate.

**5. Fermi-izing.** *Definition:* decompose into knowable and unknowable parts with explicit guesses. *Use when:* a question feels unanswerable or invites a gut number. *Mistake:* hiding guesses in a black box; or ignoring that crude guesses can compound (exposed errors still beat hidden ones).

**6. Outside view then inside view.** *Definition:* start from the base rate of the comparison class, then investigate hypothesis by hypothesis ("what would have to be true?"). *Use when:* every question. *Mistake:* starting from a vivid story (anchoring), or choosing an unrefined reference class.

**7. Granular probability.** *Definition:* single-percentage-point statements, as many degrees of maybe as the problem permits. *Use when:* stating the final judgment. *Mistake:* using 50% for "I don't know"; false precision without scoring.

**8. Frequent small updates.** *Definition:* update in steps proportional to diagnostic weight (likelihood ratio); re-forecast when the basis collapses. *Use when:* throughout the life of a question. *Mistake:* underreaction from ego (Jenga tower) or overreaction to noise (dilution effect).

**9. Scope stress-test.** *Definition:* vary time frame and threshold in your head and confirm the probability moves sensibly. *Use when:* any question with a deadline or threshold. *Mistake:* the same probability at 3 and 6 months.

**10. Regression-to-the-mean test.** *Definition:* slow regression indicates skill, fast indicates luck. *Use when:* evaluating a track record. *Mistake:* crowning anyone from one dramatic hit, ignoring how many were trying.

**11. Postmortem (wins and losses).** *Definition:* record reasoning at forecast time; at resolution ask where it went right or wrong and what luck contributed. *Use when:* whenever a question resolves. *Mistake:* hindsight rewriting; over-learning from a minor technical slip.

**12. Superteam protocol.** *Definition:* independent judgments, then sharing; precision questions; premortems; leader's view last; givers; extremize in proportion to private information. *Use when:* group forecasting or decisions. *Mistake:* mistaking cohesion for correctness, politeness for openness.

**13. Question clustering.** *Definition:* approach an unscorable big question through many small scorable ones. *Use when:* a big question matters but cannot be scored. *Mistake:* believing big questions must stay vague.

**14. Mission command.** *Definition:* deliberate with doubt, decide, execute with resolve; give goal and intent, not method. *Use when:* leading under uncertainty. *Mistake:* confusing confidence with certainty claims; micromanaging.

**15. Adversarial collaboration.** *Definition:* rivals agree on precise tests of their disagreement. *Use when:* a polarized debate. *Mistake:* presuming good faith where loud voices refuse.

**16. Reasonable vs. correct.** *Definition:* judge decisions by the evidence at the time. *Use when:* reviewing any decision. *Mistake:* bait-and-switching "good decision?" for "good outcome?"

**17. Bait and switch.** *Definition:* answering an easier question than the one asked. *Use when:* checking emotionally obvious questions. *Mistake:* answering "would I do it?" for "will they do it?" (Yasukuni).

**18. Perpetual beta.** *Definition:* try, fail, analyze, adjust, try again (growth mindset + grit). *Use when:* building a practice. *Mistake:* "I'm bad at this" as identity.

## 3(b) The big ideas of the book (numbered)

1. **Foresight is measurable and learnable.** Skill shows up when forecasts are scored; superforecasters beat regulars by about 60% and intelligence analysts with classified access by about 30%. *Where:* Ch 1, 4.
2. **Predictability is real but bounded.** Clocks-vs-clouds is a spectrum; accuracy falls to chance at 3-5 years, so the work is in the Goldilocks zone of months-long questions. *Where:* Ch 1, 13.
3. **You can't improve what you can't score.** Dates, defined terms, numbers and many instances turn rhetoric into forecasts, and Brier scores let you compare. *Where:* Ch 3.
4. **How you think matters more than what you think.** Foxes beat hedgehogs; fame correlates inversely with accuracy. *Where:* Ch 3.
5. **Judge decisions by reasonableness, not outcomes.** The WMD call was reasonable and wrong; the sin was certainty. A 70% forecast is not wrong when the 30% happens. *Where:* Ch 3, 4, 6.
6. **Skill, not luck.** Year-to-year correlation of 0.65 and ~30% turnover show skill-plus-luck, with no infallibles. *Where:* Ch 4.
7. **Method beats raw brilliance.** Above a modest threshold, Fermi-izing, base rates, and synthesis add more than IQ. *Where:* Ch 5.
8. **Think in many shades of maybe.** Yes/no are certainty claims; granularity predicts accuracy; 50% usually means "I don't know." *Where:* Ch 6.
9. **Update often, in small steps, sized to evidence.** Avoid frozen beliefs and gust-driven swings, but re-forecast when the basis collapses. *Where:* Ch 7.
10. **Perpetual beta beats intelligence.** Growth mindset plus grit predict success about 3x as strongly as IQ, and require clear forecasts, recorded reasoning and scored feedback. *Where:* Ch 8.
11. **The group is its own animal.** Process, not roster, determines whether groups are mad or wise; independence first, then aggregation. Teams +23%; superteams beat markets by 15-30%. *Where:* Ch 9.
12. **Lead with doubt, act with resolve.** Mission command gives goal and intent; humility about the problem coexists with confidence in yourself; separate facts from values. *Where:* Ch 10.
13. **Illusions can't be switched off, only checked.** WYSIATI and scope insensitivity persist; supers partly resist; black swans are mostly gray, but fat tails and rare events demand humility. *Where:* Ch 2, 11.
14. **Forecasting reform meets kto-kogo.** Accuracy competes with power, tribe and career, but evidence-based medicine and IARPA show change is possible; cluster questions, pair questioners with forecasters, and use adversarial collaboration. *Where:* Ch 12.
15. **Practice, with mindfulness, not dogma.** The Ten (eleven) Commandments are dials between errors, learned by doing and never to be treated as commandments. *Where:* Ch 13.

## 3(d) Anti-patterns to avoid

- "All expert forecasts are useless" (garbled EPJ); buying forecasts without asking for accuracy.
- Judging forecasters by storytelling, confidence or fame.
- Long-range geopolitical forecasting presented as knowledge (20-year defense reviews, "the coming century").
- Forecasts with no date, no defined terms, no number ("risk," "may," "serious possibility").
- Wrong-side-of-maybe judgments of single forecasts.
- Certainty language ("slam dunk," "has," "will") in estimates.
- Process checklists as a substitute for scoring accuracy.
- Lottery-winner logic and business books that reverse-engineer qualities from winners.
- Diving into story-rich details before checking the base rate.
- Answering the emotionally salient question (bait and switch).
- Using 50% for "I don't know"; granularity as theater.
- Ignoring signals because the actor's choices seem irrational to you; swinging 40 points on one report; leaving stale forecasts unswept.
- "I'm bad at forecasting" as identity; judging your own vague forecasts; over- or under-learning from failures; assuming domain skill transfers.
- Leaders announcing preferences first; hierarchy governing challenge; confusing cohesion with effectiveness; copy-paste team recipes.
- Confusing confidence with certainty or humility with self-doubt; micromanaging; choreographed training; wishful forecasting.
- Treating a feeling of chaos as evidence of chaos; same probability at every time frame; dismissing forecasting because black swans exist.
- Rewarding forecasters for serving the tribe; metric capture (100% survival); brute-force hammering of opponents without owning your own failures.
- Spending effort on the unforecastable; avoiding only the most recent mistake; reading about forecasting instead of forecasting.

## 3(e) If you remember only ten things

1. No date, no defined terms, no number means no forecast; make it scorable.
2. A 70% call that misses is not necessarily wrong; judge across many forecasts with calibration and resolution (Brier).
3. Foxes beat hedgehogs; aggregate perspectives, including your own second opinion.
4. Triage, then Fermi-ize.
5. Start from the base rate (outside view), then adjust on the inside view.
6. State granular numbers; 50% usually means "I don't know."
7. Update often and in small steps sized to the evidence; re-forecast when the basis collapses.
8. Keep records at forecast time; hindsight bias will rewrite your memory; postmortem wins too.
9. Protect independent judgment in teams; precision questions, premortems, givers; unanimity is a warning.
10. Perpetual beta: try, fail, analyze, adjust, try again, and don't treat the commandments as commandments.

## 3(f) Open questions and limits the notes flag

- **Long horizons and tails.** Accuracy falls to chance at 3-5 years; tournaments of 1-12 month questions say nothing about 0.1% events: "we don't know, and shouldn't fool ourselves that we do" (Ch 1, 11).
- **Black swan definitions.** Strictly inconceivable events are rare, but "highly improbable consequential" events exist and cannot be tested (Ch 11).
- **Illusions persist.** Even supers only partly resist scope insensitivity; System 2 vigilance is exhausting (Ch 11).
- **Reference classes are judgment calls;** for truly rare events the notes suggest bracketing a range and taking the midpoint (patterns).
- **Brier is a work in progress.** It treats misses and false alarms equally; asymmetric costs must be agreed in advance (Ch 12).
- **Market comparison caveat.** The prediction markets lacked liquidity and real money (Ch 9).
- **Superquestioners.** Their psychology is "probably" different and not established; good judgment includes moral judgment and question-asking, which are uncounted (Ch 12).
- **Team recipes.** Some non-supportive teams still did well; one top forecaster refused to comment to avoid groupthink; anointing "supers" risks ego (CEO disease) (Ch 9).
- **Humanists' objection.** Quantification must itself be scrutinized (Ch 12).
- **Kto-kogo.** Accuracy is often one goal among many, sometimes irrelevant (Ch 12).

## Discrepancies and handling in the notes

- The appendix is called "Ten Commandments" but lists **eleven** guidelines; this file follows the notes (eleven).
- Kent's "probable" is given as 75% ±12 in the cheatsheet and as 63-87% in the glossary; these are the same range.
- "Superforecasters are top ~2%" and "regulars above ~70%, supers above ~80% of the population" refer to different measures (forecast accuracy vs. intelligence).
- Various "better by X%" figures (60%, 78%, 30-70%, 50%, 40%, 30%) apply to different comparisons and years; each is attributed to its context above.
