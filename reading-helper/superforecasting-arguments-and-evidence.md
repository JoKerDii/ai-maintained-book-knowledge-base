# Superforecasting: Key Arguments, Stories & Evidence by Chapter
*Philip E. Tetlock & Dan Gardner*

Each chapter has: **Argument** (the claim), **Stories & evidence** (what the book uses to support it), **Key numbers**, and **So what** (the lesson). ## The Big Arc in One Paragraph
Ch 1–2 argue that forecasting skill is real but unmeasured, and that our intuition misleads us. Ch 3–4 show how to measure it and who turns out to be good. Ch 5–8 explain *how* they think (method, probability, updating, mindset). Ch 9–10 apply it to groups and leaders. Ch 11–12 test the limits and ask what could change. Ch 13 packs it into eleven guidelines.

## Ch 1. An Optimistic Skeptic
**Argument:** Forecasting is a measurable, learnable skill, neither a gift nor a fraud. The "dart-throwing chimp" finding applied to average experts on multi-year questions. On shorter horizons some people have real foresight, and what makes them good is what they do, not who they are.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Lorenz's butterfly and the Arab Spring:** one street vendor's act set off a region-wide upheaval. This is the skeptic's case that nonlinear systems limit prediction.
- **Weather forecasting:** reliable at 1–2 days, chimp-level at about 8. It improved because meteorologists run the loop of forecast, measure, revise.
- **Expert Political Judgment (EPJ), 1984–2004:** the average expert did about as well as random guessing, and worst on long horizons.
- **Good Judgment Project (GJP):** 20,000+ volunteers, ~500 IARPA questions, over 1 million judgments. It beat the control group by 60% (year 1) and 78% (year 2).
- **Bill Flack:** a retired USDA employee in Nebraska who forecast ~300 questions and was scored on every one. Tom Friedman advises presidents but has never been scored. The difference is the scoring, not intelligence.
- **A one-hour tutorial** on basic concepts improved accuracy by about 10%.

**Key numbers:** ~2% of forecasters were "super"; accuracy falls toward chance at 3–5 years.

**So what:** Be specific about *what* you predict, *how far out*, and *under what conditions*. Demand scores. Foresight is like eyesight: improving from 20/100 to 20/40 is not hawk-eyed, but it changes your life.

## Ch 2. Illusions of Knowledge
**Argument:** Medicine stagnated for millennia because doctors trusted the view from the tip of their nose. It advanced when they accepted doubt and ran controlled trials. Forecasting today looks like nineteenth-century medicine.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Archie Cochrane's misdiagnosis (1956):** an eminent surgeon told him he was dying. Cochrane, a founder of evidence-based medicine, believed him. The pathology report found no cancer at all. If the skeptic can be fooled, anyone can.
- **Cochrane's reversed-numbers trial:** he told cardiologists preliminary results favored the cardiac units, and they demanded he stop the "unethical" trial. He had actually reversed the numbers: home care was ahead. Their reaction exposed their certainty.
- **Galen's logic:** the treatment cures everyone except the incurable, who die. This is confirmation bias built into a theory.
- **Bradford Hill's randomized trial template (1921):** random assignment balances unknown differences so outcomes can be attributed to treatment.
- **Split-brain patients** invent reasons for actions they can't explain. Journalists explain market moves with news that came out after them.
- **Oslo bombing (2011):** suspecting an Islamist attack was reasonable. The error was treating the hypothesis as settled.
- **Kahneman–Klein reconciliation:** fireground commanders and chess players have valid cues and practice, so intuition works. Stock pickers have neither, so it doesn't.

**So what:** You can't switch System 1 off. Watch for bait and switch (answering an easier question) and ask "what would convince me I'm wrong?" Confidence signals a coherent story, not a true one.

## Ch 3. Keeping Score
**Argument:** You can't improve what you can't score, and most forecasts can't be scored. Fix that and collect many forecasts, and you find that foxes beat hedgehogs. Aggregating perspectives is the mechanism.

**Stories & evidence**
- **The Bernanke letter (2010):** warning of "risk" from quantitative easing, with no time frame, is unfalsifiable. The book shows how to rewrite it with a date, a defined measure, and probabilities.
- **Sherman Kent and "serious possibility":** one analyst read it as 80/20, another as 20/80. Kent proposed a numeric scale ("I'd rather be a bookie than a goddamn poet").
- **Bay of Pigs "fair chance":** the author meant 3-to-1 against, but Kennedy heard optimism.
- **Ballmer's iPhone remark:** in context it was ambiguous, not laughably wrong.
- **Brier score (1950):** 0 is perfect, 0.5 is random, 2.0 is maximally wrong. Scores need benchmarks: 0.2 is excellent in Springfield and poor in Phoenix.
- **EPJ, 284 experts and ~28,000 predictions:** hedgehogs (one Big Idea, "furthermore," "just wait") did worse than foxes, even in their own specialty. Fame correlated inversely with accuracy.
- **Galton's ox (1,197 vs. 1,198 lb):** the crowd's average was nearly exact. Foxes do inside one skull what crowds do collectively (the dragonfly eye).
- **"Guess two-thirds of the average" (FT/Thaler, 1997):** pure logic says 0, but the winning answer was 13 because contestants are human.

**So what:** A forecast needs a time frame, defined terms, a number, and many instances. Calibration and resolution are both required. Avoid the wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy.

## Ch 4. Superforecasters
**Argument:** The Iraq WMD "slam dunk" was a reasonable judgment expressed with unreasonable certainty. The shock led IARPA to run a tournament. It found that ordinary people with the right habits beat professionals, and that their edge was skill, not luck.

**Stories & evidence**
- **NIE 2002-16HC:** "Iraq has... Baghdad has..." There were no red teams, no devil's advocates, and no dissenting paper.
- **Reasonable vs. correct:** a 60–70% estimate instead of certainty might have stopped the invasion. Judging a decision by its outcome is the "poker novice" blunder.
- **IARPA tournament:** five teams, daily forecasts from Sept 2011 to June 2015, ~500 questions. Teams had to beat the control by 20% in year 1, rising to 50% by year 4.
- **Doug Lorch:** a retired IBM programmer. Brier 0.22 in year 1 (5th of 2,800), then 0.14 in year 2 (best). He beat a prediction market by 40% and the control by 60%.
- **First super cohort:** 58 people, collective Brier 0.25 vs. 0.37 for everyone else. At 300 days out they beat regulars at 100 days. They beat analysts with classified access by ~30%.
- **The luck test (regression to the mean):** year-to-year correlation was ≈ 0.65 (father–son height is ≈ 0.5). About 30% of supers drop out each year and ~70% remain. A coin-flip explanation has under 1 in 100 million odds.
- **Langer's rigged coin flips:** Yale students who saw early lucky streaks believed they had skill.

**So what:** Judge decisions by what was knowable. Meaningful accountability means scored accuracy, not process checklists. Extremizing an aggregate of top performers beat a multi-billion-dollar analytic apparatus.

## Ch 5. Supersmart?
**Argument:** Supers are smart and knowledgeable but not off the charts. What separates them is method: Fermi-ize, take the outside view, then the inside view, then synthesize. Their disposition is that beliefs are hypotheses, not treasures.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Intelligence threshold:** regulars scored above ~70% of the population and supers above ~80%. The big jump is public to forecaster.
- **McNamara's "best and brightest"** escalated Vietnam without analyzing their assumptions.
- **Pets example:** US households owning pets is 62%, but for single-family detached homes ~73%. This is how you refine a reference class.
- **Arafat exhumation question:** with no base rate, bracket it. It must be well above 0 and well below 100, so start near 50%.
- **David Rogg's forecast of an Islamist attack in Western Europe (21 Jan–31 Mar 2015):** six attacks in five years gives 1.2 per year. He adjusted to 1.8 per year, then scaled to the 69-day window: ≈ 34%. He posted the whole chain for others to attack.
- **Peggy Noonan on Bush's rebounding approval:** all ex-presidents' approval rises, so she skipped the base rate.
- **Summers's rule:** double the estimate and move up one time unit.
- **Doug Lorch's reading selector** forces ideological and geographic diversity into his news diet.

**So what:** Base rate first, then the story. Write your reasoning and read it as a stranger. Flip the question's wording to counter confirmation bias. The crowd within is nearly as good as a second person.

## Ch 6. Superquants?
**Argument:** Supers are numerate but rarely use math. What they have is probabilistic thinking: many settings of "maybe" instead of yes/no/maybe. Granularity (single percentage points) predicts accuracy.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Tversky's three-setting dial:** gonna happen, not gonna happen, maybe. It works for lions and fails for forecasting.
- **Bin Laden compound meeting:** estimates ran from ~30% to 90%+ (median about 70%). The real Panetta wanted honest disagreement. In *Zero Dark Thirty*, Maya's "100%" was right and unreasonable. Obama's "coin flip" may have discarded information.
- **Granularity (Mellers):** forecasters using tens < fives < ones. Rounding supers' forecasts to 0.05 hurt; rounding regulars to 0.2 barely mattered.
- **Certainty premium:** people pay 2–3× more to go from 5% to 0% risk than from 10% to 5%.
- **Rubin's rule:** "absolutely" is unacceptable; say 60% and argue whether it's 59.
- **Fate vs. probability:** "everything happens for a reason" correlates with well-being but with worse Brier scores. Probabilistic thinkers ask "how," not "why."
- **Munger:** without elementary probability you go through life "like a one-legged man in an ass-kicking contest."

**So what:** Ask how many degrees of maybe you can honestly distinguish. 50% often means "I don't know." Stay in 35–65% on cloudlike (aleatory) questions. Disagreement is data to aggregate.

## Ch 7. Supernewsjunkies?
**Argument:** Supers win partly by updating, but their initial forecasts are already ~50% more accurate. Good updating is as hard as forecasting: steer between underreaction and overreaction with many small steps, but jump when your basis collapses.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Arafat-polonium:** the Swiss lab's unexplained delay was a subtle signal. Flack moved 60% to 65%.
- **General DeWitt (1942):** "No sabotage has occurred is a... confirming indication that it will." This is belief perseverance. The Jenga tower is identity, expertise, and public commitments.
- **Dilution effect:** irrelevant details (Robert plays tennis, David skis) sway judgment. The highest-turnover households earned 11.4% vs. the market's 17.9%.
- **Tim Minto:** 34 updates on a Syrian-refugee question, average move 3.5 points, final Brier 0.07.
- **Ulfelder on Hagel:** base rate 23/24 nominees confirmed (96%). With generous likelihood ratios the posterior only dropped to 83%. Hagel was confirmed.
- **Doug Lorch's 95% forecast:** it rested on a report that was clearly failing. He went 95% → 55% → 15%, a fresh forecast rather than a gradual retreat.
- **Bait-and-switch update failure:** Flack asked "would I visit Yasukuni?" and discounted the tip that Abe would. Abe went.
- **Senate-race example:** a Colorado poll moves the GOP candidate 45% → 55%. Bracketing the update (more than 0, less than 10, then 2–9, 3–7) lands at +4, from 60% to 64%.

**So what:** Update often, in small steps proportional to the evidence's diagnostic weight (likelihood ratio). Know whether you tend to freeze or to swing. When the foundation fails, re-forecast.

## Ch 8. Perpetual Beta
**Argument:** The strongest predictor of becoming a superforecaster is perpetual beta, not IQ: growth mindset plus grit, running try, fail, analyze, adjust, try again. It needs informed practice and clear, timely feedback.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Mary Simpson:** an economics PhD who missed 2008 and lost her savings. She joined GJP because "I should be better at this."
- **Keynes:** lost nearly everything in 1920 and 1929, scrutinized his assumptions, and developed something close to value investing.
- **Polanyi's bicycle:** tacit knowledge can't be learned from a manual.
- **Forer effect:** astrology boilerplate was rated 4.2/5 as a personal profile. Vague forecasts, like horoscopes, can't fail.
- **Hindsight bias:** experts recalled their 1988 Soviet-collapse estimates ~31 points too high.
- **Calibration is domain-specific:** meteorologists and bridge players are calibrated because feedback is prompt. Police lie-detection confidence grows with experience while accuracy doesn't.
- **Devyn Duffy and Guinea:** he postmortemed a win ("we lucked out"). The Arafat postmortem found the team never consulted a radiochemistry expert.
- **Grit (Duckworth):** Elizabeth Sloane forecast through brain-cancer treatment. Anne Kilkenny e-mailed a UN agency to learn how refugee data were compiled.
- **Composite superforecaster:** cautious, humble, nondeterministic, actively open-minded, numerate, probabilistic, a good updater, and gritty. Perpetual beta predicts ~3× as strongly as intelligence: "75% perspiration, 25% inspiration."

**So what:** Write your reasoning at forecast time, because memory will rewrite it. Postmortem successes too. Experience (years) isn't feedback (scored outcomes).

## Ch 9. Superteams
**Argument:** Groups can be mad (Bay of Pigs) or wise (Cuban missile crisis) with the same people. Process decides which. Teams beat individuals, and superteams beat markets, by preserving independent judgment while sharing information.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Bay of Pigs (April 1961):** the plan was on the *New York Times* front page three months earlier. The escape route ran through 80 miles of swamp, and nobody asked "how far?" Cohesion substituted for scrutiny.
- **Cuban missile crisis (Oct 1962):** same people, new rules. Kennedy withheld his preference and left the room. He appointed watchdogs, suspended rank, imported outsiders, and forced ten alternatives onto the table. He later put the odds of nuclear war at one-third to one-half.
- **Janis's groupthink:** agreement taken as proof of correctness.
- **Wisdom-of-crowds failures:** tulips and 2005 housing, where errors piled up instead of canceling.
- **Results:** teams +23% over individuals (year 1). A newly minted super placed on a superteam became 50% more accurate. Ordinary teams beat the crowd by ~10%, markets beat ordinary teams by ~20%, and superteams beat markets by 15–30% (caveat: the markets lacked liquidity and real money).
- **Team AOM is emergent:** open-minded people who don't engage each other add up to less than their sum.
- **Givers, matchers, takers (Adam Grant):** givers like Marty Rosenthal, Doug Lorch, and Tim Minto lifted everyone.
- **Extremizing** helped regular teams a lot and information-sharing superteams barely at all.
- **Edmondson's psychological safety:** the nurse who tells the surgeon about the sponge.

**So what:** Form judgments independently first, then share. Keep the leader's view out of the room. Use precision questioning ("what do you mean by...?", "what evidence, over what time frame?") and premortems. Treat unanimity as a symptom.

## Ch 10. The Leader's Dilemma
**Argument:** Leaders must be confident and decisive. Superforecasters must be humble and ready to change their minds. Mission command (Auftragstaktik) dissolves the conflict: deliberate with doubt, then act with resolve, and push decisions down to those who see the ground.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Moltke:** "no plan... extends with certainty beyond the first encounter with the enemy's main strength." So train officers to think, criticize, and with good reason disobey.
- **Prussian war academies** debated scenarios collectively. In the US Army of the same era, Eisenhower was threatened with court-martial for publishing tank ideas.
- **Eben Emael (1940):** the commander's glider landed 100 km off. A sergeant took charge and knocked out the guns, and another stray sergeant improvised an attack and took 121 prisoners.
- **Hitler at Normandy:** reserves could move only on his personal order, and no one would wake him.
- **Eisenhower on D-Day:** he wrote the note accepting blame for failure beforehand, then projected calm assurance.
- **Petraeus:** unscripted live-fire exercises, and graduate school to meet "seriously bright people with very different basic assumptions."
- **Business echoes:** 3M ("we do not tell them how"), Bezos's "disagree and commit," Walmart's leadership academy.
- **Wehrmacht:** evil and, man for man, highly effective. Wishful forecasts like Lorch's on Aleppo show values leaking into probabilities.

**So what:** Give goals and intent, not methods. Project composure, not certainty. Intellectual humility (about the problem) is compatible with self-confidence. Separate facts from values.

## Ch 11. Are They Really So Super?
**Argument:** Kahneman (illusions can't be switched off) and Taleb (only unforecastable black swans matter) are partly right. But supers showed real resistance to scope insensitivity, black swans are often gray, and even planning for surprise requires probability judgments.

**Stories & evidence**
- **General Flynn's "unprecedented" conflict claim** contradicted datasets on declining battle deaths. The reports on his desk felt like all there was (WYSIATI).
- **Scope insensitivity:** ~$10 to clean lakes in one region or all 250,000. Regulars gave Assad's fall 40% at three months and 41% at six, while supers gave 15% and 24%.
- **Black swans:** 9/11 was war-gamed in 1998, and WWI was feared for a decade. These are gray swans. Months-long tournaments can't test skill at 0.1% events.
- **Black swans have forecastable anatomy:** the consequences of 9/11 (ultimatum, refusal, invasion) were each a scorable short-range question.
- **Lin Wells memo (April 2001):** every decade since 1900 had defied the previous one's expectations. Five months later came 9/11.
- **Planning for surprise:** Tokyo's earthquake codes make sense and Kansas's would not. Eisenhower: "plans are useless, but planning is indispensable."
- **Fat tails:** if war deaths follow a wealth-like distribution rather than a height-like one, a ten-million-death war in 1914 was thousands of times likelier than a bell curve implied.
- **Khosla vs. poker-style investing:** black-swan bets (90% fail, 10% change the world) vs. small edges (68% when others say 60%).

**So what:** Run a scope stress test (vary the deadline and threshold). Google the base rate before calling something unprecedented. Break "unforecastable" shocks into forecastable steps. State the probability behind every resilience investment.

## Ch 12. What's Next?
**Argument:** An evidence-based forecasting revolution is possible, as evidence-based medicine was. But kto-kogo ("who, whom") means forecasts often serve tribe and career more than accuracy. The way forward is scoring, question clusters, superquestioners, and adversarial collaboration.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Kto-kogo:** Lenin's view that argument is an adornment on a power struggle. Nate Silver went from prophet to stumblebum among Democrats after his 2014 Senate forecast. A Santander analyst was fired for an accurate but unwelcome forecast. Dick Morris defended his Romney-landslide call as duty, not belief.
- **Codman's End Result System:** record ailment, treatment, outcome, and publish. It got him expelled from Mass General and Harvard, and later the profession adopted it.
- **Other cases of the shift to measurement:** evidence-based policy, Gates Foundation evaluations, sports analytics. IARPA funding the tournament is the surprising proof.
- **Blame ping-pong:** miss 9/11 and over-alarm, then a WMD false alarm and under-alarm. Both block investment in accuracy.
- **Wieseltier's humanist objection:** "where wisdom once was, quantification will now be." Partly right: a hospital can hit 100% survival by refusing sick patients.
- **Korea cluster:** rocket launch, nuclear test, rebuffing talks, artillery fire, a naval incident. Each is scorable, and together they approximate "how does this end?"
- **Smack-the-forehead test:** a good question makes you say "if only I'd thought of that." Friedman's "Is Iraq the way it is because Saddam is, or vice versa?"
- **Keynesians vs. Austerians:** four years passed and no one budged. Signatories of the 2010 letter said in 2014 they'd change nothing.
- **Drezner's pledge:** "make a clear prediction and offer a confidence interval... I want to keep score."

**So what:** Cluster small scorable questions to approach big ones. Pair superquestioners with superforecasters. Settle debates with a joint list of precise bets. Brier scoring is imperfect but beats judging by titles and book sales.

## Ch 13 (Appendix). Ten Commandments
**Argument:** Eleven guidelines from the book and from training shown to raise accuracy ~10%. Each balances two opposing errors, and the last warns against treating any of them as binding.

| # | Guideline | Evidence / example used |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Triage | "Who wins in 2028?" is impossible; narrowing 2016 to plausible contenders is not |
| 2 | Break problems into sub-problems | Peter Backus's ~26 compatible women in London |
| 3 | Balance inside and outside views | Summers's rule; comparison classes even for Kony or Greece |
| 4 | Balance under- and overreaction | Updating is "dental hygiene"; usually move 0.4 → 0.35, jump on diagnostic signals |
| 5 | Seek clashing causal forces | Doves and hawks each list what would move them, then synthesize |
| 6 | As many degrees of doubt as the problem permits | Obama on March Madness vs. "fifty-fifty" on bin Laden; "slam dunk" = infinite odds |
| 7 | Balance under- and overconfidence | Score on calibration and resolution; avoid overcorrecting the last miss |
| 8 | Look for the errors behind your mistakes | Postmortem successes too; don't over-learn from a minor slip |
| 9 | Bring out the best in others | Perspective taking; Lasorda's dove (too tight kills, too loose loses) |
| 10 | Master the error-balancing bicycle | You can't learn it from a manual; practice with feedback |
| 11 | Don't treat commandments as commandments | Moltke: "two cases will never be exactly the same" |

**So what:** Treat the list as a pre-flight checklist. The book's worked example is a project estimate: use an outside-view rule (past projects ran ~2× over), then adjust, state "70% by 30 June, 90% by 31 August," and schedule the postmortem now.

## Recurring Stories to Remember
- **Bill Flack vs. Tom Friedman (ch 1, 12):** scored vs. unscored.
- **Iraq WMD "slam dunk" (ch 4, 6):** reasonable vs. correct, and the cost of false certainty.
- **Bay of Pigs → Cuban missile crisis (ch 9):** same people, different process.
- **Arafat's polonium (ch 5, 7, 8):** a case used for the outside view, for updating, and then for the postmortem.
- **Bin Laden compound (ch 6):** disagreement as data, and why "50%" throws information away.
- **Moltke and mission command (ch 10):** goals and intent, not methods.
- **Codman and Cochrane (ch 2, 12):** the medical precedent for forecasting reform.
