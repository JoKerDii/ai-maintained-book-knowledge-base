# Seeing Like a State Quiz Book
*Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed* by James C. Scott

**How to use:** Cover the answers, answer from memory (out loud or in writing), then check. Anything you miss is a section to reread. Each chapter has short-answer (SA), true/false (T/F), and multiple-choice (MC) questions. A cumulative 10-question final exam is at the end. Built from the `scott-seeing-like-a-state` skill notes, so it reflects that summary of the book, not the book's own wording.

## Introduction

**Q1 (SA).** What are the four elements of Scott's recipe for disaster?
**A:** (1) The administrative ordering of nature and society (state simplification, legibility); (2) a high-modernist ideology; (3) an authoritarian state willing and able to use the full weight of its coercive power; (4) a prostrate civil society that lacks the capacity to resist.

**Q2 (T/F).** Any one of the four elements is enough to produce a full-fledged disaster. **A:** False. All four are necessary; no subset is sufficient. Elements 1 and 2 are common and often benign. Under representative institutions, high modernism "could spur reform."

**Q3 (SA).** Complete Scott's summary: legibility supplies the ___, high modernism the ___, the authoritarian state the ___, and an incapacitated civil society the ___.
**A:** Capacity; desire; determination; terrain.

**Q4 (SA).** What did the work-to-rule strike (*grève du zèle*) demonstrate?
**A:** When workers follow every rule and job description exactly and do only listed duties, production halts. Formal rules are not a complete set of instructions. They depend on uncodifiable informal practice.

**Q5 (MC).** Scott's analogy of the "legible beehive" (queen excluder, standardized frames) shows:
a) bees are better at self-organization than humans  b) the modern hive is redesigned around the beekeeper's problem, not the bees'  c) hives should be left unmanaged  d) honey yields always fall under standardization
**A:** b.

**Q6 (SA).** How did Scott's study of people who "move around" (swidden hill peoples, nomads, pastoralists, Gypsies, vagrants, runaway serfs) turn into a theory of statecraft?
**A:** Sedentarization campaigns recur across regions and eras and almost never succeed. Reframed as attempts to make a population legible (for taxation, conscription, preventing rebellion), they became a general theory of how states simplify. The premodern state knew "precious little about its subjects" and had no synoptic view.

**Q7 (T/F).** Scott argues that markets are the antidote to state simplification. **A:** False. Large-scale capitalism "is just as much an agency of homogenization, uniformity, grids, and heroic simplification as the state." His case is "by no means a case for politically unfettered market coordination."

## Chapter 1: Nature and Space

**Q1 (SA).** What is a state simplification, and what is the "synoptic view"?
**A:** A state simplification is an abridged map that captures only the slice of reality that interests the official observer. The synoptic view is the single, at-a-glance perspective that standard units make possible, available to whoever holds the tables, maps, and registers.

**Q2 (SA).** What is a *Normalbaum*, and why does it matter?
**A:** The standardized abstract tree of a given size class, a calculating fiction, with yield tables built from it. Foresters then fit the forest to the tables (clearing underbrush, planting same-age spruce or pine in rows), so the fiction was planted into physical existence.

**Q3 (MC).** What happened in the second rotation of the scientifically managed German forests?
a) Yields doubled  b) Nutrient cycling collapsed, soils thinned, and site classes dropped, a 20-30% production loss (*Waldsterben*)  c) Nothing changed  d) The forests reverted to old-growth by themselves
**A:** b. The first rotation was a triumph partly because it was mining the soil capital of the old-growth forest it replaced.

**Q4 (SA).** What was "forest hygiene," and what does it show?
**A:** The German attempt to replace lost habitat with artificial nest boxes and schoolchildren-tended ant colonies while keeping the monoculture. It shows restoration by prosthesis: simulating an ecology while preserving the condition (uniformity) that destroyed it.

**Q5 (SA).** Why do cadastral maps and freehold tenure matter for the idea that "facts on paper reshape facts on the ground"?
**A:** A cadastral map built to identify taxable holders creates the tenure system it claims to describe. One legal owner per numbered parcel replaces plural, negotiated customary claims. The register versus who actually farms, grazes, and gleans routinely diverge.

**Q6 (T/F).** Scott says standardization and simplification are the villain. **A:** False. Some abstraction is necessary for all analysis. Uniform measures underwrote equal citizenship and market fairness, and the Third Estate demanded one weight and one measure. The danger is that state power remakes the terrain to match the map.

**Q7 (SA).** What did the door-and-window tax illustrate?
**A:** A metric does not merely observe, it eventually carves the system into its own shape: the tax produced houses with fewer windows.

## Chapter 2: Cities, People, and Language

**Q1 (SA).** What five characteristics do state facts share?
**A:** They are interested, documentary, static, aggregate, and standardized.

**Q2 (SA).** What did the Florentine *catasto* of 1427 and English tithe collection show about surnames?
**A:** State initiative created surnames rather than recording them. Surname density tracked state reach: about a third of Florentine households declared a second name, a fifth in secondary towns, a tenth in the countryside. In England, a clerk facing a population where six Christian names covered ninety percent invented second names when none was offered.

**Q3 (SA).** How did Claveria's 1849 decree work in the Philippines?
**A:** Pages of an alphabetized *catalogo* of names were distributed to each town, and no document, petition, or deed using any other name was valid. Compliance was partial (Magsaysay and Macapagal survive), but within a century the invented names were simply the names Filipinos had. His registers did not record prior names, which blinded officials' hindsight.

**Q4 (MC).** The "native-tracker test" asks:
a) Can the state count natives?  b) Would an outsider need a local guide to navigate this space?  c) Can natives track the state?  d) How many natives live there?
**A:** b. If outsiders need a guide, insiders keep a margin of political autonomy. Bruges (warren) versus Chicago (grid) is the comparison.

**Q5 (T/F).** Haussmann's rebuilding of Paris (1853-69) pursued only military control. **A:** False. It served military security, public health, commerce, and imperial display. But the poor were pushed to the periphery, producing Belleville, a new illegible insurrectionary quarter, and eventually the Commune. Zoning regulated facades, producing elegant frontages over crowded tenements.

**Q6 (SA).** Give both edges of legibility from this chapter.
**A:** It enables vaccination, poverty relief, rule of law, and early epidemic detection (the CDC). With the same capacity, the 1941 Amsterdam map of Jewish residents became possible.

**Q7 (SA).** Why did Edward I's tying of inheritance and copyhold to state-validated claims help surnames take hold in England?
**A:** Taking your father's name became part of claiming his property, and the taxed subject now wanted to be identified accurately to avoid paying twice.

## Chapter 3: Authoritarian High Modernism

**Q1 (SA).** Define high modernism in Scott's terms.
**A:** A supreme, muscle-bound confidence in linear progress, expanding production, rational design of social order, and control over nature including human nature. It is a faith that borrows science's legitimacy, "uncritical, unskeptical, and thus unscientifically optimistic," and should not be confused with scientific practice.

**Q2 (SA).** What three liberal-democratic brakes does Scott say have stopped high modernism?
**A:** (1) A believed-in private sphere the state may not enter; (2) a private sector (liberal political economy asserts the unknowability of the whole economy to the sovereign); (3) most important, working representative institutions through which a resistant society makes itself felt. He cites Amartya Sen: free press, assembly, and elections prevent famine.

**Q3 (MC).** In the lineage from Condorcet to Lenin, which event did the chapter use as proof a whole society could be administered?
a) The French Revolution  b) Walther Rathenau's planned war economy in WWI Germany  c) The Paris Commune  d) The 1929 crash
**A:** b. Lenin studied it as a prototype of a socialized economy and dropped his earlier dismissal of Taylorism as "the scientific extortion of sweat."

**Q4 (T/F).** Scott says high modernism was merely a mistake with no real benefits. **A:** False. He insists the ledger has a credit side: public health, longevity, and productivity gains. Its enemies were often entrenched interests and prejudice, and virtually every intervention had support from citizens seeking help.

**Q5 (SA).** What does "description becomes prescription" mean?
**A:** The state stops summarizing what exists and begins issuing designs for what shall exist, the moment the dashboard stops measuring the system and starts defining what it is allowed to be.

**Q6 (SA).** Why is devaluing politics structural to high modernism?
**A:** If specialists hold the unique correct technical answer, bargaining can only degrade it, so negotiation is illegitimate.

**Q7 (SA).** What are Taylorism and productivism?
**A:** Taylorism decomposes labor into isolable, repetitive motions, treating the worker as a standardized machine. Productivism is the belief that scientific increases in output can dissolve political conflict, and it is "politically promiscuous," at home in liberal, socialist, fascist, and communist programs.

## Chapter 4: The High-Modernist City: An Experiment and a Critique

**Q1 (SA).** What is the difference between visual/aesthetic order and functional order?
**A:** (Jacobs) No necessary correspondence exists between tidy geometric regularity and a system that meets daily needs. Most complex systems show no surface regularity; their order lies deeper. An army on the parade ground is visually ordered and doing nothing.

**Q2 (SA).** What were Le Corbusier's first and second principles as described here?
**A:** First, "The Plan: Dictator," where the despot is the exact plan, drawn up away from the mayor's office and the electorate. Second, "the death of the street."

**Q3 (MC).** Why was Brasília's site important to the planners?
a) It was near Rio  b) It was an empty, state-owned plateau a thousand kilometers from Rio with no property owners to negotiate with  c) It had an existing grid  d) It had a large population
**A:** b. A clean tablecloth, and because the city's only business was administration, the planning problem was already simplified.

**Q4 (SA).** What is *brasilite*, and what disappeared to cause it?
**A:** Residents' mock clinical term, "Brasília-itis," for a daily life stripped of distractions, conversations, flirtations, and small rituals. The corridor street and the praça, the venues of Brazilian public life, were designed out, and sidewalk cafes and street corners did not exist.

**Q5 (SA).** Who were the *candangos* and why do they matter?
**A:** About sixty thousand construction workers expected to build the city and quietly leave. They squatted, organized, and demanded services and title, and in a competitive political system they were heard. By 1980, 75 percent of Brasília's population lived in settlements never anticipated, while the planned city had not reached half its projected population.

**Q6 (SA).** What was Jacobs' evidence for functional order without visual order?
**A:** From a second-floor window she watched a man cajoling a young girl, and the butcher's wife, deli owner, two bar patrons, a fruit vendor, and a laundryman appeared on the pavement before any police officer was needed. "Eyes on the street" is an almost unconscious network of voluntary controls kept by nonspecialists.

**Q7 (T/F).** Jacobs defends every urban mess, including slums. **A:** False. She insists slums must change, but by unslumming themselves rather than by clearance, which destroys neighborhood networks (irreplaceable social capital).

## Chapter 5: The Revolutionary Party: A Plan and a Diagnosis

**Q1 (SA).** What is the core of Lenin's vanguard party in *What Is to Be Done?* (1903)?
**A:** The party is brain and the masses are body. Its authority rests on a master science (dialectical materialism) the working class cannot generate alone; unaided it develops only "trade-union consciousness," so socialist consciousness must come from outside. It is a small, secret, centralized cadre of professional revolutionists.

**Q2 (MC).** Which metaphor did Luxemburg use against Lenin's instrumental view of the mass strike?
a) A river bursting a dam  b) A pocket-knife kept clasped in the pocket, ready for all emergencies (which she rejected)  c) A chess game  d) A factory assembly line
**A:** b. She said a mass strike is not a pocket-knife. Scott also uses the river against the dam as his own image of her view, and Luxemburg as physician versus Lenin as engineer.

**Q3 (SA).** How did Kollontai's model differ from Lenin's treatment of unions?
**A:** She held that unions are managers and creators of the communist economy, not "transmission belts" or merely "schools of communism." Specialists serve producers, like an extension service answerable to farmers.

**Q4 (T/F).** According to the chapter, the Russian Revolution was largely brought about by the vanguard party. **A:** False. In January 1917 Lenin wrote that his generation might not live to see the decisive battles. Workers formed soviets and seized factories on their own; peasants seized land. Military collapse and Kerensky's lack of coercive resources broke the regime. Arendt: "The Bolsheviks found power lying in the street, and picked it up."

**Q5 (SA).** What did Luxemburg predict in 1918 about suppressing free institutions?
**A:** Without general elections, free press, assembly, and free struggle of opinion, life dies out in every public institution, a dozen heads do the leading, and an elite of the working class is invited to applaud.

**Q6 (SA).** When did Lenin's model become accurate, according to Scott?
**A:** After the seizure of power. The new state reconquered Russia: destroying independent soviets, imposing piecework and labor control, abrogating the right to strike, then grain requisition and eventually collectivization, at the cost of Kronstadt, Tambov, and the Makhnovshchina.

**Q7 (SA).** What pairing does Scott draw between this chapter and the previous one?
**A:** Lenin : Le Corbusier :: Luxemburg and Kollontai : Jacobs. The house is a machine for living; the party is a machine for revolution.

## Chapter 6: Soviet Collectivization, Capitalist Dreams

**Q1 (SA).** What did collectivization fail at and what did it succeed at?
**A:** It failed at the high-modernist goals it announced (yields, efficiency, "new men") but succeeded at traditional statecraft goals: appropriation and control. The state got its grain, financed industrialization, and dissolved the *mir*, the one institution capable of organized rural resistance.

**Q2 (MC).** In the "proletarian crop vs. petit-bourgeois crop" framework, which crop fits central management?
a) Raspberries  b) Wheat  c) Both  d) Neither
**A:** b. Wheat is robust, uniform, and low-skill. Raspberries are fragile, timing-critical, and high-skill, and need an attentive resident operator.

**Q3 (SA).** What was the lesson of the private plots?
**A:** Minuscule private plots supplied most of the country's fruit, vegetables, eggs, dairy, and small livestock. The plan's facts on paper were quotas; its facts on the ground were gardens. Even Stalin's state ceded these goods to private plots because coercion cannot manufacture attentiveness.

**Q4 (SA).** Why was Thomas Campbell's Montana farm and the Chicago hotel-room plan significant?
**A:** Campbell's 95,000-acre wheat operation engineered away context (hardy crops, flat virgin land) and collapsed within two years under drought and withdrawn price supports. Wilson, Ware, and Riggin planned a whole half-million-acre Soviet farm in a Chicago hotel room over two weeks; the resulting *sovkhoz*, Verblud, failed for reasons reducible to context.

**Q5 (T/F).** Total collectivization in 1929-30 was a carefully considered agrarian policy. **A:** False. It was the culmination of a grain-procurement war, as Conquest put it a chosen mechanism for extracting grain, from middle peasants who held it at one-fifth the market price.

**Q6 (SA).** Which tasks does Scott say centralized expert coordination suits?
**A:** Space programs, transport networks, flood control, aircraft manufacture, and epidemic and pollution control. His claim is that centralization is misapplied, not always wrong.

**Q7 (SA).** What does the "parade-ground test" say about pilots?
**A:** A lavishly equipped demonstration farm shows what is possible under supervision, not what scales.

## Chapter 7: Compulsory Villagization in Tanzania: Aesthetics and Miniaturization

**Q1 (SA).** What is miniaturization?
**A:** When reality resists, planners retreat into a small controlled zone (model villages, demonstration farms, new capitals) where the plan can be realized, then treat it as evidence about the whole.

**Q2 (SA).** Give the subsidy-density example from this chapter.
**A:** One Tanzanian scheme of 300 settlers absorbed four bulldozers, nine tractors, seven lorries, a generator, fifteen administrators, and 150 laborers.

**Q3 (MC).** Which best describes Nyerere's intent?
a) Extractive and cynical  b) Egalitarian, wanting schools, clinics, clean water, and a countryside that would not split into kulaks and laborers  c) Purely military  d) Purely commercial
**A:** b. He disavowed compulsion, but allowed it might be necessary to insist farmers grow a certain acreage, and the bureaucracy widened that seam.

**Q4 (SA).** How did officials handle the villagization campaign after the December 1973 order?
**A:** Regions competed (Mara reportedly nearly finished); where persuasion failed, famine relief was made conditional on moving, militia supplied transport, and houses were burned to prevent return. Food imports were needed from 1973 to 1975, 1.2 billion shillings, enough to buy a cow for every family.

**Q5 (SA).** What was the Ruvuma Development Association and what happened to it?
**A:** It actually practiced Nyerere's stated ideals (spontaneous, local, cooperative). It was banned in 1968 and its assets seized for refusing absorption into party routine.

**Q6 (T/F).** Scott says Tanzania was as destructive as Stalinist collectivization. **A:** False. Tanzania's weakness, unwillingness to use Stalinist methods, and peasants' options of flight, smuggling, and foot-dragging made practice far less destructive than theory. Ethiopia under the Dergue ran the same template with military force.

**Q7 (SA).** What does the Parisian taxi driver example show?
**A:** Obeying every regulation would halt central Paris, so traffic flows only because drivers master practices outside the code. If following your rules exactly would break the system, the rules are not the system.

## Chapter 8: Taming Nature: An Agriculture of Legibility and Simplicity

**Q1 (SA).** What are Scott's four systemic failures of high-modernist agriculture?
**A:** (1) Inherited unexamined assumptions from the temperate industrializing West; (2) institutional interest (the more the cultivator knows, the less the specialist matters); (3) cyclopean shortsightedness (productionist focus hides long-term outcomes and externalities); (4) incapacity for complexity (isolating one variable leaves the method unable to assess many-variable practices). Failures 3 and 4 are structural and would occur under the best administration.

**Q2 (SA).** Why is the experimental plot itself a simplification?
**A:** Its controls are a construction: weather, soil, landscape, and implicit assumptions about farm size, labor, and what the cultivator wants are held constant. Researchers called pests and bad weather "interference," yet coping with exactly that is the farmer's main concern.

**Q3 (MC).** The 1970 corn blight illustrates:
a) the benefits of monocropping  b) that 71 percent of US corn acreage in six hybrids preceded it; an exotic Mexican landrace supplied the escape  c) polyculture failure  d) a DDT problem
**A:** b. The pathogen was old; the vulnerability was new.

**Q4 (SA).** What did Edgar Anderson find in the Guatemalan garden at Santa Lucia?
**A:** What looked like an overgrown dump heap was, once mapped, a system of crosswise rows of fruit trees, cacti, coffee, medicinal herbs, maize, banana, cucurbits, and more, at once vegetable garden, orchard, medicinal garden, compost heap, and beeyard. It resisted erosion, held humidity, isolated same-kind plants, maintained fertility, and produced continuously with little effort.

**Q5 (SA).** Why couldn't the agronomist see that order?
**A:** Beyond prejudice and institutional interest, complex intercropping is unprovable by the available method because too many variables are in play for unambiguous causal proof. Polyculture resists proof, not necessarily function.

**Q6 (T/F).** Scott argues that scientific agronomy is worthless. **A:** False. Modern breeding, pathology, and soil science created knowledge even traditional cultivators use. Local knowledge also has hard limits. The claim is narrower: the method's power is bought by exclusions.

**Q7 (SA).** What did the DDT case show?
**A:** The model asked what dose kills mosquitoes and succeeded in its frame (malaria fell). It was blind to residues moving up the food chain; the alarm came from outside the paradigm, leading to eggshell thinning and a ban.

## Chapter 9: Thin Simplifications and Practical Knowledge: Mētis

**Q1 (SA).** Define mētis and name the three conditions under which it is most valuable.
**A:** Practical, local, experiential intelligence acquired by doing, Odysseus's quality. Most valuable where situations are mutable, indeterminate, and particular.

**Q2 (SA).** Contrast mētis with technē/epistēmē on scope, test, and stance.
**A:** Mētis is contextual and local; technē/epistēmē is universal and context-free. Mētis is tested by "did it work?"; technē by "is it explained and verified?" Mētis is partisan (cares about this outcome); technē is impersonal, quantitative, and probabilistic.

**Q3 (MC).** The best summary of the Squanto maxim ("plant corn when the oak leaves are the size of a squirrel's ear") is:
a) pure folklore  b) a compressed reading of a natural succession whose sequence rarely varies, so a near-foolproof frost-avoidance rule that travels  c) a scientific formula  d) an almanac rule
**A:** b. Compare the almanac's fixed-date advice (needs correcting for latitude and altitude, fail-safe for the publisher) and Quetelet's lilac formula ("hardly practical").

**Q4 (SA).** What does work-to-rule prove, and what happened in the Caterpillar action?
**A:** Actual work processes depend more on informal understandings and improvisations than on formal rules. In the Caterpillar action, workers reverted to the engineers' specified procedures, knowing the officially correct method would cost time and quality.

**Q5 (SA).** What is "locally superior" knowledge?
**A:** The harbor pilot outranks the ship's captain inside the harbor; situated knowledge of this port, these sandbars and microcurrents, "must prevail" over the general rules of navigation.

**Q6 (SA).** How did East German factories rely on mētis?
**A:** Two employees absent from the org chart kept them running: the jack-of-all-trades who improvised repairs, and the wheeler-dealer who crossed the country in a Trabant to barter for a missing valve. "A key element in the centrally planned economy was underwritten, always unofficially, by mētis."

**Q7 (T/F).** Scott romanticizes mētis. **A:** False. Its innovations are recombinations (bricolage), its transmission may favor conservation of skills over daring innovation, and it coexists with domination, monopoly, and exclusion. His objection is to replacing the collaboration between mētis and technē with an imperial scientific view.

## Chapter 10: Conclusion

**Q1 (SA).** State Scott's four rules of thumb for less disaster-prone planning.
**A:** (1) Take small steps; (2) favor reversibility; (3) plan on surprises; (4) plan on human inventiveness.

**Q2 (SA).** Explain the two maps test.
**A:** Map 1 shows the routes the planners provided. Map 2 is a time-lapse of all unplanned movement and activity. Map 2 is far more complex and in older neighborhoods nearly supersedes Map 1. "Planned Brasília is, in a thousand ways, underwritten by unplanned Brasília."

**Q3 (MC).** The mētis-friendly institution litmus test asks:
a) Is it cheap?  b) To what degree does it enhance the skills, knowledge, and responsibility of those who are part of it?  c) Is it centralized?  d) Is it popular?
**A:** b. It distinguishes "canned" forms from those open to modification.

**Q4 (SA).** What contrast do the two war memorials draw?
**A:** Maya Lin's Vietnam Memorial (low black wall, names in chronological order) virtually requires participation (rubbings, mementos) to complete its meaning. The Iwo Jima statue is symbolically self-sufficient, so visitors receive its message rather than complete it. The first renews mētis ("use it or lose it"); the second is "canned."

**Q5 (SA).** What is the "abstract citizen"?
**A:** The standardized, interchangeable planning subject with "no gender, no tastes, no history, no values, no opinions or original ideas, no traditions," the necessary first premise of any large-scale planning exercise.

**Q6 (SA).** What is the self-fulfilling logic of social engineering?
**A:** Narrow, controlled environments foster a less skilled, less innovative, less resourceful population (institutional neurosis), which then genuinely does need close supervision. The plan produces the subjects it assumed.

**Q7 (T/F).** Scott says fixed routines are never efficient. **A:** False. They are legitimately efficient where the task environment is repetitive, stable, and predictable, and he does not deny the benefits of division of labor or hierarchical coordination for some tasks. But in most human affairs that is seldom the case.

## Cumulative Final Exam (10 questions)

**F1 (SA).** Name the four elements of the recipe for disaster and say which two are the true danger signals.
**A:** Administrative ordering (legibility), high-modernist ideology, an authoritarian state, a prostrate civil society. Elements 3 and 4, where nobody can say no, are the danger signals.

**F2 (T/F).** Scott is anti-state, anti-planning, and anti-science. **A:** False. The state is "the vexed institution that is the ground of both our freedoms and our unfreedoms." Legibility has credited uses (public health, taxation fairness, rule of law, poverty relief, citizenship). His target is "an imperial or hegemonic planning mentality that excludes the necessary role of local knowledge and know-how."

**F3 (SA).** Walk through how scientific forestry moved from a map to a remade territory and what went wrong.
**A:** Foresters narrowed vision to commercial wood, derived the *Normalbaum* and yield tables, then fit the forest to the tables (single-species, same-age, straight rows). The first rotation triumphed; the second collapsed by 20-30% (*Waldsterben*), and "forest hygiene" tried to simulate lost habitat.

**F4 (MC).** Which pair of cases best shows miniaturization at work?
a) Haussmann's Paris and the kolkhoz  b) Tanzanian model villages and Brasília/the model farm showcase  c) Lenin and Luxemburg  d) The Squanto maxim and the almanac
**A:** b. Subsidized showcase zones were read as proof the design would scale (see also the parade-ground test in Ch 6).

**F5 (SA).** How do Chapters 4 and 5 parallel each other?
**A:** Lenin : Le Corbusier :: Luxemburg and Kollontai : Jacobs. Each plan entrusts a living process to a cadre with a master science; the critics argue from process, practice, and ground-level experience.

**F6 (SA).** In what sense did collectivization "succeed" and "fail"?
**A:** It failed at yields and efficiency (yields stagnated or fell below 1920s levels for half a century) but succeeded at appropriation and control (grain, industrial financing, dissolving the *mir*). The informal private plots supplied most fruit, vegetables, eggs, and dairy.

**F7 (SA).** What do work-to-rule, the harbor pilot, and the East German factories have in common?
**A:** Each shows formal order depends on informal practice (mētis) it neither recognizes nor can create. Formal order is "parasitic on informal processes."

**F8 (T/F).** Good intentions protect a development scheme from the generic failure. **A:** False. Tanzania's villagization, under an egalitarian leader and a weak, well-intentioned state, still failed ecologically and economically. The generic developmentalist logic produces generic failure regardless.

**F9 (SA).** What is thin simplification and why does thinness reduce resilience?
**A:** A schematic model too impoverished to serve as instructions for a functioning order. "The more schematic, thin, and simplified the formal order, the less resilient and the more vulnerable it is to disturbances outside its narrow parameters." Monocrops and uniform hybrids are examples.

**F10 (SA).** Apply the four rules of thumb and the two maps test to a hypothetical rollout of a nationwide, simultaneous, irreversible reform. What would Scott advise?
**A:** Take a small step, observe, then plan the next; favor an intervention that can be undone ("keep all the parts"); design in slack for surprises; assume later participants will improve on the design. Draw Map 2 of the unplanned activity the design silently depends on, and keep a channel open to the people whose knowledge was excluded. (The notes flag nationwide, simultaneous, irreversible rollout as a sign scope has defeated foresight.)
