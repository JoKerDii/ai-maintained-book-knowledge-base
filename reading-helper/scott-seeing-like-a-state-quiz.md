# Seeing Like a State - Quiz Book

*James C. Scott, "Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed"*

> **Source note.** This quiz reflects the chapter notes in the "scott-seeing-like-a-state" skill (SKILL.md, glossary, patterns, cheatsheet and the eleven chapter files), **not the book's own wording**. Quoted phrases are those the notes attribute to Scott or others. Where the notes are silent, nothing has been added. The transfers to software, org design, metrics and platform policy are the skill's analogical extensions, not claims Scott makes; scenario questions use them as practice only.

## How to use this quiz book

1. **Cover the answers.** Each question is followed by an **Answer** block. Say or write your answer first.
2. **Question types.** *SA* = short answer; *T/F* = true/false (also say why); *MC* = multiple choice; *EXPLAIN* = explain in your own words (3-4 sentences); *SCENARIO* = apply a concept to a situation.
3. **Score yourself.** 1 point for a correct answer, half a point if you got the idea but missed the term or number. Aim for 80 percent or more per section before moving on.
4. **Order.** Sections follow the book: Introduction, then Part 1 (ch1-2), Part 2 (ch3-5), Part 3 (ch6-8), Part 4 (ch9-10). A 20-question cumulative final exam closes the file (110 section questions + 20 = 130 total).
5. **Rework misses** in the companion file `scott-seeing-like-a-state-arguments-and-evidence.md`.

## Section 0 - Introduction

**Q0.1 (SA).** Name the four elements of Scott's recipe for disaster.

**Answer.** (1) The administrative ordering of nature and society (state simplification, legibility); (2) a high-modernist ideology; (3) an authoritarian state willing and able to use its full coercive power; (4) a prostrate civil society that cannot resist. This is the book's master screening framework, stated in the Introduction and formalized in ch3.

**Q0.2 (T/F).** "Any one of the four elements is enough to produce a full-fledged disaster."

**Answer.** **False.** All four are necessary; no subset is sufficient. Elements 1 and 2 are common and often benign: under representative institutions high modernism "could spur reform."

**Q0.3 (EXPLAIN).** Scott summarizes what each element supplies. Explain in your own words.

**Answer.** Legibility supplies the capacity (the state can see and act), high modernism the desire (a vision to impose), the authoritarian state the determination, and an incapacitated civil society the terrain. The lethal ingredients are 3 and 4: **nobody can say no**.

**Q0.4 (SA).** Define legibility and give three of Scott's examples.

**Answer.** Rendering exceptionally complex, illegible, local practices into a standard grid that can be centrally recorded, counted and monitored. Examples: permanent surnames, standardized weights and measures, cadastral surveys, population registers, freehold tenure, standardized language and legal discourse, city design, transport layout.

**Q0.5 (EXPLAIN).** Why is a state's map "not merely descriptive"? Use the cadastral example.

**Answer.** Allied with state power, such maps "would enable much of the reality they depicted to be remade." A cadastral map built to identify taxable holders creates the tenure system it claims to describe, because the categories get the force of law: facts on paper reshape facts on the ground.

**Q0.6 (SA).** What is a work-to-rule strike (*greve du zele*), and what does it prove?

**Answer.** Workers observe every rule and job description exactly and perform only listed duties; production halts. It proves formal rules are inadequate as a complete set of instructions and depend on uncodifiable informal practice.

**Q0.7 (SA).** What is the "legible beehive"?

**Answer.** The modern hive with its queen excluder and standardized frames, redesigned around the beekeeper's problem (seeing and harvesting) not the bees'. A homely analogy for a system reshaped to suit the observer.

**Q0.8 (T/F).** Scott argues that market coordination is the antidote to state simplification.

**Answer.** **False.** Large-scale capitalism "is just as much an agency of homogenization, uniformity, grids, and heroic simplification as the state." For capitalists simplification must pay, and markets reduce quality to quantity through price.

**Q0.9 (SCENARIO).** A national agency rolls out a single standardized registry. Planners are enthusiastic, but citizens have a working legislature, a free press and local organizations that can lobby. How worried should you be, using the four-element test?

**Answer.** Moderately, not lethally. Elements 1 and 2 are present but 3 and 4 are weak: citizens can negotiate, publicize and resist. Risk rises only if coercive power and a silenced constituency appear.

**Q0.10 (SCENARIO).** A manager says: "The documented onboarding process is the onboarding process." Which Introduction idea tests this?

**Answer.** Work-to-rule as proof. Ask what happens if everyone follows every documented rule exactly and does nothing else. If work halts, the documentation is not the system, and any plan derived from it is thin.

## Section 1 - Chapter 1: Nature and Space

**Q1.1 (SA).** What was the *Normalbaum*?

**Answer.** The "standard tree" of German scientific forestry: the saleable wood volume of a standard tree of each size class, used to build yield tables. It began as a calculating fiction and was later planted into physical existence.

**Q1.2 (EXPLAIN).** How did Prussian and Saxon foresters (c. 1765-1800) move from counting trees to remaking the forest?

**Answer.** Beckmann's crews tagged trees on a sample plot with color-coded nails by size class; leftover nails gave an inventory and scaling gave whole-forest yield. Mathematicians derived the Normalbaum and yield tables, yielding a synoptic view from the office. Then they fitted the forest to the tables: underbrush and deadfall cleared, one species (Norway spruce or Scotch pine) planted same-age in straight rows.

**Q1.3 (SA).** What happened in the second rotation, and what numbers do the notes give?

**Answer.** The first eighty-year rotation was a triumph; the second collapsed. Nutrient cycling failed, soils thinned, storms and species-specific pests hit uniform stands, and site classes dropped one or two grades: a **20-30 percent** production loss. *Waldsterben* entered the language.

**Q1.4 (EXPLAIN).** Why should the first rotation's success not be read as validation?

**Answer.** It was mining accumulated soil capital from the old-growth forest it replaced. Judge simplified systems on their second rotation, not their first.

**Q1.5 (MC).** What was "forest hygiene"? A) Pruning diseased trees; B) Nest boxes, farmed ant colonies, reintroduced spiders; C) Burning underbrush; D) Planting mixed species.

**Answer.** **B.** Hollow trees replaced with nest boxes and schoolchildren tending transplanted ant colonies: simulating a habitat while keeping the monoculture that destroyed it. An admission that the cleared "debris" was the system.

**Q1.6 (SA).** What is "fiscal forestry," and why is it a parable?

**Answer.** Resolving a domain into a single number (annual revenue yield of extractable timber) and managing to it. Diagnostic: ask what single metric your system has collapsed reality into and list what fell outside the frame (peasants' fodder, fruit, medicine, resin, grazing and fuel).

**Q1.7 (T/F).** The door-and-window tax shows a metric shaping the thing it measures.

**Answer.** **True.** It produced houses with fewer windows. A metric is a lens with a razor edge that eventually carves the system into its shape.

**Q1.8 (EXPLAIN).** Explain "facts on paper vs. facts on the ground" with the cadastre.

**Answer.** The register (one legal owner per numbered parcel, graded by soil class and presumed yield) versus who actually farms, grazes and gleans. Once the register has legal force the ground is pressed to match it; the shadow system beneath is where compliance and resistance live.

**Q1.9 (SCENARIO).** A company replaces a rich quality rubric with one score per support agent, then reorganizes workflows to raise it. Scores rise for two quarters, then satisfaction craters. Which concepts apply?

**Answer.** Fiscal forestry/thin simplification (one number for a many-purpose activity), the metric reshaping the terrain (door-and-window tax), and first-rotation vs. second-rotation: early gains may draw down reserves (goodwill, agent judgment). Write down what falls outside the frame and preserve redundancy.

**Q1.10 (SCENARIO).** Leadership wants to make Normalbaum-like standard units for every project. Does Scott say standardization is simply bad?

**Answer.** No. Uniform measures underwrote equal citizenship, market fairness and equitable taxation; the Third Estate *demanded* one weight and measure. Use standardization for comparability at scale, but expect the new legible code to create an intermediary class fluent in it and shift power toward them, and list what falls outside.

## Section 2 - Chapter 2: Cities, People, and Language

**Q2.1 (SA).** List the five characteristics of state simplifications.

**Answer.** Interested, documentary, static, aggregate, standardized. Use as an audit checklist: whose interest defined the fact, does it exist only on paper, does it freeze a process, does it average away variation, what distinctions were collapsed?

**Q2.2 (EXPLAIN).** What incentive does Porter identify?

**Answer.** Institutions prefer *precise and standardizable* measures over *accurate* ones, because accuracy that cannot be reproduced elsewhere is useless to them.

**Q2.3 (SA).** What is the native-tracker test and what does it show about Bruges and Chicago?

**Answer.** Would an outsider need a local guide? Unplanned Bruges needs one, so the community keeps political autonomy; Chicago's grid does not. Three grid consequences: order visible only from above; formal order guarantees nothing about lived experience; uniform lots become a currency-like commodity ideal for surveyors, planners and speculators.

**Q2.4 (EXPLAIN).** What did Haussmannization (1853-69) do, and what was its evil twin?

**Answer.** Demolition, boulevards, sewers and rail for military security, public health, commerce and imperial display (the patterns file adds troop movement and suppression of barricades). Zoning regulated visible surfaces, so builders raised elegant frontages over crowded tenements. The spacious center pushed the poor to the periphery, producing Belleville, a new illegible insurrectionary quarter, and eventually the Commune.

**Q2.5 (SA).** What did Florence's 1427 *catasto* reveal?

**Answer.** That state initiative *created* surnames rather than recording them, and surname density tracked state reach: a third of Florentine households declared a second name, a fifth in secondary towns, a tenth in the countryside, with the poorest districts holding out until the seventeenth century. The census collapsed under cost and resistance.

**Q2.6 (EXPLAIN).** How did invented English surnames become real?

**Answer.** A tithe collector facing a male population where six Christian names covered ninety percent invented a second name when none was offered. They became real through incentive: Edward I tied inheritance and copyhold to state-validated claims, so taking your father's name became part of claiming his property, and taxed subjects wanted accurate identification to avoid paying twice. Naming meant taxes, so it drew revolt: the 1381 rising followed a decade of poll-tax registrations.

**Q2.7 (SA).** What did Claveria's 1849 decree do and what mistake did it embed?

**Answer.** Distributed pages of an alphabetized *catalogo* to each town; no document using any other name was valid. Compliance was partial (Magsaysay and Macapagal survive), yet within a century the invented names were simply Filipinos' names. His registers failed to record prior names, so officials could no longer trace property and tax history across the reform.

**Q2.8 (T/F).** The 1941 Amsterdam map shows legibility only ever serves benevolent ends.

**Answer.** **False.** Legibility is two-edged: the same capacity enables vaccination, poverty relief and rule of law and also enabled the map of Jewish residents. The tool does not choose its purpose.

**Q2.9 (SCENARIO).** A platform introduces mandatory real-name verification to cut abuse. Which Chapter 2 concept applies?

**Answer.** A surname campaign. It delivers accountability and abuse reduction and simultaneously strips anonymity that protects dissidents. Expect it to be reused beyond its stated purpose and to advantage those fluent in it.

**Q2.10 (SCENARIO).** A company migrates to new employee IDs and discards the old ones. Two years later pay history cannot be traced. Which story, and what rule?

**Answer.** Claveria's registers without prior names. When you migrate identifiers, retain the mapping to the old one, or you blind your own hindsight.

## Section 3 - Chapter 3: Authoritarian High Modernism

**Q3.1 (SA).** Define high modernism in the notes' terms.

**Answer.** A supreme, muscle-bound confidence in linear progress, expanding production, rational design of social order and control over nature including human nature. A faith that borrows science's legitimacy: "uncritical, unskeptical, and thus unscientifically optimistic."

**Q3.2 (T/F).** Scott says high modernism is inherently destructive.

**Answer.** **False.** It becomes catastrophic only when fused with a coercive state and a prostrate civil society. The first two elements alone are not dangerous.

**Q3.3 (SA).** Name the three liberal-democratic brakes.

**Answer.** (1) A believed-in private sphere the state may not enter; (2) a private sector (Foucault: the whole economy is unknowable to the sovereign); (3) most important, working representative institutions. Sen: free press, assembly and elections prevent famine because rulers who never go hungry otherwise have no incentive to learn of it.

**Q3.4 (EXPLAIN).** What does "description becomes prescription" mean?

**Answer.** The state stops summarizing what exists and begins issuing designs for what shall exist; a dashboard stops measuring the system and starts defining what it may be. The 19th-century "discovery of society" (statistics on fertility, crime, literacy) made society an object that could be engineered.

**Q3.5 (SA).** What is Taylorism and who embraced it?

**Answer.** Decomposing labor into isolable repetitive motions, treating the worker as a standardized machine. Embraced across the political spectrum, including by Lenin, who dropped his earlier dismissal of it as "the scientific extortion of sweat."

**Q3.6 (EXPLAIN).** What is productivism, and what is social taxidermy?

**Answer.** Productivism: belief that scientific increases in output can dissolve political conflict; "politically promiscuous," at home in liberal, socialist, fascist and communist programs. Social taxidermy: freezing a living, plastic social order into fixed designated positions.

**Q3.7 (EXPLAIN).** Trace the Rathenau-Lenin episode.

**Answer.** In WWI Walther Rathenau (industrial engineer, head of A.E.G.) invented a planned economy step by step at the Office of War Raw Materials and kept Germany supplied far past what observers predicted. Lenin studied it, called it the prototype of a socialized economy and demanded Russia adapt it: a command economy at the macro level plus Taylorist coordination on the factory floor.

**Q3.8 (EXPLAIN).** What is the "who were the enemies?" test?

**Answer.** An unsympathetic account is grossly unfair without it: 19th-century public-health engineers, urban reformers and inventors were blocked by real-estate interests, entrenched politics and prejudice, and virtually every intervention had the support of citizens seeking help. We are all beneficiaries (public health, longevity, productivity).

**Q3.9 (SCENARIO).** A planning office announces a reorg; staff have no way to object, and the justification is "analysis shows one correct structure." Which concepts apply?

**Answer.** The four-element recipe at small scale (unilateral power, silent constituency) and the anti-pattern of banishing politics: a claim of a unique technical answer makes negotiation illegitimate. Build a channel for the affected before you need it.

**Q3.10 (SCENARIO).** A leader is certain a program's payoff will arrive in five years and accepts heavy costs now. What does Scott's future-discount check ask?

**Answer.** Whether the plan discounts the future honestly. High modernism privileges the future so heavily it stops discounting for uncertainty, which licenses present sacrifice; the more heroic the forecast, the more of someone else's life it is spending.

## Section 4 - Chapter 4: The High-Modernist City

**Q4.1 (SA).** What are Le Corbusier's first two principles as the notes present them?

**Answer.** "The Plan: Dictator" (the exact plan, drawn away from the mayor's office and the electorate, ignoring existing usages) and "the death of the street" (functional segregation of work, residence, commerce and traffic).

**Q4.2 (EXPLAIN).** Explain Jacobs' visual vs. functional order with the army image.

**Answer.** No necessary correspondence exists between tidy geometric regularity and a system that meets daily needs. An army on the parade ground is visually ordered and doing nothing. Planners inferred functional order from visual order, driving them to single-use zoning, since mixed use looks like confusion.

**Q4.3 (SA).** Why does Scott say single-purpose zoning was chosen?

**Answer.** Because it makes planning tractable (easier to plan a zone with one purpose, as easier to plan a forest yielding only timber), not because it makes the result better. Cross-use carries invisible load: safety, commerce and trust were byproducts of mixing.

**Q4.4 (SA).** Describe Brasilia's origins and real gains.

**Answer.** Kubitschek promised "fifty years of progress in five"; Niemeyer ran a competition won by Lucio Costa on rough sketches; the site was empty state-owned plateau a thousand kilometers from Rio, a clean tablecloth. Real gains: 25 square meters of green space per resident, uniform housing, a nursery and school in every superquadra (roughly 1,500-2,500 residents).

**Q4.5 (EXPLAIN).** What did the plan subtract, and what is "brasilite"?

**Answer.** The corridor street and the praca; sidewalk cafe, street corner, neighborhood square. The Plaza of the Three Powers is so vast it dwarfs a military parade. Brasilite is residents' mock clinical term for life stripped of distractions, conversations, flirtations and small rituals. Pedestrian jams, the point of social conviviality, went with the traffic jams.

**Q4.6 (SA).** What did Holston find when asking nine-year-olds in superquadras to draw "home"?

**Answer.** Not one drew an apartment (facades were identical, with not even balconies). Holston's irony: macro-order, micro-confusion, as practical knowledge of the city falls while systematic rationality rises.

**Q4.7 (EXPLAIN).** Who were the candangos and why do they matter?

**Answer.** About 60,000 construction workers expected to build and leave. Kubitschek had made them rhetorical proletarian heroes; they squatted, organized and demanded services and title, and in a competitive political system no politician could ignore a voting bloc. By 1980, **75 percent** lived in unanticipated settlements while the planned city had not reached **half** its projected population. The two Brasilias were symbiotic.

**Q4.8 (SA).** What does Jacobs see from her second-floor window?

**Answer.** A man cajoling a young girl; the butcher's wife, deli owner, two bar patrons, a fruit vendor and a laundryman appear on the pavement before any police officer is needed: eyes on the street, functional order with no visual order, produced by no plan.

**Q4.9 (SCENARIO).** An architecture review proposes strict single-purpose service boundaries because they are easy to draw. What does Chapter 4 say to check?

**Answer.** Ask what the separation is *for* and whether prohibited cross-cutting paths were carrying real traffic (the notes' transfer). Single-use zoning serves planner tractability. Apply the kitchen test: versatility draws people.

**Q4.10 (SCENARIO).** A pilot neighborhood for a city policy is beautifully ordered but residents quietly build workarounds on the edges. Which ideas apply?

**Answer.** Facts on paper vs. facts on the ground, and the unplanned periphery doing load-bearing work (candangos); the same pattern recurred at Chandigarh (chowks replacing bazaars, hawkers banned, another unplanned city at the margins). Plan for the unplanned, in a system open enough for those people to be heard.

## Section 5 - Chapter 5: The Revolutionary Party

**Q5.1 (SA).** In *What Is to Be Done?* (1903), what is the vanguard party and why is it needed?

**Answer.** A small, secret, centralized cadre of professional revolutionists; the party is brain, the masses body. Unaided, workers develop "only trade-union consciousness," so the master science (dialectical materialism) must come from outside.

**Q5.2 (EXPLAIN).** Name Lenin's four organizing metaphors and what they smuggle in.

**Answer.** General staff (only the center sees the battlefield), classroom (the party must never be "degraded to the level of the labor masses"), factory ("specialization necessarily presupposes centralization"), bricklayer needing a guiding line. Each answers in advance whose knowledge counts.

**Q5.3 (SA).** What does the notes' analysis say is wrong with "the masses"?

**Answer.** It conveys quantity without order, history or ideas, erasing differences in political experience, organizational skill, ideology, religion, ethnicity and language, the differences that determine outcomes.

**Q5.4 (SA).** How did *State and Revolution* change the tone, and what stayed the same?

**Answer.** The tone turned egalitarian and nearly anarchist ("the creation of the popular masses themselves"), but capitalism has built a technostructure any literate person could run and machine industry "calls for absolute and strict unity of will." The revolution changes who stands on the ocean liner's bridge; the crew's jobs are unchanged.

**Q5.5 (EXPLAIN).** Describe Luxemburg's stance and metaphors.

**Answer.** Physician, not engineer: the proletariat has its own constitution; a mass strike is a living process, not a pocket-knife to be decided at pleasure. Her metaphors move from military and factory to growth and learning; a real movement's errors are "infinitely more fruitful" than the best central committee's infallibility. Dissection kills.

**Q5.6 (SA).** What did Luxemburg predict in 1918?

**Answer.** Without general elections, free press, assembly and free struggle of opinion, life dies out in every public institution, a dozen heads do the leading, and an elite of the working class is invited to applaud.

**Q5.7 (EXPLAIN).** The notes say the Russian Revolution was not to any significant degree brought about by the vanguard party. What evidence?

**Answer.** In January 1917 Lenin wrote his generation might not see the decisive battles. Workers formed soviets and seized factories on their own; peasants seized land, most never having heard of the Bolsheviks. What broke the regime was military collapse in Austria and Kerensky's want of coercive resources. Arendt: "The Bolsheviks found power lying in the street, and picked it up." Carr: Bolshevism succeeded to an empty throne.

**Q5.8 (SA).** State Kollontai's disagreement with Lenin, Trotsky and Bukharin, and what happened to the Workers' Opposition.

**Answer.** The Workers' Opposition sees in the unions the managers and creators of the communist economy; the leadership leaves them only the role of schools of communism. She held communism cannot be decreed. The Workers' Opposition was outlawed and she was silenced.

**Q5.9 (SCENARIO).** A strategy team holds "the totality" while delivery teams execute assigned tasks and report one-way. Which model, and what alternative?

**Answer.** The vanguard party in miniature (transmission belt, broadcast-only). Alternative: Kollontai's extension-service model, with technical centers near production, producers setting the agenda, and officials without floor experience required to acquire some.

**Q5.10 (SCENARIO).** You must choose between command-and-control and plural experiments. What first question does Chapter 5 give?

**Answer.** "What kind of task is this?" Producing axles admits factory logic; making a revolution or inventing a mode of production is uncharted water, approachable only by successive approximation and experience.

## Section 6 - Chapter 6: Soviet Collectivization, Capitalist Dreams

**Q6.1 (EXPLAIN).** What did collectivization actually achieve?

**Answer.** It failed at nearly every high-modernist goal (yields, efficiency, "new men") but succeeded at the older aims of statecraft: **appropriation and control**. The countryside was made visible and commandable.

**Q6.2 (SA).** Describe Thomas Campbell's Montana farm and its fate.

**Answer.** A ninety-five-thousand-acre wheat operation with Morgan money and federal subsidy, built on hardy crops and flat virgin land. He engineered away context and declared farming ninety percent engineering; drought and withdrawn price supports collapsed it in two years, and decades on it showed no advantage over family farms.

**Q6.3 (EXPLAIN).** Why is the Chicago hotel-room episode a lesson?

**Answer.** Wilson, Ware and Riggin planned an entire half-million-acre Soviet farm (layout, machinery, labor, schedule) in two weeks in a Chicago hotel room. That they thought it possible is the point. The sovkhoz, Verblud, produced wheat briefly and failed for one reason: context.

**Q6.4 (SA).** Explain proletarian vs. petit-bourgeois crops.

**Answer.** Wheat is robust, uniform and low-skill, so it survives central management; raspberries are fragile, timing-critical and high-skill, needing an attentive resident operator. Even Stalin ceded fruit, vegetables, dairy and eggs to private plots because coercion cannot manufacture attentiveness.

**Q6.5 (SA).** Summarize the procurement war and the 1929-30 decision.

**Answer.** Armed requisition provoked over 150 uprisings and ended in the NEP retreat. For forced-draft industrialization Stalin needed grain held by middle peasants at one-fifth the market price. Total collectivization in 1929-30 was the culmination of that war, not a considered agrarian policy; the kolkhoz was a chosen mechanism for extracting grain (Conquest).

**Q6.6 (SA).** Give the violence and famine figures.

**Answer.** 25,000 urban plenipotentiaries; over half the livestock slaughtered; the gulag enlarged; the 1933-34 famine with roughly 63 percent of the 1931 harvest confiscated; death estimates from three or four million past twenty.

**Q6.7 (EXPLAIN).** What does the private plot show?

**Answer.** Minuscule private plots supplied most of the country's fruit, vegetables, eggs, dairy and small livestock. The plan's facts on paper were quotas; its facts on the ground were gardens. The system survived on improvisation it never legitimized.

**Q6.8 (T/F).** Scott attributes the production record to high modernism alone.

**Answer.** **False.** It was overdetermined by Lysenko, war, weather and Stalin's obsessions. He grants centralized expert coordination is right for space programs, transport networks, flood control, aircraft manufacture and epidemic and pollution control.

**Q6.9 (SCENARIO).** A firm centralizes every regional process; the efficiency case is thin yet the project proceeds. Which lens?

**Answer.** "What does this make visible?" (appropriation lens): colonial powers favored plantations over more efficient smallholders because plantations were easier to tax and supervise. List the stated goal, what the center must see, the units created and who absorbs the mismatch.

**Q6.10 (SCENARIO).** Centralize or devolve an activity that is fragile, timing-critical and judgment-heavy?

**Answer.** Devolve it to operators who live with the outcome (raspberries); centralize robust, uniform, slack-tolerant work (wheat). Scott argues centralization is misapplied, not always wrong.

## Section 7 - Chapter 7: Compulsory Villagization in Tanzania

**Q7.1 (SA).** What was the scale and nature of the campaign?

**Answer.** Nyerere's ujamaa campaign (Operation Planned Villages after the December 1973 order) moved about five million people into gridded roadside villages under a weak, well-intentioned state, and failed ecologically and economically.

**Q7.2 (EXPLAIN).** What is miniaturization and its failure mode?

**Answer.** Retreat into a small controlled zone (model village, demonstration farm, new capital) where the plan can be realized, then citing it as evidence for the whole. A wind tunnel is a legitimate miniature; the failure is mistaking the parade ground for the battlefield.

**Q7.3 (SA).** Give the subsidy-density example.

**Answer.** One scheme of 300 settlers absorbed four bulldozers, nine tractors, seven lorries, a generator, fifteen administrators and 150 laborers, and counted as successful only if you ignored its inefficiency and irrelevance to Tanzanian conditions.

**Q7.4 (EXPLAIN).** What was Nyerere's intent and where was the "seam"?

**Answer.** Not extractive: schools, clinics, water, electrification, and no sorting into kulaks and laborers. He disavowed compulsion yet allowed it might be necessary to insist farmers grow a certain acreage until they realized it served them. That concession was the seam the bureaucracy widened.

**Q7.5 (SA).** What did the Malawi and groundnut schemes show?

**Answer.** Malawi's lower Shire Valley scheme (rectangular bunds, a 3D architect's model, a "miniature TVA") failed almost completely; ridging was unstable on sandy soil and dried the ground so white ants attacked roots. The Tanganyika groundnut scheme (three million acres, nine weeks of mostly aerial reconnaissance) was abandoned by 1950 having grown fewer nuts than it sowed.

**Q7.6 (SA).** How was compliance enforced, and what were the results?

**Answer.** Regional competition (Mara), famine relief conditional on moving, militia transport, burned houses. About 60 percent of sites were on semiarid land; cultivators lived far from fields; people and livestock exceeded carrying capacity (erosion, cholera, stock epidemics). Food imports 1973-75 cost 1.2 billion shillings, enough to buy a cow for every family (Nyerere).

**Q7.7 (EXPLAIN).** What is the "dark twin" in Tanzania's communal fields?

**Answer.** Land under communal cultivation was far smaller than reported, section leaders went through the motions, and households treated the communal plot as a residual while private holdings carried subsistence. The yield gap mirrored the attention gap.

**Q7.8 (T/F).** Good intent protects against the generic developmentalist failure.

**Answer.** **False.** The generic logic produces the generic failure regardless of regime. Tanzania's weakness (no Stalinist methods, peasant flight, smuggling, foot-dragging) made practice less destructive than theory. The Ruvuma Development Association, which practiced Nyerere's ideals, was banned in 1968.

**Q7.9 (SCENARIO).** Leadership showcases a flagship pilot with dedicated staff and manual review as proof for automated global rollout. What do you do?

**Answer.** Apply the subsidy-density test: total capital, headcount and supervision per beneficiary, what the boundary excluded rather than solved, and that intensity at full scale. A pilot shows feasibility under lavish conditions, not scalability.

**Q7.10 (SCENARIO).** A reorg shuffles teams, destroying informal coordination. Which anti-pattern?

**Answer.** Forced resettlement as de-skilling, and "reorganizing boxes is easier than changing how work is done." Ask what place-specific expertise you are about to render worthless; recall the Paris taxi drivers who could halt traffic just by obeying every rule.

## Section 8 - Chapter 8: Taming Nature

**Q8.1 (SA).** List Scott's four systemic failures of high-modernist agriculture.

**Answer.** (1) Inherited unexamined assumptions; (2) institutional interest; (3) cyclopean shortsightedness (soil structure, water quality, tenure, externalities); (4) incapacity for complexity. Failures 3 and 4 are structural and would occur under the best administration.

**Q8.2 (EXPLAIN).** Why is an experimental plot itself a simplification?

**Answer.** Controls are a construction: weather, soil and landscape held constant, with implicit assumptions about farm size, labor and the cultivator's wants. Researchers called pests and bad weather "interference," yet coping with that is the farmer's main concern. Many narrow studies do not sum to one study of the corresponding complexity.

**Q8.3 (SA).** Describe van der Ploeg's Andean potatoes contrast.

**Answer.** Craft starts with this plot and selects varieties likely to thrive there. Science starts with an ideal genotype and determines the conditions realizing it, so extension remakes every farmer's field; the genotype thrives in a narrow band and within three or four years often becomes incapable of even low production.

**Q8.4 (SA).** What did Howard Jones (1936) and Sir Albert Howard add?

**Answer.** Jones, in Nigeria: intercropped plants sat at proper distances on hillocks arranged so rain neither waterlogged them nor sheeted off carrying soil, and ground stayed occupied. Howard, after three decades in India: fertility is structure (tilth, aeration, moisture holding, the mycorrhizal fungus bridge), approached from the field not the lab; a plot does not even represent the field it sits in.

**Q8.5 (EXPLAIN).** Describe Anderson's Santa Lucia garden and its lesson.

**Answer.** An apparent dump heap mapped to crosswise rows: fruit trees, cacti, coffee, medicinal herbs, hawthorn, two maize varieties (one a bean trellis), banana leaves as wrapping, cucurbits, a chayote compost pit, a beehive of boxes and tin cans. It was garden, orchard, medicine garden, dump, compost heap and beeyard at once, on a steep slope without erosion, producing continuously with little effort. Anderson: the Indian was budgeting time better than his critics.

**Q8.6 (EXPLAIN).** Why couldn't the agronomist see it?

**Answer.** Beyond prejudice and institutional interest, intercropping is unprovable by the available method: too many variables for unambiguous causal proof. Science has an elective affinity with pure-stand yield maximization and, where it could, simplified environments to match its way of knowing.

**Q8.7 (SA).** State the 1970 corn blight facts and the rescue.

**Answer.** 71 percent of US corn acreage was in six hybrids; the pathogen was old, the vulnerability new. An exotic Mexican landrace supplied the escape: the standardized system is parasitic on diversity it displaces. Interplanting is the norm on 80 percent of West African farmland.

**Q8.8 (EXPLAIN).** What do the Davis tomato and DDT show?

**Answer.** Tomato: the organism optimized for the measuring apparatus (thick-walled, uniform, picked green for the harvester). DDT: the model asked what dose kills mosquitoes and succeeded in its field of view; residues moving up the food chain were outside it, and the alarm came from outside the paradigm (eggshell thinning, a ban).

**Q8.9 (SCENARIO).** A staging environment passes all tests; production behaves differently. Which concept?

**Answer.** The staging environment is an experimental plot: controlled, normalized, silent about interaction effects only production produces. Enumerate what was held constant before generalizing.

**Q8.10 (SCENARIO).** A two-season trial finds no benefit from a many-variable practice. Drop it?

**Answer.** No. A null on a many-variable practice is not evidence of failure; short-run trials on long-run questions answer institutional pressure, not scientific method. Use a long-view criterion (Hicksian income) and note Scott does not dismiss science, which gives unambiguous, universal results by exclusion.

## Section 9 - Chapter 9: Thin Simplifications and Metis

**Q9.1 (SA).** Give the chapter's central quotation.

**Answer.** Formal order is "always and to some considerable degree parasitic on informal processes, which the formal scheme does not recognize, without which it could not exist, and which it alone cannot create or maintain."

**Q9.2 (SA).** Define metis and the conditions where it is most valuable.

**Answer.** A wide array of practical skills and acquired intelligence in responding to a changing natural and human environment (Detienne and Vernant), Odysseus's quality. Most valuable when situations are mutable, indeterminate and particular.

**Q9.3 (SA).** Name four features by which to recognize metis, and a failure mode.

**Answer.** Hard to teach apart from doing; acquired by practice and apprenticeship; rules of thumb whose content is knowing which to apply, in what order and when to throw the book away; tested by practical success. Failure mode: conservatism ("may favor the conservation of skills rather than daring innovation").

**Q9.4 (EXPLAIN).** Contrast metis and techne/episteme.

**Answer.** Metis: contextual, rules of thumb, practice and apprenticeship, "did it work?", partisan. Techne/episteme: universal, hard-and-fast deduction, formal teaching and verification, "is it explained and verified?", impersonal. Techne is settled knowledge, not discovery.

**Q9.5 (EXPLAIN).** Explain Squanto's maxim vs. the almanac and Quetelet.

**Answer.** "Plant corn when the oak leaves are the size of a squirrel's ear" compresses a natural succession (skunk cabbage, willows leafing, red-winged blackbirds, first mayfly hatch): timing varies, sequence rarely does, so it is a near-foolproof frost rule that travels. The almanac ("after the first full moon in May" or May 20) needs corrections for latitude, altitude and slope and is deliberately fail-safe, costing growing days. Quetelet's lilac formula ((4264 C) squared) is "hardly practical." Metis is as economical and accurate as the problem requires.

**Q9.6 (SA).** What did East German factories rely on before 1989?

**Answer.** Two employees absent from the org chart: the jack-of-all-trades who improvised repairs, and the wheeler-dealer who crossed the country in a Trabant full of soap powder, champagne and fashionable clothes to barter for a missing valve.

**Q9.7 (EXPLAIN).** What does "locally superior knowledge" mean, and what happened in the Caterpillar work-to-rule?

**Answer.** The harbor pilot outranks the captain inside the harbor: situated knowledge "must prevail." At Caterpillar workers reverted to the engineers' specified procedures, knowing the official method would cost the company time and quality.

**Q9.8 (EXPLAIN).** Explain "metis : rules :: speech : grammar" and the Red Adair test.

**Answer.** Grammar derives from speech; perfect grammar is compatible with inability to speak; rules of thumb summarize practice and are taught after competence. Adair's team must begin with the unpredictable and invent the technique for that fire; real work rarely allows a cookie-cutter solution.

**Q9.9 (SCENARIO).** A migration will retire the on-call engineers who know why odd configs exist. Which concept and action?

**Answer.** Metis as load-bearing knowledge. Treat documentation/practice gaps as information, run a work-to-rule thought experiment, and keep a channel to the operators.

**Q9.10 (SCENARIO).** A general safety rule conflicts with local expertise at a specific site. Who prevails?

**Answer.** Inside its locality, the local practitioner (harbor pilot), when the environment is mutable, indeterminate and particular. "The more general the rules, the more they require in the way of translation."

## Section 10 - Chapter 10: Conclusion

**Q10.1 (SA).** Give Scott's one-sentence condensation of why such plans fail.

**Answer.** "The progenitors of such plans regarded themselves as far smarter and farseeing than they really were and, at the same time, regarded their subjects as far more stupid and incompetent than they really were."

**Q10.2 (SA).** List the four rules of thumb.

**Answer.** Take small steps; favor reversibility; plan on surprises; plan on human inventiveness.

**Q10.3 (EXPLAIN).** Explain Haldane's mouse and Leopold's quote.

**Answer.** A mouse dropped down a mineshaft walks away; "a rat is killed, a man broken, a horse splashes" (scale of intervention vs. survivable error). Leopold: "The first rule of intelligent tinkering is to keep all the parts" (reversibility).

**Q10.4 (EXPLAIN).** What is the two maps test?

**Answer.** Map 1: routes the planners provided. Map 2: time-lapse of all unplanned movement (baby carriages, window shopping, shortcuts, hopscotch). The older the neighborhood, the more Map 2 supersedes Map 1. "Planned Brasilia is, in a thousand ways, underwritten by unplanned Brasilia."

**Q10.5 (SA).** State the metis-friendly institution litmus test.

**Answer.** "To what degree does it promise to enhance the skills, knowledge, and responsibility of those who are a part of it?" It separates "canned" situations from those open to metis.

**Q10.6 (EXPLAIN).** Contrast the Vietnam Memorial and the Iwo Jima statue.

**Answer.** Lin's memorial: a low black wall ordered chronologically, no prose claim; visitors touch names, make rubbings and leave mementos (a high-heeled shoe, a glass of champagne, a full-house poker hand); "virtually requires participation in order to complete its meaning." Iwo Jima: heroic, patriotic, "more symbolically self-sufficient"; visitors "receive its message rather than completing it." Use it or lose it.

**Q10.7 (SA).** What is the abstract citizen, and the self-fulfilling logic of social engineering?

**Answer.** The standardized planning subject with "no gender, no tastes, no history, no values, no opinions or original ideas, no traditions," the necessary first premise of large-scale planning. Narrow environments foster a less skilled, less resourceful population (institutional neurosis), which then does need supervision: the plan produces the subjects it assumed.

**Q10.8 (EXPLAIN).** When are fixed routines legitimately efficient, and why is the invisible hand not the escape?

**Answer.** When the task environment is repetitive, stable and predictable; "in most economies and in human affairs generally, this is seldom the case." The market is itself an instituted formal system dependent on contract and property law, coercive enforcement, trust norms and a finite ecosystem; a single metric is invaluable until it becomes hegemonic.

**Q10.9 (SA).** On what grounds does Scott temper his condemnation?

**Answer.** Supplanted orders were often manifestly unjust; high modernism came cloaked in emancipatory ideals (equality before the law, citizenship, rights to subsistence, health, education, shelter); representative institutions force accommodation; ordinary resistance remakes plans over time. He also affirms the benefits of division of labor and hierarchical coordination for some tasks.

**Q10.10 (SCENARIO).** An engineering team plans to delete the old system the day the new one launches. Which rules apply?

**Answer.** Favor reversibility and keep all the parts; take small steps (incremental migration, feature flags, rollback path); do not delete the old system before the new one survives a full cycle. For a developer wanting locked floor plans, the parallel advice is flexible, adjustable designs from the outset.

## Cumulative Final Exam (20 questions)

Answer all twenty before checking. 18+ is mastery; 14-17 solid; below 14, reread Part 1 of the companion file and retry.

**F1 (SA).** Give the four-element recipe and what each supplies.

**Answer.** Administrative ordering/legibility (capacity), high-modernist ideology (desire), authoritarian state (determination), prostrate civil society (terrain). All four are needed; 3 and 4 are lethal: nobody can say no. *Source:* Intro, ch3.

**F2 (T/F).** Scott is anti-state, anti-planning and anti-science.

**Answer.** **False.** The state is "the vexed institution that is the ground of both our freedoms and our unfreedoms"; legibility enables public health, fair taxation, rule of law, poverty relief. His target is "an imperial or hegemonic planning mentality that excludes the necessary role of local knowledge and know-how." *Source:* SKILL.md, Intro.

**F3 (EXPLAIN).** Why is the German forest the canonical case for every later thin simplification?

**Answer.** Normalbaum and yield tables gave a synoptic view; the forest was then remade to fit the tables (monocrop spruce in rows); the first rotation was spectacular and the second fell 20-30 percent (*Waldsterben*); nest boxes and ants admitted the cleared "debris" was the system. The arc recurs in Brasilia, collectivization, ujamaa and hybrid corn. *Source:* ch1.

**F4 (SA).** What do surnames, cadasters and measures share, and what is the two-edged caution?

**Answer.** Each replaces illegible local practice with a standard grid readable from the center, and each creates what it describes. The same capacity enables vaccination and relief and also the 1941 Amsterdam map. *Source:* ch1, ch2.

**F5 (EXPLAIN).** Define high modernism and explain why it is not science.

**Answer.** A muscle-bound faith in scientific and technical progress, mastery of nature and rational design of social order, reading order in visual aesthetic terms. It borrows science's legitimacy but is "uncritical, unskeptical, and thus unscientifically optimistic." *Source:* Intro, ch3.

**F6 (SA).** What are the three liberal brakes and Sen's argument?

**Answer.** A private sphere, a private sector, working representative institutions. Free press, assembly and elections prevent famine because rulers otherwise have no incentive to learn of it. *Source:* ch3.

**F7 (EXPLAIN).** Pair Le Corbusier-Jacobs, Lenin-Luxemburg/Kollontai, agronomist-Anderson, and general navigation rules-harbor pilot. What do the pairs share?

**Answer.** Each pits a central, abstract, visual-order view (the Plan, vanguard party, pure-stand agronomy, general rules) against street-level practical knowledge (sidewalk eyes, learning by the movement, intercropped garden, local pilot): the second member holds metis. *Source:* ch4, ch5, ch8, ch9.

**F8 (SA).** What did candangos and the 75-percent figure show?

**Answer.** The planned city depended on the unplanned: by 1980 75 percent of residents lived in unplanned settlements while the planned city was under half its projected population. *Source:* ch4, ch10.

**F9 (SA).** What actually brought down the regime in 1917, and what does that say about vanguard theory?

**Answer.** Soviets and factory seizures, peasant land seizures, military collapse in Austria and Kerensky's lack of coercive resources. The theory failed as description and succeeded disastrously as prescription after the seizure. *Source:* ch5.

**F10 (EXPLAIN).** What did collectivization achieve and fail at, and what do private plots prove?

**Answer.** Failed on yields; succeeded at appropriation and control (grain, industrial financing, dissolving the mir). Private plots supplied most fruit, vegetables, eggs and dairy: forbidden informal production was load-bearing. *Source:* ch6.

**F11 (MC).** The wheat/raspberry lesson is best captured by: A) wheat is more valuable; B) match institution to the work's robustness and skill demands; C) private farming always beats collective; D) Soviet farmers lacked skill.

**Answer.** **B.** Robust, uniform, low-skill work survives central management; fragile, timing-critical, high-skill work needs an attentive resident operator. *Source:* ch6, ch8.

**F12 (EXPLAIN).** Why is miniaturization dangerous as evidence? Two examples.

**Answer.** The demonstration works because of subsidy and supervision that cannot scale: Tanzania's 300-settler scheme absorbed four bulldozers, nine tractors, seven lorries, a generator, fifteen administrators and 150 laborers; Campbell's Montana farm engineered context away with Morgan money and subsidy, then collapsed in two years. *Source:* Intro, ch6, ch7.

**F13 (SA).** What is the "dark twin"? Give three examples across chapters.

**Answer.** The unofficial reality without which the official version stops working: private plots (ch6), informal settlements and gray economies (ch4, ch7), landraces (ch8), the East German jack-of-all-trades and wheeler-dealer (ch9).

**F14 (EXPLAIN).** Why does a null result on polyculture not prove it fails?

**Answer.** Intercropping puts too many variables in play for unambiguous proof (ten bodies' orbits never repeat). The method guarantees verifiable results by radical simplification, so what resists it reads as unproven, not unexamined. Anderson's garden shows the order beneath apparent disorder. *Source:* ch8.

**F15 (SA).** Compare the corn blight and second-rotation numbers.

**Answer.** 71 percent of US corn acreage in six hybrids; 20-30 percent loss in the second rotation. Both show uniformity is fragile: the pathogen was old, the vulnerability new, and a landrace or diverse forest was hidden insurance. *Source:* ch1, ch8.

**F16 (EXPLAIN).** What does work-to-rule prove, and where does it appear?

**Answer.** Formal rules depend on informal practice: following every rule exactly halts work. It appears in the Introduction, Paris taxi drivers (ch7) and Caterpillar (ch9); it is the fastest diagnostic that documentation is not the system.

**F17 (SA).** List the four rules of thumb and two tests from Chapter 10.

**Answer.** Small steps, reversibility, plan on surprises, plan on human inventiveness; the two maps test and the metis-friendly litmus test (does the form enhance its participants' skills, knowledge and responsibility?).

**F18 (EXPLAIN).** Show how three cases substituted aesthetic or countable order for functional order.

**Answer.** Normalbaum/spruce rows: legible tables, hollow ecology. Superquadra: identical facades, no streets, brasilite. Ujamaa village: straight rows and roadside layout judged by resemblance to the modern, ~60 percent of sites on semiarid land. *Source:* ch1, ch4, ch7, ch10.

**F19 (SCENARIO).** A company launches one global score for all teams, mandated from HQ, punishes dissent as obstruction and offers no appeal. Diagnose and propose mitigations.

**Answer.** Thin simplification/hegemonic metric, elements 3 and 4 at small scale, suppression of metis. Mitigations from the notes: write down what the metric excludes and keep a channel for the excluded; restore a real veto held by someone who bears the cost; convene the affected before rollout; keep old records; use small reversible steps. (Transfers are analogical.) *Source:* cheatsheet, ch1, ch3, ch10.

**F20 (EXPLAIN).** Why is Scott not recommending the market, and what does he recommend?

**Answer.** The market is a formal system dependent on law, enforcement, trust norms and a finite ecosystem, and large-scale capitalism homogenizes as the state does. He offers rules of thumb, the two maps test and mētis-friendly institutions like language, common law and democracy, not a program. *Source:* Intro, ch10, SKILL.md.
