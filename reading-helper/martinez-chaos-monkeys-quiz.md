# Chaos Monkeys Quiz Book
*Chaos Monkeys: Obscene Fortune and Random Failure in Silicon Valley* by Antonio García Martínez

**How to use:** Cover the answers, answer from memory (out loud or in writing), then check. Anything you miss is a chapter section to reread. Each chapter has short-answer (SA), true/false (T/F), and multiple-choice (MC) questions. A cumulative 10-question final exam is at the end. Chapter numbering follows the study notes (14 chapters grouped from the book's sections), not the book's own 46 sections. Figures are period data (2007-2016) as given in the notes.

---

## Chapter 1: The Undertakers of Capitalism / The Human Attention Exchange (Prologue, Part One)

**Q1 (SA).** What does "price on perception" mean, and how does it link Goldman Sachs to ad tech?
**A:** A credit-default swap prices the perceived probability that a corporation won't pay; an ad prices the perceived value of a slice of attention. "The same difference either way; only the scale of the money pile changes." The same quant skills and price-making dynamics apply to both.

**Q2 (MC).** Why did Goldman decline to move CDS onto exchanges?
a) Regulators forbade it  b) With few players, total information asymmetry and price-making power, transparency only invites competition  c) The technology wasn't ready  d) Clients preferred it
**A:** b. "Goldman felt more comfortable owning a small market than merely participating in a larger one," a pattern Martínez says he saw again at Facebook.

**Q3 (SA).** What was the SLK lesson?
**A:** Goldman's acquired broker went from hundreds of traders and two programmers (2000) to two traders and twenty quants with fast machines (2007). Generalized (via Andreessen): two future job types, people who tell computers what to do and people told by computers what to do. Finance saw it first; ad tech (RTB) followed, then Uber/Airbnb/Instacart.

**Q4 (SA).** Distinguish direct response from brand advertising.
**A:** Direct response has a short, trackable attention-to-action window. Brand has a long window, offline conversion, and huge budgets spent in "embarrassingly large broadsides."

**Q5 (T/F).** By 2011 Facebook's rich store of user data had already transformed its monetization. **A:** False. A year of ingesting posts, check-ins, Likes and friends barely moved monetization; advertiser first-party data (what you bought and browsed) outranks publisher demographics, which is the thesis the rest of the book proves.

**Q6 (SA).** What was the outcome of the April 13, 2012 Zuck meeting?
**A:** Three proposals were on the table: Like-button browsing data (Zuck: no) and two ways to join external data. The quoted result: "yes to retargeting, but no to using social plugins."

**Q7 (SA).** What advice does the DUI gambit illustrate?
**A:** Negotiate from weakness by reframing the encounter from judgment to haggling (he offered the officer a taxi under observation instead of a station blood test). "If you want to be a startup entrepreneur, get used to negotiating from positions of weakness."

## Chapter 2: Knowing How to Swim / Abandoning the Shipwreck

**Q1 (SA).** List the four signs of a doomed startup.
**A:** (1) Only the founder survives (the Team page has been purged). (2) It makes lots of money from something other than the product. (3) No clients for the actual product (rotating beta-customer logos). (4) Loyal employees despite mistreatment (Stockholm-syndrome retention).

**Q2 (MC).** Under the miracle count, a startup idea that needs zero miracles is:
a) a startup  b) a laundromat, not a startup  c) a bad idea  d) a unicorn
**A:** b. One miracle is a startup (Airbnb: user behavior; Google: technical; Uber/Instacart: consumer workflow; Slack: business workflow). Two or more is "the classic sign of a shitty startup idea."

**Q3 (SA).** How many miracles did the team's bar-code-to-local-ad idea need, and how does the book define a "pivot"?
**A:** Five. A pivot is not a ballerina's turn but "a panicked sprint" for the last life raft; their product ended as "Plan J."

**Q4 (T/F).** Y Combinator judges the idea first and the team second. **A:** False. YC judges the team and uses the idea only as a probe. Claiming "stealth" or demanding an NDA is a "LOSER" tattoo.

**Q5 (SA).** Why is Adchemy's roughly $6M/month of lead-gen revenue described as a sedative rather than a runway?
**A:** It was money from something other than the product; like a trust fund it made "a drug addict's spiral more long lasting" and masked the product's failure.

**Q6 (SA).** What does H-1B "peonage" mean in the chapter, and how did Adchemy use it?
**A:** Visa holders can't quit without risking deportation, giving employers retention leverage. Adchemy's immigrant executives threatened to report a former employee to ICE for planning to work on an F-2 ("former slaves turned slave owners").

**Q7 (SA).** What was the "wrong answer" when Paul Graham called with the YC acceptance, and what is the lesson?
**A:** "I should probably ask him first" (about the cofounder). Decide commitment among cofounders before the offer arrives.

## Chapter 3: War Face / Like Marriage / Speed Is a Feature / D-Day

**Q1 (SA).** What does YC actually sell, according to the chapter?
**A:** (1) Access to partners, (2) the YC-founder network (a private microcosm where you can rebuild ~80% of the stack from YC companies, with discounts, preferential adoption and logos), (3) bankable prestige, (4) Demo Day.

**Q2 (SA).** How does Google's auction work, and what is ROAS?
**A:** An auction on every query: bid x estimated click probability, highest product wins. ROAS = revenue / cost - 1, the universal marketer metric (the "nevada cheap divorce" example: 47%).

**Q3 (MC).** Why did Clickable, Lexity and Trada fail?
a) Bad engineering  b) They tried the small-business last mile, where high-touch sales at $50-100/month can't scale and SMBs cancel as fast as they join  c) Google sued them  d) No demand for search ads
**A:** b. "Not every problem has an engineering solution. That doesn't mean you can't sell one."

**Q4 (SA).** What does "Have a leader" mean, and what went wrong with equal thirds?
**A:** Either a "Vulcan mind-meld" forged by shared hard experience or one person with 51%+; the only early title that matters is CEO. Equal thirds produced decision paralysis and rotating two-against-one factions. About six of thirty batch companies had disputed leadership and crumbled or had coups.

**Q5 (SA).** Describe the content-marketing launch recipe.
**A:** Pick the biggest nerve (New York's self-regard, then Goldman Sachs); post Tuesday morning (Monday too groggy, Thursday drifting, Friday for burying news); seed upvotes on Hacker News; reply to fans, egg on trolls; double down when it works; have servers ready (the single-server blog melted).

**Q6 (T/F).** In the book, "chaos monkey" originally refers to a tool that randomly kills servers to test resilience, and is used metaphorically for entrepreneurs who pull the plug on industries. **A:** True (Netflix's tool; Uber vs. medallions, Airbnb vs. hotels, Tinder vs. dating).

**Q7 (SA).** What lesson did Rapportive teach about comparing yourself to peers?
**A:** "This too shall pass." Its polished Prototype Day pitch dismayed him, but two years later both companies had the same acqui-hire exit.

## Chapter 4: Conclave of Angels / Hill of Sand / Widening Gyre / ¡No Pasarán!

**Q1 (SA).** Contrast the old-school angel (Russ Siegelman) with the micro-VC (Chris Sacca).
**A:** Russ invests his own money, wants 2-4x, wants it back if you're imploding, does haphazard diligence through friends. Sacca runs a $20-40M fund ("a fund of bosses"), needs outsized wins, and supplies a gold-plated name that makes weak-kneed investors follow.

**Q2 (SA).** A $100K note has a $3M cap and the Series A prices at $10M. What does the angel own?
**A:** About 3.3%, regardless of the A's price. Without a cap, the angel's stake would shrink as the company does better (1% at a $10M A, 0.1% at $100M).

**Q3 (MC).** The best test to cut through VC titles is to ask:
a) "What is your fund size?"  b) "Do you have decision-making power?"  c) "Who else is investing?"  d) "What is your carry?"
**A:** b. Any hedge means a lackey; route around them. What matters is carry and whether the person can present and defend a deal at the Monday partner meeting.

**Q4 (SA).** What was the Death Clock, and what did the author do with it?
**A:** A cash-burn spreadsheet with legal costs (Fenwick's estimate: ~$500K to reach trial, ~$1.5M total over 18 months, ~$20K/month); every version crashed. He hid it from cofounders and investors and "cooked the books" (lower legal costs, impossible launch date, "unconscionable" growth rate).

**Q5 (SA).** Explain the Fenwick deal as a "mispriced option."
**A:** Fenwick fronted $250K of fees as a loan for a small equity slice plus options. If AdGrok died, nothing was repaid; if it won, it could buy the equity back at the seed price with no prepayment penalty: "a one-in-a-million lottery ticket to win $250,000... for $250,000."

**Q6 (T/F).** After the lawsuit, Russ stayed in but cut the cap from $4M to $2M. **A:** True. About 22% was sold in the seed for ~$500K, roughly double typical seed dilution, accepted because the alternative was death.

**Q7 (SA).** What are "forgotten crimes"?
**A:** Balzac: "The secret of great fortunes without apparent cause is a forgotten crime." Examples: Gates cloned Kildall's QDOS and kept the copyright; Jobs pocketed the Atari bonus and paid Wozniak $350; Zuckerberg ran with the idea he was hired to build.

## Chapter 5: Dog Shit Sandwich / Victory / Launching!

**Q1 (SA).** What are the two weak points of any tech company, and why?
**A:** Investors (board votes and personal sway; a CEO serves at the board's pleasure) and potential business partners (enterprise deals are lifeblood and even large companies fear public shaming and legal quagmires).

**Q2 (SA).** How was Adchemy's lawsuit actually defeated?
**A:** Paul Graham planned to anathematize Adchemy's VCs (August, Mayfield) by disinviting them from Demo Day; Sam Altman got Microsoft's BD to tell Adchemy the litigation was "problematic" for a Bing deal; the lawyers filed rebuttals and started hostile depositions. Adchemy offered to dismiss in late October; settled February 2011.

**Q3 (MC).** PG's instruction on handling the crisis inside the company was:
a) Tell the team everything  b) "Come alone... keep them out of it"  c) Hire more lawyers  d) Go public with it
**A:** b. The team adds no value and loses productivity if the crisis becomes the internal narrative.

**Q4 (SA).** What two traits do founders actually need?
**A:** Monomaniacal focus on one thing at the expense of all else, and endurance of endless shit (learnable via hardship: cross the US by bicycle, sail an ocean, or join the Marines). Intelligence, technical skill and vision are not the scarce inputs. "It helps to have enemies."

**Q5 (T/F).** The Trough of Despair is a dip in the middle of fund-raising. **A:** False. It is the post-launch curve (spike, crash, long flatline, slight rise) that kills 90%+ of startups that survived founder fights, shipping and fund-raising.

**Q6 (SA).** How does the chapter define product-market fit?
**A:** Iterating from an approximately correct starting point, each iteration costing wages, servers and time, with a hard deadline when cash hits zero; fit means users pay more than they cost to serve and acquire.

**Q7 (T/F).** The tech press covers launches, so a launch spike is a reliable signal of success. **A:** False. A "launch" is a PR event (embargoed, timed Monday); the anti-pattern is "believing the launch spike."

## Chapter 6: Dates @Twitter / Acquisition Chicken / Getting Liked / Getting Poked

**Q1 (SA).** What does "know them better than their mothers do" prescribe?
**A:** A Facebook-Twitter-LinkedIn stalk before any meeting: read CV tenure (<1 year = didn't vest; exactly 4 years = a plodder), mutual connections, where they eat, origin. "Know what they want... and you can predict 90 percent of what they eventually do."

**Q2 (SA).** What are the two channels in a deal, and what does email signal?
**A:** A formal channel (email, documents) and a clandestine one (phone, in person; the "dragoman" layer). "If you're still emailing about it, there's actually nothing real going on."

**Q3 (MC).** The right time to create an auction is:
a) After the no-shop is signed  b) Before the no-shop lands (typically 60 days, enforceable)  c) After the term sheet closes  d) Never
**A:** b. "In play" was the phrase used to get a rival's product team to engage.

**Q4 (SA).** Why did the Google bluff work at Facebook?
**A:** Corp-dev chief Amin Zoufonoun, three days out of Google, had no back channel there to verify; "everybody in tech feared Google." A bluff works when the target can't check it.

**Q5 (SA).** Why do angels and fund-manager VCs differ on a good-enough offer?
**A:** Angels investing their own money (Russ) take 2x happily; fund managers (Sacca) need outsized multiples because funds are fire-and-forget, so VCs push you to swing for the fences. Use the VC as the cudgel when you want to reject a low offer.

**Q6 (SA).** How are acqui-hires priced and how should you approach the interview day?
**A:** Valley engineers fetched $0.5-2M each in four-year vesting equity; Twitter's "$5 million thing" for three hires was "way too cheap." Every wanted employee passes the standard hiring screen; the bundle is your union ("don't like the price, we're all off the table"). Get the interviewer list a day early and stalk each one.

**Q7 (T/F).** "Cultural fit" is a reliable measure of whether someone will succeed. **A:** False. The notes call it a license that in practice excludes the happy-hour buzzkill, the quiet Indian or Chinese engineer, and the self-taught kid from a no-name college.

## Chapter 7: Forking Paths / Retweets / The Dotted Line / Endgame

**Q1 (SA).** What did Facebook want from AdGrok, and what did Twitter do after the walkout?
**A:** Facebook rejected the engineers but wanted the CEO. Twitter, expected to go silent, called back within an hour with $5M+ for the two engineers.

**Q2 (SA).** What does a fast callback reveal, and what is a "call station"?
**A:** A weak hand. Twitter should have gone silent for days and bought AdGrok "for a twenty-dollar Starbucks gift card"; instead its reflex was to call back above the original price.

**Q3 (MC).** In an acqui-hire, the acquirer is:
a) Careful to preserve the investors' pro rata  b) Targeting a price per high-value person and indifferent to the founder/investor split  c) Paying for the product  d) Paying mostly cash
**A:** b. The headline "sold for $X million" may be ~10% company price and 90% signing bonuses and vesting; Twitter's default gave investors ~$1M of ~$10M instead of ~$2M pro rata.

**Q4 (SA).** What is a "replicating portfolio" and how was it used?
**A:** Demanding that the new employer match your slice of the other deal. Facebook's offer at ~$32/share pre-IPO came to ~$2.3M, about a 25% AdGrok stake after the split.

**Q5 (T/F).** Founders own their equity from day one, so they can walk away mid-deal. **A:** False. Founders' 20-40% stakes vest like employees' so a departing founder can't hold a gun to the company; "nobody at AdGrok actually owned anything."

**Q6 (SA).** What does "resign before you sign" mean?
**A:** Per the employment lawyer: accept immediately and resign first, or breach both noncompetes. He sent the resignation email as corp dev opened the door (the 3:07 Caltrain).

**Q7 (SA).** What was the "master play" he did not make, and why?
**A:** Negotiate both deals in secret, front-load Twitter with single-trigger acceleration or up-front cash, accept, quit day one, and walk to Facebook. It required deceiving cofounders, "a step too far out on the prick spectrum." His rule: lie to investors and acquirers if you must; deceiving cofounders is the line.

## Chapter 8: Boot Camp / Product Masseur / Google Delenda Est / Leaping Headlong

**Q1 (SA).** How does the chapter describe Facebook's onboarding?
**A:** As a citizenship oath ("Ellis Island"): shed your prior culture; truth, selfless collaboration, ownership; posters (MAKE AN IMPACT, DONE IS BETTER THAN PERFECT, FORTUNE FAVORS THE BOLD); the blue T-shirt. "Think of onboarding as conversion, not orientation."

**Q2 (SA).** What is the PM as "shit umbrella / pirate captain"?
**A:** Internally, the PM does everything but coding (legal, sales pitches, horse-trading, fronting for management) and is "golden" only if engineers build what they stipulate; externally, a two-line email summons any company's leadership, and the craven mistake Facebook's power for the PM's own.

**Q3 (MC).** What was the "monetization = data per pixel" insight about 2011 Facebook?
a) Its CPMs were the highest in the industry  b) The revenue dashboard's average CPM was bottom-of-the-barrel; it made money on volume while usage was "up there with heroin"  c) It had no ads  d) Mobile was its main revenue
**A:** b. The notes call the impression monetization "utter dog shit."

**Q4 (SA).** What was Project Chorizo and what was its result?
**A:** Systematic tests feeding every user action (posts, messages, links, check-ins) into topic targeting. Nothing improved advertiser performance: "A mile-long trainful of meat cows... one measly sausage."

**Q5 (SA).** What was the PM's first tiebreaker decision, and the reasoning?
**A:** Keep existing keywords (20% of revenue) and mark new topic segments with a Twitter-style "#"; no UI redesign (design was the bottleneck); launch fast. Kitten shipped in two months (launched August 1, 2011).

**Q6 (T/F).** The notes say "very few companies have died due to launching early... countless companies have died by losing the nerve to ship." **A:** True (bias for launching; knowing when to launch is "a subtle art at best, and a drunken coin toss at worst").

**Q7 (SA).** What triggered Lockdown, and what competitive intelligence did the author note?
**A:** Google Plus (June 2011, ad-free, subsidized by AdWords). Zuck invoked Cato's "Carthage must be destroyed." Google's engineering lot was empty on a Sunday; Facebook's was full.

## Chapter 9: One Shot, One Kill / Twice Bitten / Ads Five-Oh / Narcissism of Privacy

**Q1 (SA).** What is the noisy-bar metaphor?
**A:** Facebook is every bar and cafe on earth; the targeting PM has a transcript of every conversation, but people rarely say anything a CMO cares about, sarcasm is unparseable, and it must work in Tigrinya. Social data is mostly commercially inert.

**Q2 (MC).** To "earn your beer money" at Facebook scale a new ads idea needed to move about:
a) 0.1% of revenue  b) 1%  c) 5% of revenue (~$100M/year then)  d) 50%
**A:** c. Unsexy, programmatic, platform-wide changes (Kitten) beat niche ideas such as political-district or travel-intender targeting.

**Q3 (SA).** Why must a PM be technical?
**A:** To sniff-test engineers' claims, which may be accidental (wishful memory), passive-aggressive ("that'll eat up all our servers"), or lazy ("impossible"). A senior engineer's assurance about the Irish audit turned out wrong.

**Q4 (SA).** What happened on Christmas Eve 2011?
**A:** With the Irish discontinuation deadline hours away, the author ran an UPDATE on the live targeting tables behind ~$2B in spend, with one engineer watching, to verify what he'd vouched for; then he went for a beer. Lesson: verify the thing you personally vouched for, even in production.

**Q5 (T/F).** Facebook sells user data to advertisers. **A:** False. Facebook buys data: it provides services that incentivize advertisers to let it ingest their outside data, a "black hole of data that can never leave"; handing data out would be "suicidally stupid."

**Q6 (SA).** What was the 2011 data-policy referendum and its outcome?
**A:** Zuck's 2009 pledge to vote on policy changes with a 30% participation threshold (~300M people). About 90% of voters opposed the new policy but almost nobody voted, so the result was "taken under advisement." Without the new policy, the 2012-13 company-saving products would have been impossible.

**Q7 (SA).** Why is "embarrassing" not "valuable"?
**A:** No advertiser cares about your secrets, sex life or brownie recipe; they want last night's Netflix pick, your Amazon cart, Best Buy browsing, your car's age, your devices. Blaming Facebook for a creepy ad is like blaming Gmail for spam: Facebook is the telephone exchange, the advertiser addressed you.

## Chapter 10: Are We Savages? / O Death / Barbaric Yawn / Going Public

**Q1 (SA).** Describe the blue-tape loop.
**A:** Bold experiment (employees paint the new campus) -> fast failure (two days of vandalism) -> prune (Zuck walked the halls with blue masking tape marking what came down) -> internalize ("bluetape" became a code-review macro meaning "remove this for sanity").

**Q2 (SA).** What is the corporate memento mori?
**A:** Sun logos deliberately left on doors and on the back of the Facebook sign, reminders that complacency (Sun ignored Linux on commodity hardware) leads to extinction. Death awareness as discipline, not fear.

**Q3 (MC).** The vesting math says to value a big-company offer at:
a) Its headline  b) Half the headline (you'll leave in about two years), halved again if it is stock rather than options  c) Double the headline  d) Only the salary
**A:** b. Standard vesting is four years, 25% at one year, then quarterly.

**Q4 (SA).** What is "track racing vs. commuting"?
**A:** After a startup, a big company feels like crawling at 80 mph. Facebook was an autobahn (fast lane by Zuck's permission), Apple/Google the 101 at rush hour, Oracle a Delhi intersection. The itch to leave starts within months.

**Q5 (SA).** Why do big companies buy startups beyond recruiting?
**A:** Cultural hybridization: cross obedient-graduate DNA with founder wildness (the cattle-dog analogy). The product almost always vanishes; acquirees succeed only with Zuck-granted latitude (Instagram) or by reining themselves in. First rule: act like you belong.

**Q6 (T/F).** Haves and have-mores discuss money openly on Facebook's campus. **A:** False. Money was never discussed; the wealthy "furiously denied their very existence," and middle managers hired late knew the gap yet sided with the owners.

**Q7 (SA).** Why does Martínez call Facebook "the People's Republic"?
**A:** Mission cultures at scale resemble revolutionary regimes: endless toil for lapidary ideals from an unquestioned leader, a leadership caste chosen for adherence, a flattering media, foot soldiers who sacrifice family. Opting out is theoretical given Bay Area housing and tuition.

## Chapter 11: Flying Saucers / Monetizing the Tumor

**Q1 (SA).** What did Festinger's flying-saucer cult teach about product organizations?
**A:** The cult grew stronger when the prophecy failed (1954); companies likewise "go temporarily mad" under market expectation and arrogance, ignoring contradicting data. Sponsored Stories persisted through this cognitive dissonance.

**Q2 (SA).** Why was Open Graph + Sponsored Stories a "two-miracle" plan?
**A:** It required every company to integrate and donate data at dubious benefit, and marketers to abandon proven workflows for an unprovable paradigm. Hence "almost certain to fail."

**Q3 (MC).** Social context lift of 40-60% matters little because:
a) It was a bad study  b) It came from an abysmal base (~0.05% clickthrough vs. >=0.1% for the worst display ads and ~1% for well-targeted ads)  c) It was only on mobile  d) Advertisers hated it
**A:** b. A 60% lift on a 20x deficit is "piddly-boo." Judge lift against the best alternative, not your own baseline.

**Q4 (SA).** What did Sheryl declare in March 2012?
**A:** That no brand would keep paying ~$10M a year for Likes; the Like-buying party was over (in "Only Good News," the appellate court for Ads disputes).

**Q5 (T/F).** Ads and Growth are aligned, since both want a bigger Facebook. **A:** False. They are yin-yang and anticorrelated: Ads turns users into money; Growth turns money into users. "What makes a user use the product does not necessarily make money."

**Q6 (SA).** What was LOX and why did it show the Ads-Growth conflict?
**A:** The Logout Experience: selling the logout page to brand and movie marketers for a few hundred thousand dollars. Growth couldn't quantify the usage cost, so outrage and personal sway decided; LOX ran only where Facebook had won (the US), not where rivals like Orkut were "still warm" (Brazil).

**Q7 (SA).** What is "running out of humans"?
**A:** ~3B internet users minus ~600M in China and most of Russia, minus a 25% never-join haircut is ~1.8B addressable; at 1.44B (Q1 2015) and ~13% growth, roughly a year of growth left. Hence Internet.org and solar drones to create connectivity.

## Chapter 12: Great Awakening / Barbarians at the Gates / IPA > IPO / IPO Reevaluation / Flash Boys

**Q1 (SA).** Explain "advertising is name-calling."
**A:** Direct mail uses a postal address, mobile a quasi-immutable device ID, the web a cookie. The dominant puzzle in marketing is joining these names and controlling the links; the platform war is over who controls the joins.

**Q2 (SA).** Who is the "real Big Brother" in the chapter?
**A:** Not Facebook, Google or the NSA but data brokers (Acxiom, Experian, Epsilon, Merkle, Neustar), who have tracked consumer America since the 1960s and sell segments no citizen can inspect.

**Q3 (MC).** Direct mail is a prize because it is:
a) Tiny and growing  b) A $50B+ industry at ~$1,000 CPMs vs. ~$1 online  c) Free  d) Illegal
**A:** b. About $17B in postage; online can be a thousand times less effective and break even.

**Q4 (SA).** What is data on-boarding?
**A:** Firms (Datalogix, Neustar, LiveRamp) place invisible pixels where name/email meets a browser (newsletters, dating sites), drop or read a cookie, and store the join hashed. Facebook and Google out-on-board everyone; Custom Audiences match rates reach ~90%.

**Q5 (SA).** Describe the FBX build.
**A:** Five weeks to ship by June 15, 2012 to book Q3-Q4 revenue; three engineers; a parallel ads system with separate data stores, real-time global bidding hundreds of thousands of times per second, dynamic creative, separate stats and monitoring, partner docs; bought with a promise of $100M in 2012 revenue. First successful bid June 15 via TellApart.

**Q6 (SA).** Why is 120 ms significant?
**A:** It is the bound (a third of an eye-blink) to return a bid including network latency. North Carolina to Amsterdam is ~23 ms one way at light speed and ~60 ms in practice, so European requests timed out and FBX lost money there.

**Q7 (SA).** What was the "unforgivable sin" of FBX?
**A:** Its bids fed Facebook's own auction, making Facebook "merely one more DSP"; outside bidders who knew you'd just shopped for a handbag outbid Facebook's own data. Equality with outsiders was a feature to the author, a bug to management.

## Chapter 13: Full Frontal Facebook / Microsoft Shrugged / Ad Majorem / Adiós, Facebook

**Q1 (SA).** What were the open and closed options, and what was decided on April 12, 2013?
**A:** Open: FBX (standard programmatic exchange, instant adoption, outside data on equal footing). Closed: Custom Audiences plus an in-house ad server (Atlas), years to build, total lock-in. Boz recommended to Sheryl no identity matching and no mobile on FBX; she said "if that's what you think... that's what we'll do."

**Q2 (SA).** Define incrementality and apply it.
**A:** New spend that would not otherwise have landed on Facebook. CA's dashboard dollars largely came from existing "Facebook" budget lines (discount them by half); FBX competed for budgets earmarked for Google's AdX, so it was 100% incremental.

**Q3 (MC).** Why does Google's ad server count clicks while Facebook wants viewthroughs?
a) Technical limitation  b) Search exploits existing intent; Facebook must manufacture desire, so it wants credit for impressions, Likes, shares  c) Legal requirement  d) Advertisers demand it
**A:** b. The ad server is the oracle that counts delivery, frequency and geography; nobody in the chain trusts anyone.

**Q4 (SA).** What was Atlas and why was it a questionable acquisition?
**A:** Microsoft's creaky ad server (ran only on certain Internet Explorer versions; 20% share) bought for roughly the $0.1B left after Microsoft wrote down $6.1B of its $6.2B aQuantive purchase. "It's not what you pay, it's what they cost you": two-plus years of delays.

**Q5 (SA).** What does "do something vs. be someone" mean?
**A:** (Via Col. Boyd) One road is impact, truth to your vision and management's scorn; the other is plum products, facile praise and forgettable work. The author chose the first despite children's tuition riding on unvested stock.

**Q6 (SA).** Why are middle managers what they are?
**A:** Negative selection: the ambitious leave; the rest master managing up, acting visibly on feedback, championing reports, allying with sales, routing around incompetence, and banking the logo on the business card.

**Q7 (SA).** List the exit-playbook steps.
**A:** Don't quit before the vest; take vacation over the vesting date; clear your desk over the weekend; secure-erase personal files; expect a severance-for-non-disparagement offer ($30,000 for a year of silence) and decline to sign on the spot; keep the badge and phone "at home."

## Chapter 14: Pandemonium Lost / Epilogue

**Q1 (SA).** How did the outsider's blog post work as an "insider's mouthpiece"?
**A:** Executives in an acquisition can't explain strategy publicly, but they can retweet a perceptive outsider. The Medium post on Twitter-MoPub was circulated by the deal's principals, reopening a door slammed two years earlier. Lesson in the meeting: "if in doubt, show more."

**Q2 (SA).** Give the two structural reasons mobile News Feed ads saved Facebook.
**A:** (1) Data: mobile browsers block third-party cookies and apps silo data, so even age/gender was valuable ("in the land of the mobile data blind, the one-eyed Facebook man was king"). (2) Inventory: mobile's savvy monetizers charged users directly, leaving exchanges with tiny bars, interstitials and Grindr; Facebook's in-feed units had no real competition.

**Q3 (MC).** "Which of the ten dice came up?" is the book's way to:
a) Pick a product  b) Discount any founder's retrospective genius: try ten things, seven fail, two do OK, one transforms for reasons found afterward  c) Measure risk  d) Allocate headcount
**A:** b. The press then writes the narrative fallacy.

**Q4 (SA).** What happened to FBX?
**A:** About $500M by early 2014, all incremental, built by a handful and now costing nothing: a "perpetual motion machine of cash" and Facebook's window into programmatic competitors. Kept alive because too profitable to kill, starved of identity and mobile. Prediction: Facebook will eventually run a full exchange (even Goldman moved CDS clearing to ICE in 2013).

**Q5 (T/F).** The author profited hugely from his Facebook stock. **A:** False. Restricted stock was taxed as income at a $38 basis, then a lockout expired near $20, leaving "practically nothing" ("paying taxes on money I hadn't made"), while the AdGrok engineers' Twitter options made them rich.

**Q6 (SA).** Name the two acquisitions cited as Facebook "buying your way out."
**A:** Instagram (2012, $1B) and WhatsApp (2014, $19B).

**Q7 (SA).** What did the author do after finishing the book?
**A:** After his mother's death (summer 2014) he sold everything, finished the book, and outfitted *Ayala*, a forty-foot cutter, to sail around the world alone.

# Cumulative Final Exam (10 questions)

**F1 (SA).** What do a credit-default swap and an ad have in common, and why did Goldman (and later Facebook) resist transparent markets?
**A:** Both price a perception (CDS: probability of non-payment; ad: a slice of attention). Incumbents with price-making power and information asymmetry prefer owning a small market to participating in a large transparent one, which only invites competition.

**F2 (MC).** Which idea needs the fewest miracles?
a) Sponsored Stories  b) AdGrok's original bar-code idea  c) A laundromat  d) A one-miracle startup like Airbnb
**A:** c (zero). Sponsored Stories needed two, AdGrok's first plan five, Airbnb one.

**F3 (SA).** Compute: a $100K note has a $3M cap. What share does it convert to, and why do founders care about the number?
**A:** 3.3% regardless of the A's price. Dilution is the enemy; the higher the cap, the less you sell for the same cash.

**F4 (SA).** Name the two weak points used to beat Adchemy and the two people who activated them.
**A:** Investors (Paul Graham threatening to blacklist Adchemy's VCs from Demo Day) and potential partners (Sam Altman getting Microsoft's BD to flag the litigation).

**F5 (T/F).** In an acqui-hire, the acquirer cares how the money is split between founders and investors. **A:** False. It targets price per head and is indifferent; founders decide what investors get. Founders own nothing until they vest.

**F6 (SA).** Give two "tells" from the notes and what each signals.
**A:** Any two: "stealth"/NDA demand = naive founder; a counterpart calling back within an hour = weak hand; first-ever reminder of a one-on-one = HR is in the room; Google's parking lot empty on Sunday = they're not fighting; a prospect's hesitant headshake = will move if pushed.

**F7 (SA).** Why was Facebook's own social data worth less than outside data, and what evidence did the book use?
**A:** Noisy-bar metaphor (people rarely say anything of commercial intent); Project Chorizo (no user data improved performance); FBX outside bidders beat Facebook's own data in its own auction; what embarrasses you is worthless to advertisers, who want purchases and browsing.

**F8 (MC).** Which is the correct reading of a Facebook-style IPO?
a) A big first-day pop is a triumph  b) A pop means bankers and their clients won; a flat open with ~1% fees is the real win  c) IPOs are always underpriced for employees' benefit  d) Fees were up to 7%
**A:** b.

**F9 (SA).** Contrast Custom Audiences and FBX on incrementality, openness and what happened on April 12, 2013.
**A:** CA: closed, creaky, roughly half its dashboard dollars non-incremental, paired with the Atlas ad server. FBX: open, standard, built in five weeks by three engineers, fully incremental. Facebook chose closed on April 12, 2013 (Boz's recommendation to Sheryl).

**F10 (SA).** What actually saved Facebook, and what general lesson does the author draw about narratives of success?
**A:** Mobile News Feed ads, for structural reasons (no third-party cookies, siloed apps, weak mobile inventory) nobody argued about at the time. Lesson: try ten things, one wins for reasons discovered afterward, and a narrative is written later ("Man plans and God laughs"); being right about the technology isn't the same as being right about what saves the quarter.
