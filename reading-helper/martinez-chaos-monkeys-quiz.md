# Chaos Monkeys: Quiz Book

**Book:** *Chaos Monkeys: Obscene Fortune and Random Failure in Silicon Valley* by Antonio García Martínez.

> **Source note.** This quiz reflects the `martinez-chaos-monkeys` skill's chapter notes (SKILL.md, glossary, patterns, cheatsheet and the 14 chapter files), **not the book's own wording**. Nothing here comes from outside those notes. Where the notes are silent, the quiz is silent. The notes group the book's 46 sections into 14 chapters, so "Chapter" below means the notes' chapter, not necessarily the book's.

## How to use this quiz

1. Work one chapter at a time, right after you read it (or its notes). Cover the answers; write or say your answer before looking.
2. Question types are marked: **[SA]** short answer, **[T/F]** true/false, **[MC]** multiple choice, **[Own words]** explain in your own words, **[Scenario]** apply a concept to a situation.
3. Score yourself: 2 points for a correct answer with the reason, 1 point for the right idea without the reason, 0 otherwise. Re-quiz anything below 60% after a day.
4. Finish with the 20-question cumulative final exam at the end (it includes cross-chapter synthesis questions).
5. Numbers are period data (events 2007-2016) as given in the notes; learn them as evidence, not as current facts.

## Chapter 1: Prologue, The Undertakers of Capitalism, The Human Attention Exchange

**Q1.1 [SA]** What is the "price on perception" idea that opens the book, and what is the only thing that changes between a credit-default swap and an ad?

**Answer:** A CDS prices the perceived probability that a corporation will not pay; an ad prices the perceived value of a slice of attention. It is the same quant job, and "only the scale of the money pile changes." This is why Wall Street skills map onto ad tech.

**Q1.2 [MC]** Why is a credit-default swap "not like car insurance"? (a) it costs more; (b) anyone can buy or write protection on someone else's "car," including the thief; (c) it is regulated by exchanges; (d) it pays out only on theft.

**Answer:** (b). Because outsiders (even the would-be thief) can buy and write protection, the asymmetry makes credit derivatives dangerous. Related example: the "derivative" as a signed slip of paper with no intrinsic worth, value derived from something else, highly levered (worth zero or thousands).

**Q1.3 [SA]** Give the SLK numbers and the generalization Martínez draws from them.

**Answer:** Goldman's acquired broker SLK went from hundreds of traders and two programmers (2000) to two traders and twenty quants with fast machines (2007). Generalization (via Andreessen): there will be two job types, people who tell computers what to do and people told by computers what to do. Finance saw it first; ad tech (RTB) is next, then Uber/Airbnb/Instacart.

**Q1.4 [T/F]** Goldman moved credit-default swaps onto exchanges because a bigger transparent market was good business. 

**Answer:** False. Goldman declined: with few players, total information asymmetry and price-making power, transparency only invites competition. "Goldman felt more comfortable owning a small market than merely participating in a larger one," a pattern Martínez says he saw again at Facebook.

**Q1.5 [Own words]** Explain why incumbents tend to keep markets inefficient, and name the rule of thumb that follows.

**Answer:** If you make the price and hold information others lack, a transparent, larger market mostly creates rivals. So a dominant player prefers owning a small market to participating in a large one. Rule: expect a dominant player to choose asymmetry over openness (Goldman/CDS in Ch 1, Facebook/FBX later).

**Q1.6 [SA]** What decisions came out of the April 13, 2012 Zuck meeting, and how did Martínez describe how such decisions get made?

**Answer:** Facebook's IPO was imminent, revenue was plateauing and Open Graph/Sponsored Stories were a market failure. Of three proposals, the Like-button browsing data idea got a no and retargeting got "yes to retargeting, but no to using social plugins." Decisions are "made on gut feel, the residue of whatever historical politics were in play, and the ability to cater persuasive messages to people either busy, impatient, or uninterested."

**Q1.7 [SA]** Distinguish direct response from brand advertising, and explain why programmatic buying shifted power toward advertisers with first-party data.

**Answer:** Direct response has a short attention-to-action window and is trackable; brand has a long window, offline conversion and huge budgets spent in "embarrassingly large broadsides." Programmatic buying (Right Media, 2007) moved power from publishers who own eyeballs to advertisers who hold what you bought and browsed; Amazon's data beats "35-year-old male in Ohio." Only Google and Facebook resist being arbitraged by owning the tech between themselves and the dollars.

**Q1.8 [Worked example]** The Friday ID lottery: the pot is $2,000 and ten IDs remain. What is fair value per ID, what did Martínez observe, and what is the lesson?

**Answer:** Fair value is $200 per ID. IDs sold above that, and the premium rose as fewer remained (irrational overbidding for large, likelier payouts, plus a structural push from Friday cash). Lesson: markets deviate from fair value in predictable, exploitable directions when payouts are large and participants face outside pressures.

**Q1.9 [Scenario]** A founder is cornered by a regulator or counterpart who holds all the cards. Which Chapter 1 concept applies and what would you do?

**Answer:** "Negotiate from weakness" (the DUI gambit). At 0.91 blood alcohol, Martínez offered the officer a taxi under observation instead of forcing a station blood test, reframing the encounter from castigation to haggling. Lesson: "if you want to be a startup entrepreneur, get used to negotiating from positions of weakness." Reframe judgment into a trade the other side can say yes to.

**Q1.10 [Scenario]** A social-media company in 2011 has a year of posts, check-ins, Likes and friend graphs and believes it is sitting on a gold mine. Which Chapter 1 idea warns against this, and what asset does the chapter say wins instead?

**Answer:** "Facebook data isn't that valuable" / mistaking a rich data store for commercially useful data. A year of ingesting such data barely moved monetization. The asset that beats publisher demographics is advertiser first-party data (purchases and browsing). Facebook learned this "painfully in 2011."

## Chapter 2: Knowing How to Swim, Abandoning the Shipwreck

**Q2.1 [SA]** List the four signs of a doomed startup.

**Answer:** (1) Only the founder survives (the Team page purged "like a Stalin photo"). (2) It makes lots of money from something other than the product (Adchemy's ~$6M/month lead-gen revenue). (3) No clients for the actual product (new beta logos each quarter are just the sales team's churned lead list). (4) Loyal employees despite mistreatment (Stockholm syndrome; Murthy broke the author's finger with a baseball and mocked him for months, yet staff bought stock).

**Q2.2 [SA]** State the miracle count with an example of each category.

**Answer:** Zero miracles = a laundromat, not a startup. One = most successful startups (Airbnb: user behavior; Google: technical; Uber/Instacart: consumer workflow; Slack: business workflow; consumer apps: the usage lottery). Two or more = "the classic sign of a shitty startup idea." The team's bar-code-to-local-ad idea needed five.

**Q2.3 [T/F]** A pivot is a deliberate, graceful ballerina's turn toward a better market.

**Answer:** False. A pivot is "a panicked sprint" for the last life raft; their product ended as "Plan J."

**Q2.4 [Own words]** Why does Martínez treat a cash cow unrelated to the product as a bad sign rather than a runway?

**Answer:** It works like a sedative or trust fund that makes "a drug addict's spiral more long lasting": it hides product failure and lets the company keep going without ever finding a customer for the real product. Adchemy's lead-gen revenue is the example.

**Q2.5 [MC]** What does Y Combinator judge when it interviews? (a) the idea; (b) the team, using the idea as a probe; (c) the demo; (d) the revenue.

**Answer:** (b). "Ideas are worthless; teams aren't." Claiming "stealth" or demanding an NDA is a "LOSER" tattoo: "if your idea is any good, it won't get stolen, you'll have to jam it down people's throats."

**Q2.6 [SA]** How did the YC application and interview go (dates, tactics, mistakes)?

**Answer:** March 1-29, 2010: deadline March 3 noticed while procrastinating on PG essays; two days at Peet's writing the application ("Goldman Sachs of advertising"; the Craigslist man-in-the-middle hack); a demo was built; they took the last interview slot on the last day (the "first or last on the list" gambit); a ten-minute interruption-filled interview with four partners and no demo reached; then PG called and the author fumbled with "I should probably ask my cofounder first," the wrong answer, because commitment should be decided before the offer. MRM was convinced by phone and the acceptance came by voicemail.

**Q2.7 [SA]** Why is H-1B status described as indentured servitude, and how did Adchemy use it?

**Answer:** Visa holders can't quit without risking deportation; big slow companies and consultancies profit from the artificial monopoly, and startups often won't sponsor. Adchemy's immigrant executives threatened to report their former employee to ICE for planning to work on an F-2 ("former slaves turned slave owners"); the notes identify Argyris as the non-citizen cofounder and say the immigrant cofounder bears the worst of leaving. The author also made an error by blurting the legal workaround to the party that wanted to hurt them.

**Q2.8 [Own words]** What is the "creed" of Silicon Valley capitalism and what is it good for?

**Answer:** Fourteen lines (paraphrased in the notes): investors have more money than time; employees the reverse; entrepreneurs are the seductive go-betweens; startups are experiments with other people's money; only losers pay for marketing; culture is what goes without saying; no rules, only laws; success forgives all sins; people who leak to you leak about you; meritocracy is propaganda; greed and vanity are the twin engines; most managers survive on inertia and politics; lawsuits are expensive feints; capitalism is an amoral farce in which everyone is complicit. Use: read it before you believe a mission statement.

**Q2.9 [Scenario]** You are considering a job at a startup. The Team page lists only the CEO, revenue is strong from an unrelated side business, the customer slide changes every quarter, and employees defend the boss despite public humiliations. Which Chapter 2 tool applies and what is the call?

**Answer:** The four signs of a doomed startup. Three or four are present; the notes say "Don't work for the survivors or architects of massacres" and treat retention pressure as confirmation to leave. Check CrunchBase/LinkedIn for who left.

**Q2.10 [Scenario]** A friend pitches you an idea that requires users to change a habit, retailers to integrate, and a new device to be adopted. What do you say?

**Answer:** Count the miracles: that is three or more, "the classic sign of a shitty startup idea." The idea might still be probed through the team, but a one-miracle reframing is needed before committing.

## Chapter 3: War Face, Like Marriage, Speed Is a Feature, D-Day

**Q3.1 [SA]** What does YC actually sell, according to the notes?

**Answer:** (1) Access to partners; (2) the YC-founder network, a private microcosm in which you can rebuild ~80% of the consumer/infrastructure stack from YC companies, with discounts, preferential adoption and pitch-deck logos; (3) bankable prestige; (4) Demo Day. Not "the goop on rice."

**Q3.2 [SA]** How does Google's auction work and what is ROAS?

**Answer:** An auction on every query: bid times estimated click probability, highest product wins. Google makes $70B this way. ROAS = revenue divided by cost, minus 1 (the "nevada cheap divorce" example: 47%). The priciest word was "mesothelioma" at ~$90/click; Google is "the bouncer at the door" of every internet business.

**Q3.3 [T/F]** The SMB (Mom-and-Pop) last mile is an easy market because small businesses will try anything.

**Answer:** False, and that is the trap: they try anything and cancel just as fast; at $50-100/month high-touch sales can't scale. Clickable ($32M), Lexity ($6M) and Trada ($19M) all died trying. Partner with someone who owns the SMB relationship or build on a platform that has them.

**Q3.4 [SA]** What went wrong with the three-way equal-equity founding team, and what are the two valid alternatives?

**Answer:** Equal thirds produced decision paralysis and rotating two-against-one factions. Alternatives: a "Vulcan mind-meld" forged by shared hard experience, or one person with 51%+. The only early title that matters is CEO; startups are pirate ships with egalitarian spoils and one captain. PG diagnosed the leadership gap in ten minutes of a walk; about six of thirty batch companies had disputed leadership and crumbled or had coups.

**Q3.5 [Own words]** "Every real startup problem is a people problem." Explain, with the Ralston line.

**Answer:** Technical problems are easy unless at the edge of knowledge; "startups are experiments in group psychology." Geoff Ralston: "people don't really change, they just become better actors." AdGrok's eventual undoing was the founders' natures, not an enemy.

**Q3.6 [Worked example]** Walk through the New York post launch.

**Answer:** Trigger: Ron Conway and PG praised New York's startup scene at dinner. Thesis: New York's economy is monopolies of information (banks, agents, rental brokers) that go unchallenged, while San Francisco builds Craigslist and makes the pimp obsolete. Execution: two nights writing, a Tuesday 9 a.m. post to Hacker News, friends upvote, #1 within minutes, a Scoble tweet, the single server melts, MRM clones the blog across new AWS machines, traffic jumps from about a dozen views/day to 50,000, Business Insider and 20/20 call; the Goldman post is held in reserve for Monday's attention decay.

**Q3.7 [MC]** Why Tuesday morning for a launch post? (a) Monday is too groggy, Thursday drifting, Friday for burying news; (b) Tuesday has the most servers; (c) HN is closed other days; (d) investors read on Tuesday only.

**Answer:** (a). Also: pick the biggest nerve, seed upvotes, reply to fans, egg on trolls, double down when it works, and have servers ready. Result for AdGrok: a viral post every 3-4 weeks.

**Q3.8 [SA]** Where does the book's title come from and what is the metaphor?

**Answer:** Netflix's chaos monkey randomly kills servers to test resilience. Entrepreneurs are "society's chaos monkeys" (Uber vs. medallions, Airbnb vs. hotels, Tinder vs. dating), fed by VC "bananas"; the open question is whether society survives them and at what human cost.

**Q3.9 [Scenario]** Your three cofounders are equal partners and have deadlocked on a bet-the-company decision. Which concept applies and what do you do?

**Answer:** "Have a leader." Equal equity as "fairness" is fatal for such decisions. Either a genuine mind-meld or one person with 51%+ and the CEO title; if it cannot be settled, don't start (or restructure early, before the coups).

**Q3.10 [Scenario]** You will present at Demo Day in two weeks: 2.5 minutes, around 30 companies. What do the notes advise?

**Answer:** Pitch the business, not a demo (investors care about the business); rehearse until the script haunts your dreams; expect two rehearsals (batch, then alumni); expect hours of "nut-to-butt" networking afterward. Anti-pattern: demoing when you should pitch. Also recall "This too shall pass" (Rapportive's polished Prototype Day pitch dismayed him, and two years later both companies had the same acqui-hire exit).

## Chapter 4: Conclave of Angels, Hill of Sand, Widening Gyre, ¡No Pasarán!

**Q4.1 [SA]** Contrast the old-school angel (Russ Siegelman) with the micro-VC (Chris Sacca).

**Answer:** Russ invests his own money, wants 2-4x, wants it back if you're imploding, does haphazard due diligence through friends and is committed. Sacca runs a $20-40M fund ("a fund of bosses"), needs outsized returns (the Ch 4 notes say 100x outliers; Ch 6 and the summary say 10x) and supplies a gold-plated name that makes weak-kneed investors follow; he said "I'm in!" in half an hour.

**Q4.2 [SA]** What is a convertible note and what does a cap do? Give the arithmetic example.

**Answer:** Seed money is nominal "debt" that converts to equity at the next round. The cap sets a maximum valuation for conversion: $100K at a $3M cap = 3.3% even if the Series A prices at $10M. Without a cap, the angel's stake shrinks as the company does better (1% at $10M, 0.1% at $100M).

**Q4.3 [T/F]** A cap is the company's valuation.

**Answer:** False. Founders bandy caps as valuations but a cap is "an input to a hypothetical calculation," a proxy.

**Q4.4 [MC]** YC 2010 cap benchmarks: (a) $1M/$2M/$3M; (b) $3-4M middle, $6M good, ~$8M stellar (Hipmunk); (c) $10M/$20M/$30M; (d) no caps existed.

**Answer:** (b). AdGrok discussed $4M and closed at $2M after the lawsuit, selling about 22% for about $500K from four investors.

**Q4.5 [SA]** How do you cut through VC hierarchy?

**Answer:** Titles are noise; what matters is carry and whether the person can present and defend a deal at the Monday partner meeting. Ask "Do you have decision-making power?"; any hedge means a lackey; route around them. The meeting runs four to five hours; yeses arrive that evening, nos drift in over the week or never.

**Q4.6 [Own words]** Describe the Death Clock and what Martínez did with it.

**Answer:** A cash-burn spreadsheet including legal costs (Fenwick's estimate: ~$500K to reach trial, ~$1.5M total over 18 months, ~$20K/month) in which every version crashed into the ground. He hid it from cofounders and investors and "cooked the books": lower legal costs, an impossible launch date, an "unconscionable" growth rate. The lie held.

**Q4.7 [SA]** Explain the Fenwick deal as a "mispriced option."

**Answer:** Fenwick fronted $250K of fees as a loan for a small equity slice plus options. If AdGrok died, nothing was repaid; if it won, the company could buy the equity back at the seed price with no prepayment penalty: "a one-in-a-million lottery ticket to win $250,000... for $250,000." Cash-paying solo lawyers at $400/hour would have cost more than $700/hour partners who take equity ("cash is the poor man's credit"). The French-mathematician gambit: tell the lawyers the investors are in, tell the investors legal costs are minimal because lawyers take equity.

**Q4.8 [SA]** What were the "forgotten crimes" and why do they matter?

**Answer:** Gates cloned Kildall's QDOS and kept the copyright; Jobs pocketed the Atari bonus and paid Wozniak $350; Zuckerberg ran with the idea he was hired to build. Balzac: "The secret of great fortunes without apparent cause is a forgotten crime." Thesis: there are no real rules, only laws, and success forgives all sins.

**Q4.9 [Scenario]** A lawsuit lands the week your seed round is closing. How do you handle your lead investor and the paperwork?

**Answer:** Disclose only when pens hover (a lawsuit is an "HIV positive" T-shirt), but price it into terms: Russ stayed but cut the cap from $4M to $2M and required $200K more from others; ~22% was sold (a future Series A problem, "worrying about cholesterol while dying of cancer"). Use the underdog story: incumbents' employees are mercenaries, brands are fragile, America loves an underdog.

**Q4.10 [Scenario]** A VC at a big firm says "I love it, let me take it to the team." You have a competing deadline. What do you check?

**Answer:** Ask "Do you have decision-making power?" and route around any hedge. Remember Sequoia's polite pass citing "white space" with a competing investment (Kenshoo): "a truth that is also a polite lie"; the masterful are polite and prompt, while "it's the incompetent and insecure who are generally the assholes." Also do not smirk at hypothetical exit questions (Bill Tai's "would you sell for $50M?" cost a deal and "would prove prescient").

## Chapter 5: Dog Shit Sandwich, Victory, Launching!

**Q5.1 [SA]** What are the two weak points of any tech company and why?

**Answer:** (1) Investors: they hold board votes and personal sway, and a CEO who serves at the board's pleasure wears a leash. (2) Potential business partners: enterprise deals and partnerships are the revenue lifeblood, and "even large companies with billions in capital fear public shaming and legal quagmires."

**Q5.2 [Own words]** Why was "deal flow" the key to PG's power in 2010?

**Answer:** The 2010 shift (angel glut, $20-40M micro-funds, YC, AWS, Rails, "shovel" startups like Parse) made seeds look like old A rounds; pro-rata rights meant later-round access depended on being in early. YC controlled "the water sluice," so big VCs had to kowtow to attend Demo Day, giving PG a credible sanction.

**Q5.3 [SA]** What is "reward friends, punish enemies" and how did PG apply it?

**Answer:** "To have influence in the world, you need to be willing and able to reward your friends and punish your enemies." PG went to Wilson Sonsini on a bicycle, emailed Murthy, then planned to anathematize Adchemy's VCs (August, Mayfield): disinvite them from Demo Day and steer founders elsewhere. YC blacklists investors who cross its companies regardless of fund size.

**Q5.4 [SA]** What was PG's "firewall" instruction and why?

**Answer:** "Come alone... keep them out of it." Don't let lawsuits or money trouble become the internal narrative; the team adds no value and loses productivity. The author did not even tell the boys about PG's plan in case it failed.

**Q5.5 [SA]** What did Sam Altman do and what was the effect?

**Answer:** Found by LinkedIn-stalking for a two-hop connector to the adversary's critical partner. Altman ("Manson Lamps"; PG: "parachute him into an island full of cannibals... he'd be the king") got Microsoft BD to tell Adchemy litigation was "problematic" for the Bing deal (a $50M-scale deal and Murthy's "last business bet"): "the decapitated horse's head in Murthy's bed." Adchemy's tone flipped from menace to conciliation; dismissal offered late October, settled February 2011.

**Q5.6 [Worked example]** Summarize "knife to balls, October 2010" week by week.

**Answer:** Week 1: an employee's drunken leak reveals the Microsoft deal; LinkedIn search lands on Altman. Week 2: PG's email thread with Murthy; a Saturday summons to Old Palo Alto; PG's plan to cut Adchemy's VCs from deal flow. Week 3: Altman calls from the 280; Microsoft BD raised AdGrok in a meeting with Adchemy; meanwhile Fenwick's litigator (the Undertaker) offers audited code and starts depositions; Dalal is seen dressing down Murthy. Late October: unilateral offer to dismiss. Pattern: identify dependencies, activate loyal power brokers, make the fight cost the aggressor more than the target.

**Q5.7 [MC]** According to Martínez what is scarce in founders? (a) intelligence; (b) technical skill; (c) vision; (d) monomaniacal focus plus endurance of endless shit.

**Answer:** (d). Focus can't be taught (he watched his daughter grow up "through a Skype window"); endurance is learnable ("ride a bicycle across the United States, sail a boat across an ocean, or join the Marines"). And "it helps to have enemies... hate and fear last longer" than love.

**Q5.8 [SA]** Draw the Trough of Despair and say who it kills.

**Answer:** The YC whiteboard curve: launch spike, crash, long flatline, slight rise, dip (from "misconfigured analytics code"), steady climb to acquisition. The chasm after launch is where you see the product is "misshapen and misbegotten"; it kills 90%+ of the startups that survived founder fights, shipping and fund-raising.

**Q5.9 [Scenario]** A competitor sues you and your team is panicking. What three-front plan do the notes imply?

**Answer:** Treat it as a three-front war: court (slowest), cap table (their investors) and pipeline (their partners). Firewall your team; attack dependencies rather than the claims; use "who needs what from whom?" (Murthy needed Microsoft's money and his VCs' patience more than AdGrok dead). Anti-pattern: letting the crisis into the team's headspace.

**Q5.10 [Scenario]** Your product launched with a PR spike and now traffic is flat. What does the chapter tell you to expect and to avoid?

**Answer:** Expect the Trough of Despair; avoid believing the launch spike. Product-market fit is iterative convergence from an approximately correct start, each iteration costing wages, servers and time; when cash hits zero you lose; fit means users pay more than they cost to serve and acquire. AdGrok's launch (March 7, 2011) was PR-agency-driven ("like losers, we paid for it") with a small sign-up wave.

## Chapter 6: Dates @Twitter, Acquisition Chicken, Getting Liked, Getting Poked

**Q6.1 [Own words]** What does "know them better than their mothers do" mean in practice, and what can a CV tell you?

**Answer:** Before any meeting, do a full Facebook-Twitter-LinkedIn stalk: tenure gaps (under 1 year = didn't vest, someone sucked; exactly 4 years = a plodder), mutual connections, where they eat (taqueria ramen-eaters with nothing to lose are dangerous; Benu/Saison regulars are harmless tools), lifestyle (whale-digested commitment vs. bike-ride/date-night bourgeois), and origin (Chevy Chase/"Prep" = slow-and-steady social-capital accruers; backwater chutzpah = fearsome). "Know what they want... and you can predict 90 percent of what they eventually do."

**Q6.2 [SA]** What is the "always be closing" lesson from the lunch with Adam Bain?

**Answer:** The author had no laptop at lunch when Bain asked "what are you working on?" and was saved by MRM's hosted demo, runnable from any machine. Lesson: build the demo that works on someone else's laptop; anti-pattern: showing up without a demo.

**Q6.3 [SA]** Describe the two deal channels and the "dragoman."

**Answer:** A formal channel (email, documents) and a clandestine one (phone, in person, no records; recording calls is illegal in California). The dragoman layer (mis)translates between parties. "If you're still emailing about it, there's actually nothing real going on." Insiders trade information for scores or private motives; in public markets this would be indictable, in tech nobody regulates it. A composite source, Deep Tweet (Epicenter Café's back door to Twitter), leaked Twitter's internals.

**Q6.4 [SA]** What is a no-shop and what must you do before it lands?

**Answer:** A clause in the term sheet, typically 60 days and enforceable, pausing solicitation of other acquirers. Create the auction first: get every plausible acquirer "in play" (the magic phrase with Facebook, via a YC batchmate turned FB PM). Note: "Fiduciary duty... means they are granting themselves moral license to screw someone."

**Q6.5 [MC]** What was the Google bluff? (a) claiming Google had offered $50M; (b) gesturing at the Google keywords on screen and saying "we want to be part of the future, not the past" when Facebook's corp-dev chief asked who else was bidding; (c) naming Microsoft; (d) refusing to answer.

**Answer:** (b). It worked because Amin Zoufonoun, three days out of Google, had no back channel there; "Everybody in tech feared Google." Rule: pick the name everyone fears and check whether the counterpart can verify it.

**Q6.6 [SA]** How were acqui-hires priced and why was Twitter's first offer "way too cheap"?

**Answer:** Valley engineers fetched $0.5-2M each in four-year vesting equity (salary just covers rent). Twitter's verbal "$5 million thing" (March 29) for three hires plus IP and YC bling was way too cheap against a pre-lawsuit $4M cap and fresh YC seeds above $5M.

**Q6.7 [Own words]** Why do VCs push you to reject a good-enough offer?

**Answer:** Angels investing their own money (Russ) take 2x happily; fund managers (Sacca) need 10x because funds are fire-and-forget, not compounding, so they sneer at "lifestyle businesses." Use the VC as the cudgel when you want the team to reject a low offer. Caveat from the chapter: wimping out on the call so the VC plays bad cop invited Sacca's angry texts.

**Q6.8 [SA]** Describe the Facebook interview day's structure and the five PM archetypes.

**Answer:** April 1: Alon Amit, Rohit Dhawan, Jared Morgenstern (design a music app; explain shake-to-pair), Justin Shaffer (VC-style interrogation), Gokul. Every wanted employee passes the standard hiring screen; the bundle is your union ("don't like the price, we're all off the table"). Get the interviewer list a day early and stalk each one; prepare two or three plausible answers and pick by reading the interviewer's "local version of truth." Archetypes: designer, analyst (Fermi estimate, planes in the sky), old-timer, culture ("FoZ" arrogance) and the domain boss (Gokul: "What's the first thing you would change?" - "a conversion-tracking system," since an ads system without one "is like a car without even rear or side windows").

**Q6.9 [Scenario]** A big company's corp dev says they love your team and want to talk. Another potential acquirer has not responded. What do you do over the next week?

**Answer:** Parallelize and create an auction before any no-shop: reach Google (the March case got silence), Microsoft (remote pitch), Facebook ("in play"). Treat phone calls as yes, email as no. PG's counter-warning: most "offers" are market intelligence conducted on founders' time, so shield the team. The ten days in March show the pattern: lunch 17th, Weil floats acquisition 22nd, NDA/Orrick 25th, Deep Tweet "create an auction" 27th, Twitter's $5M 29th, Facebook pitch 30th.

**Q6.10 [Scenario]** Your lead investor also owns a big stake in the likely buyer. How should you run the process?

**Answer:** Do not let the sell-side investor with a stake in the buyer run your process: Sacca was a major Twitter holder (reportedly helped JPMorgan buy 10% at $21/share) and "a man who only ever talked his book" (his email: "No fucking way should you engage on this offer."). The author chose not to tell Sacca about Facebook. Also an anti-pattern: hiring by "cultural fit," which in practice excludes the happy-hour buzzkill, the quiet Indian or Chinese engineer and the self-taught kid from a no-name college.

## Chapter 7: Forking Paths, Retweets, The Dotted Line, Endgame

**Q7.1 [SA]** What is the Whereoscope template (Mick Johnson)?

**Answer:** Shop the company to Twitter, Zynga, Google and Facebook; when two bidders each want only part, force them into the same room to fight; accept a Frankenstein of cash, dual vesting schedules and an investor side-deal. Big companies "bid for what they wanted... 'We want A and B, but not C, and we don't care about the tech'" and leave founders to double-deal.

**Q7.2 [SA]** How does acqui-hire consideration work, and who decides the investor share?

**Answer:** The acquirer targets price per high-value person and is indifferent to the founder/investor split. The headline "sold for $X million" may be 10% company price and 90% signing bonuses and vesting for founders. Twitter's default term sheet gave investors ~$1M of ~$10M instead of the ~$2M pro rata. Founders face a zero-sum moral choice: reward investors out of their own wallets or accept the reputational cost. Anti-pattern: assuming pro rata.

**Q7.3 [T/F]** Founders own their equity from day one, so a founder can walk mid-deal and keep everything.

**Answer:** False. Founders' 20-40% stakes vest like employees' so a departing founder can't hold a gun to the company; ten months in, "nobody at AdGrok actually owned anything." Vesting governs every exit decision.

**Q7.4 [SA]** What is a replicating portfolio and what was Facebook's offer?

**Answer:** Demanding the new employer match your slice of the other deal. Facebook's offer at ~$32/share pre-IPO came to about $2.3M, roughly a 25% AdGrok stake after the split.

**Q7.5 [Own words]** What is the "call station" insight about Twitter?

**Answer:** After the walkout Twitter should have gone silent for days and bought AdGrok "for a twenty-dollar Starbucks gift card"; instead it called back within an hour above the original price ($5M+ for the two engineers, 2:00 p.m. after a noon announcement). Weak hands reveal themselves by speed; read the opponent's hand from their tempo.

**Q7.6 [SA]** What does "resign before you sign" mean?

**Answer:** An employment lawyer (a whole class exists to negotiate "the sale of yourself") advised accepting immediately and resigning first, or you breach both noncompetes. The 3:07 Caltrain, resignation email sent as corp dev opened the door.

**Q7.7 [Own words]** What is the "desengaño" and why present departure as fait accompli?

**Answer:** Confessing the lie before the moment of reckoning. Present your departure as a done decision, not a group one, to avoid a mutiny. The CEO had lied to cofounders for two weeks (April 5-18), helped them win Twitter's interviews, then confessed April 14 and walked at the April 18 meeting where Satya Patel asked "So you're telling us you're bailing on the deal?"

**Q7.8 [SA]** What was the "master play" Martínez did not make and why not?

**Answer:** Negotiate both offers in secret, front-load Twitter with single-trigger acceleration or up-front cash, accept, quit day one, walk to Facebook (Twitter too embarrassed to sue). Impossible without deceiving cofounders, "a step too far out on the prick spectrum." Rule: lie to investors and acquirers if you must; deceiving cofounders is the line; "morality... is an expensive hobby."

**Q7.9 [Scenario]** Your startup has stalled: no shipping for a month after launch, your technical cofounder checked out. A bigger company shows interest. What concept applies?

**Answer:** "When the engine dies, sell": "the dev team is the engine of a tech company. If they were done, then we were dead in the water." Also: "AdGrok was simply a long, stressful job interview"; corp dev = "HR with a fatter checkbook"; AdGrok "was merely leverage to score the job offers." Expect technical due diligence (possibly line by line) since AWS and off-the-shelf components let anyone fake a product.

**Q7.10 [Scenario]** You hold two offers and one counterparty phones to ask about your cap table. What does it mean and how do you respond?

**Answer:** Phone calls are yeses, emails are nos; a call about the cap table means they are already designing the founder/investor split. Respond by treating the acqui-hire as N separate job offers wrapped together: negotiate each head's price, demand a replicating portfolio from any alternative employer, decide the investor split knowing it comes from your own wallet, and accept the same day when counsel says so.

## Chapter 8: Boot Camp, Product Masseur, Google Delenda Est, Leaping Headlong

**Q8.1 [SA]** What is Chris Cox's description of Facebook and why does it matter?

**Answer:** Not a social network but "the New York Times of You," the personal newspaper: the teleological endpoint of media personalization, famous 24/7 to fifteen people rather than fifteen minutes to everyone.

**Q8.2 [Own words]** Explain "onboarding as Ellis Island" and Martínez's reaction.

**Answer:** New hires are told to shed prior culture (Pedram), "just fucking do it" (Chamath), and absorb truth, selfless collaboration, "don't worry about who gets credit" and ownership. Posters (MAKE AN IMPACT, DONE IS BETTER THAN PERFECT, FORTUNE FAVORS THE BOLD) and a blue T-shirt reinforce it. Martínez: "corporate fascism was intoxicating," an immortality project for a world without transcendent values, and he was seduced too. Think of onboarding as conversion, not orientation.

**Q8.3 [SA]** What is the PM's role as "shit umbrella / pirate captain"?

**Answer:** Internally everything but coding: legal, sales pitches, horse-trading with other PMs, fronting for management. "If you can convince engineers to build things you stipulate, you are golden," otherwise you are a government in exile. Externally, a two-line email summons any company's leadership, and the craven mistake Facebook's power for their own: power is borrowed from Facebook and from engineers' trust.

**Q8.4 [SA]** What does "monetization = data per pixel" mean and what did the revenue dashboard show?

**Answer:** Targeting is the alchemy that turns data into cash. The dashboard's big yellow number, average CPM, was bottom-of-the-barrel ("utter dog shit") while usage was "up there with heroin"; Facebook made money on volume (even through a 90-second-latency mobile app). A billion times any number is still big.

**Q8.5 [Own words]** How did Martínez characterize Zuckerberg, and why are true believers scarier than the greedy?

**Answer:** Not an autistic savant or a Jobsian product genius (Home, HTML5, first search, Paper) but an "old-school genius" of the Jefferson/Napoleon/Jim Jones type whose messianic vision converts followers: "have a mad vision, and you're a kook. Get a crowd to believe in it as well, and you're a leader." True believers can't be bought.

**Q8.6 [SA]** What triggered Lockdown, and what did "Carthago delenda est" signify?

**Answer:** Google Plus (June 2011, ad-free, subsidized by AdWords). The neon Lockdown sign and Zuck's impromptu speech framed it as zero-sum, raise the quality bar, and Cato's "Carthage must be destroyed." Cafés opened on weekends, families were invited, posters were printed by the Analog Research Laboratory, and a defected Google Plus PM briefed leadership the day it launched. Reconnaissance: Google's engineering lot was empty on a Sunday; Facebook's was full.

**Q8.7 [Worked example]** Walk through the hashtag decision in week one.

**Answer:** A dozen engineers and PMMs were deadlocked: replace keyword targeting with new topic "super-keywords," or add them alongside. The new PM's call: keep keywords (20% of revenue can't be migrated abruptly), denote topics with "#" (users already read #Golf as an abstract concept, drafting off Twitter), no UI redesign (design was always the bottleneck), launch fast, then have marketing push partners toward "#Action Movies." Kitten shipped in two months (launched August 1, 2011, no hitch); hundreds of thousands of advertisers adopted hashtagged segments. Pattern: preserve revenue, minimize scarce resources, launch now.

**Q8.8 [SA]** What is Project Chorizo and what did it find?

**Answer:** Systematic tests feeding every user action (posts, messages, links, check-ins) into topic targeting. Nothing improved advertiser performance: "A mile-long trainful of meat cows... one measly sausage." It tested his faith in Facebook's data primacy.

**Q8.9 [Scenario]** You are a new PM and your team is split on a launch design that would force users onto a new paradigm. Which concepts apply?

**Answer:** Be the tiebreaker; layer new features on old paradigms instead of forcing a new one; adopt a bias for launching ("very few companies have died due to launching early... countless companies have died by losing the nerve to ship"); and build a dashboard you know to the decimal, because "you make what you measure" (choose the line carefully since a good PM will drive it up and to the right).

**Q8.10 [Scenario]** A rival launches a threatening product. How do you measure whether your own company is at war, and what is Facebook's example?

**Answer:** Treat an empty parking lot as competitive intelligence: measure commitment by who shows up on Sunday. Also note companies are countries and people vote with their feet: Google's instant counteroffer policy for anyone with a Facebook offer created a rush to interview at Facebook for leverage. Anti-pattern: insular teams that don't know what they don't know.

## Chapter 9: One Shot One Kill, Twice Bitten, Ads Five-Oh, Narcissism of Privacy

**Q9.1 [SA]** Explain the noisy-bar metaphor.

**Answer:** Facebook is every bar and café on earth; the targeting PM has a transcript of every conversation with anonymous IDs and locations, and still not much commercial intent, because people rarely say anything a CMO cares about, "Obama" in an Alabama bar is usually preceded by an expletive, sarcasm is unparseable, and it must work in Tigrinya.

**Q9.2 [SA]** What is the "$100M bar" (revenue-impact threshold)?

**Answer:** On the ads side a new idea needs about 5% of revenue (~$100M/year then) to earn its keep; political-district targeting, a Hispanic cluster and travel-intender geodata all failed or were too small. Unsexy, programmatic, platform-wide changes (Kitten) beat whizzy niche ideas. "Go big or go home."

**Q9.3 [SA]** How did Facebook test ads and what is the New Zealand joke?

**Answer:** Users were the unit of account; ads tests dosed rich-market tranches like drug trials, measuring clickthrough, downstream conversion (rarely available) and usage in a zero-sum trade-off; engineers horse-traded test populations the size of countries when experimentation was capped. New Zealand ("Aotearoa... roughly translated means 'Facebook test set'") was the user-product sandbox.

**Q9.4 [Own words]** What is the PM's technical knowledge for?

**Answer:** To detect bullshit, not to design: sniff-test engineers' claims that are accidental (wishful memory), passive-aggressive ("that'll eat up all our servers") or lazy ("impossible"). A senior engineer's assurance about the Irish audit turned out wrong.

**Q9.5 [Worked example]** What happened on Christmas Eve 2011?

**Answer:** The Irish audit was settled with a discontinuation deadline that day. Despite the most authoritative ads engineer's assurance, a "paranoid itch" led the PM to open a terminal into production, with Hong Ge watching, and run an UPDATE on the live targeting tables behind ~$2B in spend, check a real-time dashboard, then have a beer. Lesson: "all the responsibility, none of the power"; verify what you personally vouched for, even on a holiday.

**Q9.6 [SA]** Explain the 2011 data-policy referendum.

**Answer:** Zuck's 2009 pledge that policy changes would be voted on with a 30% participation threshold (~300M people). 90% of voters opposed the new policy Facebook needed, but almost nobody voted, so the result was "taken under advisement." Without the new policy, the company-saving products of 2012-13 would have been impossible.

**Q9.7 [T/F]** Facebook sells your personal data to advertisers.

**Answer:** False in the notes' argument: Facebook buys data; it provides services that incentivize advertisers to let it ingest outside data, a "black hole of data that can never leave." Handing data out would be "suicidally stupid." Facebook is a telephone exchange: the advertiser addresses the message; blaming Facebook for a creepy ad is like blaming Gmail for spam.

**Q9.8 [MC]** Which data do advertisers actually want? (a) your secrets and sex life; (b) brownie recipes; (c) last night's Netflix pick, your Amazon cart, Best Buy browsing, your car's age, devices and browsers; (d) your political rants.

**Answer:** (c). What embarrasses you is commercially worthless. The 49ers-jersey anecdote (a journalist's "aha!") is explained by a game weekend and a broad campaign, not moon-landing-grade image recognition.

**Q9.9 [Scenario]** Your team proposes a clever niche targeting feature that would add 0.5% to revenue. What do you say?

**Answer:** It fails the revenue-impact threshold (about 5% at scale); prefer a programmatic, platform-wide change. Scale cuts against ads: any user-side launch reaches hundreds of millions, but ads need ~$100M/year to matter.

**Q9.10 [Scenario]** A journalist asks whether your safety team's wins should be publicized. What does the chapter suggest?

**Answer:** Anti-pattern: publicizing safety wins when the frame would backfire. Facebook stays quiet about the work (Scalps@Facebook, the trophy group of predators caught with law enforcement) because publicizing it would scare parents, and users treat a social utility "on par with running water" as a toy. Also remember privacy is like "Iran's nukes," a permanent cloud taking about a quarter of the PM's time, with data-policy changes written for the world's lawyers.

## Chapter 10: Are We Savages, O Death, Barbaric Yawn, Going Public

**Q10.1 [Own words]** Explain the blue-tape loop.

**Answer:** Bold experiment, fast failure, prune, internalize. Zuck let employees paint the new campus; two days of vandalism followed; he walked every hall with blue masking tape marking what came down; an engineer made "bluetape" a code-review macro meaning "remove this for sanity." Martínez calls the loop Facebook's core cultural mechanism, as central as the Like button.

**Q10.2 [SA]** Why did Facebook keep Sun's logos on its doors and sign?

**Answer:** As corporate memento mori: Sun ignored Linux on commodity hardware and went extinct. The red book's warning is that the internet grants irrelevant things no ruins, they simply disappear. It is death awareness "of the person planning on living forever": discipline against decay, not fear.

**Q10.3 [SA]** What is the logic of acquisitions as "cultural hybridization"?

**Answer:** Beyond recruiting, big companies buy startups to cross their obedient-graduate DNA with founder wildness (the cattle-dog analogy). The product almost always vanishes; acquirees succeed only with Zuck-granted latitude (Instagram) or by reining themselves in. First rule: act like you belong.

**Q10.4 [SA]** Explain "track racing to commuting."

**Answer:** After a startup a big company feels like crawling at 80 mph. Facebook was an autobahn (fast lane by Zuck's permission only), Apple/Google the 101 at rush hour, Oracle a Delhi intersection. The itch to leave starts within months; use speedometer recalibration to predict how long an acquired founder will stay.

**Q10.5 [Worked example]** Apply the vesting math to an offer.

**Answer:** Standard four-year vesting with 25% at one year then quarterly ("VIP" = "vesting in peace"). A startup founder won't last four years, so halve the offer's headline; halve again if it is stock rather than options (tax treatment). Martínez ran a countdown clock to his first vest.

**Q10.6 [Own words]** Why does Martínez call Facebook "the People's Republic" and how does it relate to wealth?

**Answer:** Capitalism and communism converge at extremes: endless toil for lapidary ideals from an unquestioned leader, a leadership caste chosen for adherence, flattering media, foot soldiers who sacrifice family and measure worth by advancement. Opting out is theoretical given Bay Area housing and tuition. Every sweeping ideology feeds on the founder's will to power plus the mass man's hunger for a story.

**Q10.7 [SA]** Who are the "haves and have-mores"?

**Answer:** Early employees with life-changing wealth beside later hires merely well paid; money is never discussed on campus; the NR250 group is a how-to for the new rich (LLCs for compounds, jets, Maui); the nobility "furiously denied their very existence" like fish unaware of water; 401(k) matching arrived late because "the IPO was the pension." Middle managers hired late knew the gap yet sided with owners, Marx's managerial-class argument. Facebook is the United States in microcosm.

**Q10.8 [SA]** What happened at the IPO tent on Feb 1, 2012?

**Answer:** An email from Zuck (email meant serious; "the Sec will come get you") announced the filing, warned about public ridicule and urged staff to "stay focused on our mission." The author watched a Harvard old-timer browse $2.4M Diamond Heights listings during the speech.

**Q10.9 [Scenario]** You've joined a startup acquired by a large company and feel bored a few months in. What does the chapter predict and what should you do?

**Answer:** Predicted deceleration: boredom sets in "a few months in" (anti-pattern: sleeping through your own deceleration). Act like you belong, seek latitude from the top, or plan the exit around the vest rather than quitting early.

**Q10.10 [Scenario]** You manage a company where early staff are wealthy and later hires are not, and nobody talks about money. What does the chapter say you are looking at?

**Answer:** "We don't talk about money" is the tell of a two-class company (haves and have-mores). Watch for middle managers identifying with owners whose economics they don't share (anti-pattern) and remember that the 401(k) came late because the IPO was supposed to be the pension for some.

## Chapter 11: When the Flying Saucers Fail to Appear, Monetizing the Tumor

**Q11.1 [Own words]** How does Festinger's flying-saucer cult explain Sponsored Stories' persistence?

**Answer:** Festinger's 1954 cult grew stronger when the prophecy failed; companies likewise "go temporarily mad" under market expectation and arrogance, ignoring data that contradicts the plan. Facebook's pre-IPO plan persisted through cognitive dissonance. Every large company has a product folly it wakes from only when reality arrives.

**Q11.2 [SA]** What were the two revenue streams circa 2010?

**Answer:** (1) Agencies convincing brands (Starbucks, Burberry) to buy millions of page Likes at ~$10M a pop, with no explanation of how Likes became sales, a mystery of faith not to be measured. (2) Direct-response advertisers barely present because targeting was too weak, except game developers who found tiny proxies (energy-drink Likes predicting mob-game conversions: a Facebook "beer and diapers").

**Q11.3 [SA]** What were Open Graph and Sponsored Stories and which two miracles did they require?

**Answer:** Open Graph expanded "Like" to verbs (play, listen, watch, buy) broadcast by outside developers in exchange for feed distribution; Sponsored Stories monetized by "boosting" stories advertisers paid for (you never saw anything you couldn't have seen, just more often). Miracles: (1) every company integrating and donating data at dubious benefit; (2) marketers abandoning proven workflows for an unprovable paradigm. Hence almost certain to fail.

**Q11.4 [Worked example]** Evaluate "social context lifts clickthrough 40-60%."

**Answer:** The Facebook Research finding (your friend Joe liked) was real but from an abysmal base (~0.05%, vs. at least 0.1% for the worst display ads and ~1% for well-targeted first-party-data ads). A 60% lift on a 20x deficit is "piddly-boo"; the paper listed confounds; the cheerleading was wishful. Partners reported no live performance difference. The missing ingredient was first-party targeting data.

**Q11.5 [SA]** What is "gavage-style launching" and "fog"?

**Answer:** Gavage: Ads didn't ship what people asked for, so products were forced down partners' throats and then advertisers'; when partners reported no difference, the response was to push harder. Fog: cultish product development can't be seen from inside; nobody from Sheryl to line PMs called bullshit; a VP kept a magnum of champagne with a Post-it targeting "10% revenue from Sponsored Stories" by end-2011, never approached.

**Q11.6 [SA]** What is "Only Good News" and what happened in March 2012?

**Answer:** Zuck outsourced monetization to Sheryl; her conference room was the appellate court for product disputes. In March 2012 she declared the Like-buying party over: no one will pay ten million for Likes anymore (Burberry, Ford, Starbucks, BMW had bought Likes that were at best a license to spam feeds).

**Q11.7 [Own words]** Why are Ads and Growth "yin-yang" and anticorrelated?

**Answer:** Ads turns users into money; Growth turns money into users; they sat in opposing wings. "What makes a user use the product does not necessarily make money, and the reverse is also true." Growth held Facebook's only real marketers (retargeting near-registrants on ad exchanges before Ads knew exchanges existed, three-decimal conversion stats, Skinnerian reminder-email cadences, carrier deals, a Risk-board map of countries yet to "tip").

**Q11.8 [SA]** Do the "running out of humans" arithmetic.

**Answer:** ~3B internet users minus ~600M in China and most of Russia, minus a 25% never-join haircut gives about 1.8B addressable. At 1.44B (Q1 2015) and ~13% growth, roughly a year of growth was left; hence Internet.org and solar drones to create connectivity, rich-market ad revenue subsidizing it: "money follows eyeballs, even if slowly."

**Q11.9 [Scenario]** Your team proposes selling the logout page to brands for a few hundred thousand dollars. A growth team objects but cannot quantify the harm. How should the decision be made, and what happened at Facebook?

**Answer:** This is LOX (Logout Experience): the most common webpage on the world's shared computers and the primary on-ramp for new users in developing countries. Growth couldn't quantify the usage cost, so outrage and personal sway decided it: LOX ran only where Facebook had already won (the US), not where rivals like Orkut were "still warm" (Brazil). Lesson: usage first, money when usage is safe; anti-pattern: springing a cross-team trade-off without a way to quantify it and selling the on-ramp for a quarter's revenue.

**Q11.10 [Scenario]** A VP presents a plan whose success needs partners and brands both to change behavior, and a metric no one can measure. What do you apply?

**Answer:** Count the miracles (Sponsored Stories needed two) and ask for lift against the best available alternative rather than your own baseline. Treat a plan nobody measures as a religion ("the Shroud of Turin must not be carbon-dated"). Listen when partners say it isn't working. FMC (Feb 2012) shows the mood: "never let the facts get in the way of a good story," and Paul Adams's *Grouped* thesis was contradicted by Facebook's own research.

## Chapter 12: The Great Awakening, Barbarians at the Gates, IPA > IPO, IPO Reevaluation, Flash Boys

**Q12.1 [Own words]** What does "advertising is name-calling" mean?

**Answer:** Every channel is a different "name" for the same nervous primate: direct mail uses a postal address, mobile a quasi-immutable device ID, the web a cookie. Each key carries behavioral data and they differ in longevity (address > device > cookie), which is why mobile privacy is harder. The platform war among Facebook, Google, Amazon and Apple is over who controls the joins between names; everything else is "a parasitic sideshow."

**Q12.2 [SA]** Who is the "real Big Brother" in this chapter?

**Answer:** Data brokers (Acxiom, Experian, Epsilon, Merkle, Neustar) who have tracked consumer America since the 1960s, ingest retailers' CRM data and sell segments no citizen can inspect. Nielsen's PRIZM segments ("Beltway Boomers") are a predictive zodiac: "You consume; therefore, you are."

**Q12.3 [SA]** Give direct mail's numbers and why they matter.

**Answer:** ~$17B in postage, a $50B+ industry (roughly a Google, three internets or three Facebooks of money), at ~$1,000 CPMs vs. ~$1 online, so online can be a thousand times less effective and still break even. It is a $50B pile waiting to move online once identity translates.

**Q12.4 [SA]** How does data on-boarding work?

**Answer:** Firms (Datalogix, Neustar, LiveRamp) place invisible pixels where name/email meets a browser (newsletters, dating sites, second-tier social networks), drop or read a cookie and store the join in hashed form ("privacy theater" if everyone uses the same hash); the join is resold constantly. Facebook and Google out-on-board everyone because they have more data and see you constantly: Custom Audiences match rates up to ~90%.

**Q12.5 [SA]** How did Facebook "learn" about this, and what was the Experian anecdote?

**Answer:** By "mooching" information from potential acquirees and partners (Turn, MediaMath, BlueKai, Acxiom, Epsilon) in meetings of dubious good faith; they explained their plumbing while privately incredulous. Experian sent a junior team bearing a box of bakery cookies to a meeting about cookies: anti-pattern, sending the B team to the join of the century.

**Q12.6 [Worked example]** How was FBX sold internally (March 13, 2012)?

**Answer:** Find the single hour KX, Hegeman, Boland, Rabkin and a dozen engineers are free; physically round them up five minutes before; let an outsider with market credibility (Zach Coelius of Triggit, a poker player who read the author's aces) hold the room for an hour on real-time programmatic buying; sell the gee-whiz real-time decisioning to engineers and whiteboard the business case; end in a rare lovefest; follow up within days with a written product and business case tied to the post-IPO quarters. Result: approval by a leadership that had never heard of retargeting, and three engineers.

**Q12.7 [SA]** Describe the FBX build.

**Answer:** Five weeks to ship by June 15 (to book Q3 and Q4 revenue), three engineers (a "brogrammer," a recruit poached from Adchemy, a reviewer nothing passed without), a parallel ads system with separate data stores, real-time global bidding hundreds of thousands of times per second, dynamic creative, separate stats, monitoring tools, partner on-boarding docs and tests, and a sales spiel; bought with a promise of $100M in 2012 revenue. First successful FBX bid June 15 via TellApart.

**Q12.8 [MC]** Why did FBX lose money in Europe? (a) language; (b) taxes; (c) 120 ms bid window versus ~60 ms practical one-way latency from North Carolina to Amsterdam (about 23 ms at light speed); (d) no advertisers.

**Answer:** (c). A third of an eye-blink must include network latency; European requests timed out: "the Flash Boys of media."

**Q12.9 [Own words]** What was the "unforgivable sin" of FBX?

**Answer:** FBX bids fed into Facebook's own auction, making Facebook "merely one more DSP"; outside bidders, who knew you'd just shopped for a handbag, bid higher than Facebook's data and won. Equality with outsiders was a feature to the author and a bug to management; incumbents prefer monopoly pricing power and asymmetry, the Goldman CDS lesson forgotten.

**Q12.10 [Scenario]** A newspaper headline says a company's IPO "popped 30% on day one" and calls it a triumph. How do you read it, and what were Facebook's numbers?

**Answer:** Use "who gets the pop?": the syndicate underprices, hypes to deep pockets and engineers the pop; an orderly open never moves 20-30% for an ordinary stock, so a pop means founders and employees were skinned and bankers' clients made fortunes. Facebook opened ~$42, closed ~$38.37, paid ~1% in fees instead of up to 7% and raised a mountain of money with minimal dilution: "the most successful tech IPO in financial history," whatever *Fortune* and *Forbes* said. Also recall the wealth inflections: below fuck-you money only the indifference threshold moves; the author's package (75,000 shares over four years, $175K salary, 5,000 shares cashed up front) was ~$971K/year at the IPO price, ~$550K after tax.

## Chapter 13: Full Frontal Facebook, Microsoft Shrugged, Ad Majorem, Adiós Facebook

**Q13.1 [SA]** What does "great powers and temporary allies" mean for Facebook?

**Answer:** Facebook has no partners, only existential rivals (Amazon, Google, once Apple, mirrors of Zuck) and convenient alliances; negotiations with peers are arms-control treaties with red-lined contracts and mutual suspicion. Twitter didn't register ("a clown car that drove into a gold mine").

**Q13.2 [SA]** What did the Google Plus numbers reveal?

**Answer:** Google Plus claimed 400M registered/100M active by counting anyone who clicked a Plus button. Inside Facebook, panic gave way to the recognition of Enron-style accounting; Larry Page's ultimatum reoriented all of Google, including Search, around social; Vic Gundotra became the internal Emmanuel Goldstein.

**Q13.3 [Own words]** Explain "do something vs. be someone."

**Answer:** Via Col. Boyd: one road is impact, truth to your vision and management's scorn; the other is plum products, facile praise and forgettable work. With children's tuition riding on unvested stock, the author chose the first: "double down and dig in."

**Q13.4 [SA]** Define incrementality with the CA vs. FBX comparison.

**Answer:** Revenue that would not otherwise have landed on the platform. Custom Audiences' dashboard dollars came largely from existing "Facebook" budget lines (discount by half); FBX competed for budgets earmarked for Google's AdX, so it was 100% incremental, but needed new creative and explicit reallocation from burned retailers before Q4. Advertisers budget by channel, not technique.

**Q13.5 [SA]** Why does the ad server matter and how do Google and Facebook differ?

**Answer:** Nobody in the chain trusts anyone; the ad server is the source of truth that counts delivery, frequency and geography and defines the units of marketing value. Google counts clicks only ("clicks trump impressions") because search exploits existing intent; Facebook must manufacture desire, so it worships viewthroughs (the billboard vs. the turn-off) and wants credit for Likes, shares and comments, hence its own yardstick. Containers: IAB/MMA standard units are the intermodal containers of media, which Google Search, Facebook and Twitter refuse.

**Q13.6 [Worked example]** Tell the Atlas arithmetic and moral.

**Answer:** Microsoft's creaky ad server (ran only on certain Internet Explorer versions; 20% share; thousands of publishers) was bought for roughly the $0.1B left after Microsoft wrote down $6.1B of its $6.2B aQuantive purchase: "Facebook's biggest ad tech acquisition to date was Microsoft's accounting rounding error." Boland and ex-Microsoft colleagues engineered it; "it's not what you pay, it's what they cost you": two-plus years of delays.

**Q13.7 [SA]** What happened on April 12, 2013?

**Answer:** In "Only Good News," Boz recommended to Sheryl: no identity matching and no mobile on FBX. Sheryl: "if that's what you think... that's what we'll do." Gokul fled the room; FBX was on life support. The decision was for the closed stack (Custom Audiences plus Atlas) over the open one. Earlier, LiveBoz (April 4) had produced a pissing match: Boland said CA was way ahead, the author said only if you count non-incremental dollars.

**Q13.8 [Own words]** Why are middle managers "what they are"?

**Answer:** Negative selection: the ambitious leave, the rest master managing up, acting visibly on feedback, championing reports, allying with sales, routing around incompetence and banking the logo on the business card. Boz was dispatched to end Ads' Lord-of-the-Flies anarchy and reconfigured Ads along one axis, from "Boz likes you" to "fired."

**Q13.9 [SA]** Give the exit playbook.

**Answer:** Don't quit before the vest; take vacation over the vesting date so you can't be fired; clear your desk over the weekend; secure-erase personal files (you don't need to be at fault to be sued); expect a severance-for-non-disparagement offer ($30,000 for a year of silence) and decline to sign on the spot; keep the badge and unwiped phone "at home." April 16: Miss HR's script, the kegerator wheeled out, Gokul's "Remember the non-solicit! (seriously)."

**Q13.10 [Scenario]** You've bet your career on an internal product that makes money, but the leadership narrative has already chosen a different path. What does the chapter say?

**Answer:** Anti-pattern: betting a career on winning an internal argument with money when the narrative is fixed. "Money talks... only if what it said was part of the bigger, accepted narrative." "The Spartans lost at Thermopylae": a movie reference in reply to a resource plea means no support. Also the data-portability argument: closed plan would brick over the opening FBX created; the Fidelity analogy shows lock-in only works if your identity data is so superior that advertisers accept inferior tooling, otherwise it's "Sponsored Stories Part II."

## Chapter 14: Pandemonium Lost, Epilogue

**Q14.1 [Own words]** What is the "outsider's blog post as insider's mouthpiece"?

**Answer:** Executives in an acquisition can't explain strategy publicly (FTC, secrecy) but can retweet a perceptive outsider. The Medium post on Twitter-MoPub, written during eight weeks immobilized by a plantar fascia rupture, was circulated by the deal's principals and reopened a door slammed two years earlier. Twitter bought MoPub (~$350M deal) to build the exchange Facebook had refused.

**Q14.2 [SA]** What is "show enough skin for a second date"?

**Answer:** In the reconciliation meeting the author gave Twitter the full whiteboard (data flows, cross-device de-duplication, leakage-safe targeting): "if in doubt, show more." An advisory offer followed within days: a thousand shares a quarter (~$160K/year), no badge, no email.

**Q14.3 [Own words]** Why did mobile save Facebook?

**Answer:** Structural reasons, retrospective. (1) Data: mobile browsers block third-party cookies, apps silo data, Apple polices device IDs and developers hoard; so where desktop had a mature data marketplace that made Facebook's data marginal, mobile was data-starved and even age/gender was a godsend ("in the land of the mobile data blind, the one-eyed Facebook man was king"). (2) Inventory: mobile's savvy monetizers (games, commerce) charged users directly, leaving ad exchanges with tiny bars, interstitials and Grindr, so Facebook's stylish in-feed units with single-digit-percent clickthroughs had no competition. Q2 2013: mobile actives up 50%+, mobile revenue roughly doubled.

**Q14.4 [SA]** What is the "success generator"/ten dice idea?

**Answer:** Try ten things on hunches and mythology; seven fail and are forgotten, two do OK, one becomes transformational for reasons discovered afterward; the press writes the narrative fallacy; you're crowned a genius and roll again until the dealer sweeps your chips. Use "which of the ten dice came up?" to discount any founder's retrospective genius.

**Q14.5 [SA]** What happened to FBX?

**Answer:** ~$500M in new revenue by early 2014, all incremental, second only to News Feed ads, built by a handful and now costing nothing: a "perpetual motion machine of cash" and Facebook's window into programmatic competitors (like Goldman watching order flow). Kept alive because too profitable to kill, starved of identity and mobile. The author predicts Facebook will eventually run a full exchange; even Goldman moved CDS clearing to ICE in 2013.

**Q14.6 [SA]** Name Facebook's buy-your-way-out moves.

**Answer:** Instagram (2012, $1B, a weekend, a growth curve like early Facebook's; Dorsey outmaneuvered) and WhatsApp (2014, $19B, phone number as identity). Someday a founder as proud as Zuck will refuse, and Facebook will have to build, "a far riskier proposition."

**Q14.7 [T/F]** Google Plus ultimately beat Facebook.

**Answer:** False. Vic Gundotra left in April 2014 and Google Plus was demoted to a "platform," "an army... advancing in reverse"; Facebook's lockdown had won.

**Q14.8 [Worked example]** Explain why the desktop thesis didn't transfer to mobile.

**Answer:** On desktop, Facebook's data lost to first-party and broker data because browsers leak cookies to everyone (Chorizo and FBX findings were correct there). On mobile, the same data (age, gender, interests) became scarce and valuable because nothing leaks. Same asset, different market structure, opposite outcome, so mobile News Feed ads (championed by Fidji Simo) made the numbers, not identity joins.

**Q14.9 [Scenario]** You were ejected from a company and a rival offers advisory work. Which pattern applies and what are its limits?

**Answer:** The grudge job and the Land of the Stateless Machines: working for Twitter against Facebook's interests while employed by Facebook's biggest partner (Nanigans), building buying tools to move spend from Facebook back to Google. The notes call it "just shy" of corporate treason, "which was how Silicon Valley worked." Caveat: he was under NDA from Facebook; it is a description, not an endorsement.

**Q14.10 [Scenario]** You received restricted stock at $38 that is taxed as income, a lockout expires near $20 and you are ejected two years in. What does the epilogue say about this outcome versus the AdGrok engineers'?

**Answer:** Compensation form, tax timing, lockouts and tenure, not talent, decided who got rich: Twitter's options, a post-lockout price above $50 and four-plus years of vesting made the AdGrok engineers rich, while Facebook's restricted stock left the author with little ("paying taxes on money I hadn't made," the dot-com bankruptcies' plight in miniature). Afterward he sold everything and outfitted *Ayala*, a forty-foot cutter, to sail around the world alone.

## Cumulative Final Exam (20 questions)

**F1 [SA]** State the book's one-line judgment of ad tech and the three rules that follow from it.

**Answer:** "Ad tech is finance for attention; incumbents keep markets inefficient; count miracles, price per head, vest before you leave." (Ch 1, 2, 7, 10)

**F2 [Own words]** Link Goldman's refusal to move CDS onto exchanges to Facebook's treatment of FBX.

**Answer:** Both incumbents preferred owning a small market with information asymmetry and price-making power to competing in a larger transparent one. Facebook's FBX showed outside data beating its own in its own auction, so it chose the closed stack (Custom Audiences, Atlas). Irony: Goldman itself moved CDS clearing to ICE in 2013, and the author predicts Facebook will eventually run a full exchange. (Ch 1, 12, 13, 14)

**F3 [MC]** Which of these is a "two-miracle" idea in the notes? (a) Airbnb; (b) Sponsored Stories; (c) a laundromat; (d) Google. 

**Answer:** (b) (and AdGrok's first plan, which needed five). Airbnb and Google are single-miracle (user behavior; technical); a laundromat is zero. (Ch 2, 11)

**F4 [SA]** What is the difference between a cap and a valuation, and what is $100K at a $3M cap?

**Answer:** The cap is a maximum conversion valuation, "an input to a hypothetical calculation"; $100K at a $3M cap = 3.3% regardless of the A price. (Ch 4)

**F5 [SA]** Name a giant's two weak points and the specific tactic used against Adchemy.

**Answer:** Investors and prospective partners. PG threatened to blacklist Adchemy's VCs (August, Mayfield) from YC deal flow; Sam Altman got Microsoft BD to call the litigation "problematic" for the Bing deal. (Ch 5)

**F6 [Scenario]** A founder wants to be "fair" and split equity three ways, and is also tempted to hide a dire cash forecast from the team and investors. Evaluate both choices using the book.

**Answer:** Equal splits produce paralysis and factions: have a leader (51%+ or a true mind-meld). On the forecast: Martínez hid the Death Clock from cofounders and investors and "cooked the books," and the lie held. The notes' later rule is to lie to investors and acquirers if you must but never deceive cofounders (he himself deceived his cofounders for two weeks in the acquisition and calls the optimal line "a step too far out on the prick spectrum"). So the notes themselves are inconsistent or at least morally ambivalent ("morality... is an expensive hobby"); a good answer names that tension and the firewall logic (keep the crisis out of the team's headspace). (Ch 3, 4, 5, 7)

**F7 [SA]** What are the tells listed in the quick-tells section?

**Answer:** "Stealth" or an NDA before the pitch means loser; a counterpart calling back within the hour means weak hand; "six months" of dashboard revenue that merely reallocates budget means not incremental; an empty parking lot on Sunday means not at war; a first-ever reminder of your one-on-one means HR is waiting; champagne with a revenue Post-it signals a cult in progress. (Ch 2, 7, 8, 11, 13)

**F8 [Own words]** Compare how a bluff worked in Ch 6 and how a verification failure mattered in Ch 9.

**Answer:** The Google bluff worked because the counterpart couldn't verify it ("Everybody in tech feared Google"). In Ch 9 the Irish audit assurance from a senior engineer couldn't be trusted, so the PM verified it himself on production Christmas Eve. Common principle: whether claims can be checked decides who wins; verify what you vouched for, bluff only where the target has no back channel. (Ch 6, 9)

**F9 [SA]** Summarize the compensation rules: vesting, offers, and wealth thresholds.

**Answer:** Four-year vesting with 25% at one year; halve any big-company offer (you'll leave in two years) and halve again for stock vs. options; acqui-hire headlines are mostly signing bonuses and vesting for founders; founders own nothing until they vest; two inflection points matter, fuck-you money and fuck-the-world money. (Ch 7, 10, 12)

**F10 [SA]** Name the Facebook cultural mechanisms: blue tape, memento mori, onboarding, posters, Faceversaries.

**Answer:** Blue tape = bold experiment, fast failure, prune, encode the lesson as a macro; Sun logos = memento mori; onboarding as citizenship oath with posters as commandments; Faceversaries celebrate start dates and "Facebook deaths" mourn departures. Zuck's mission culture is compared to a revolutionary regime ("People's Republic of Facebook"). (Ch 8, 10)

**F11 [Own words]** Why is Facebook's social data both "inert" and later "scarce and valuable"?

**Answer:** On desktop a mature data marketplace and cookies leaked everywhere, so Facebook's data was marginal (Chorizo's "one measly sausage," the noisy bar). On mobile, third-party cookies are blocked, apps are siloed and Apple polices device IDs, so even age and gender were valuable. Market structure decides the value of a dataset. (Ch 8, 9, 14)

**F12 [MC]** Which metric does the book say to use when judging a new ad product? (a) dashboard revenue; (b) clickthrough lift vs. your baseline; (c) incremental dollars and lift vs. the best outside alternative; (d) number of partners.

**Answer:** (c). Ch 11 gives the lift rule (40-60% on a 20x deficit is piddly-boo) and Ch 13 gives the incrementality rule (CA discounted by half; FBX 100% incremental).

**F13 [SA]** What are the two traits founders need and which can be learned?

**Answer:** Monomaniacal focus (cannot be taught) and endurance of endless shit (can be: cross an ocean, join the Marines). Enemies help. (Ch 5)

**F14 [Scenario]** You are a product manager at a large company and an engineer says your idea is impossible. Which concepts apply?

**Answer:** Be technical enough to detect bullshit (lazy "impossible"), be the tiebreaker, build a dashboard you know to the decimal, and verify anything you personally vouched for even in production. Remember power is borrowed from engineers' trust and from the company's name. (Ch 8, 9)

**F15 [Own words]** Synthesize what the Twitter acqui-hire (Ch 6-7) and Facebook's FBX decision (Ch 12-13) both teach about speed and tempo.

**Answer:** Tempo reveals hands: Twitter called back within the hour (weak, a "call station"), and an acquirer's speed is a function of the fire you've lit under it. FBX was built in five weeks to book Q3 and Q4 revenue (a fiscal-calendar deadline), yet the notes say light speed and management could still beat it. A counterpart's behavior tells you more than its words, and deadlines (a fiscal calendar, a no-shop) shape leverage. (Ch 6, 7, 12, 13)

**F16 [SA]** Why does Martínez say acquirers don't care about the founder/investor split, and how did this play out for investors?

**Answer:** Acquirers price people (per-head), so the split is the founders' choice from their own wallet. Twitter's default term sheet gave investors ~$1M of ~$10M instead of the ~$2M pro rata; Sacca's rage followed. (Ch 7)

**F17 [T/F]** The book argues that the open FBX would have been the right strategy because mobile News Feed ads were inevitable.

**Answer:** False. The epilogue says the 2013 debate was moot: neither FBX nor Custom Audiences saved Facebook; mobile News Feed ads did, for structural reasons nobody argued about at the time. Being right about the technology was not the same as being right about what would save the quarter. FBX nevertheless grew to ~$500M. (Ch 13, 14)

**F18 [Own words]** Compare "Open Graph/Sponsored Stories" and "FBX" as strategies using the miracle count and incrementality.

**Answer:** Sponsored Stories needed two miracles and rested on cognitive dissonance and unmeasurable brand Likes; FBX needed one idea (adopt the industry standard, outside data), was built in five weeks, and produced fully incremental revenue. (Ch 11, 12, 13)
