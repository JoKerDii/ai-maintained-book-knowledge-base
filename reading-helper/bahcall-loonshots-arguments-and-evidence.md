## Chapter 1 - Introduction: Structure, Not Culture

**Central argument.** Teams that suddenly stop innovating (Nokia, Merck, Disney after *The Lion King*) did not change their people or culture; they crossed a phase transition. Innovation is an emergent property of group structure, so the fix is structure, not culture. Being good at loonshots (original films, new drugs) and being good at franchises (sequels, the ninth statin) are distinct phases of organization, like liquid and solid, and small changes in a control parameter (chiefly size) can flip the same people from risk-takers to project-killers.

**Full stories.**

*Bahcall's own biotech.* The notes tell of a physicist-CEO at the bedside of his first patient. For sarcoma there were no more chemotherapy options than there had been 40 years earlier. Later he sat at his father's bedside with leukemia and "no new drugs, not even promising trials." Yet dozens of candidates for his father's disease were sitting buried in labs: not for lack of ideas, but because large groups with the means to develop them had rejected them. The book's question follows: not "how do we get more ideas?" but "why do good teams, with excellent people and the best intentions, kill great ideas, and what structural change stops it?"

*Miller's piranha (ibrutinib).* An idea ridiculed by every large pharma, whose champion was fired by his own board, later bought for $21 billion. The lesson: ask of any dismissed idea, "what would have to be true for the critics to be wrong?"

*The Amgen post-hoc culture example.* Picking winners by stock price and reverse-engineering "lessons" from them. In the Amgen case the notes say the real lesson was "pick up the check and hire good lawyers": the point being that after-the-fact culture analysis is reading the socks of a lottery winner. Victors rewrite the twisted path into a straight line (revisionist history).

*The Nokia/Merck/Disney pattern.* Each had been a star at original work, then stopped. The notes offer no internal details; the argument is that these are phase transitions, not cultural rot.

**Evidence.** The chapter's evidence is analogical and anecdotal rather than statistical: the phase-transition analogy (water at 32 F; ice and a single molecule; iron's much higher melting point because of stronger binding energy, covered in Ch 7); the bedside observations; and the sale price of ibrutinib ($21B). Caveat: the notes frame this chapter as setting the premise; the quantitative proof arrives in Ch 8. Phil Anderson's "more is different" is cited as the principle.

**Key terms.** Loonshot; moonshot; phases of organization; franchise phase; loonshot phase; stake vs. rank; more is different; phase transition; control parameter; revisionist history.

**So what.** (1) Classify projects honestly: if leadership already loves it, it is a moonshot and needs no protection; if it is being laughed at, it is a loonshot and needs a nursery. (2) When a good team turns, ask which phase the structure is in and what control parameter put it there. (3) Hold the edge where both phases coexist (water at 32 F). (4) Distrust straight-line success stories; ask for the Three Deaths. (5) "Small changes in structure can melt steel; one molecule yelling at its neighbors cannot."

## Chapter 2 - How Loonshots Won a War: Life on the Edge

**Central argument.** Vannevar Bush (OSRD) and Theodore Vail (Bell Labs) rescued stagnant franchises not by championing any single idea but by building a structure in which loonshots and franchises coexist: phase separation plus dynamic equilibrium. Break apart but stay connected, like patches of ice in 32 F water. Both rules are needed; either alone leads to a trap.

**Full stories.**

*Radar and the Navy.* Two Navy radio engineers proposed radar in 1922 after a passing ship doubled their signal. The Navy ignored it. In 1930 it rejected a $5,000 request because results "might take two or three years". It then took five years to assign one full-time person, and the system was still being field-tested on the morning of Pearl Harbor. In 1940 Bush changed the structure, not the technology: a civilian agency reporting directly to the President, quarantined in anonymous MIT buildings (separation), with Bush personally flying to battlefield HQ and arranging for the Secretary of War to ride an experimental radar plane so generals would look ("I've seen the new radar equipment. Why haven't you?") (transfer). Within about 30 months microwave radar cut Allied shipping losses by 95% and forced the U-boat withdrawal. Same Navy, same engineers, different structure.

*Top cover: FDR.* Bush could "bump heads" only because FDR said "you go ahead and bump, and I will back you up."

*The field feedback loop (PPI display).* Early airborne radar was ignored by pilots, not because of a core technology flaw but because of a UI problem: pilots had to fiddle with switches. The fix, the PPI display, came only because Bush forced pilots to explain *why* to scientists. Lesson: most first versions fail for reasons unrelated to the core idea; force field feedback back into the nursery.

*Jobs 1.0 and the "bozos".* Calling the Apple II team "bozos" led Wozniak to quit and the Mac launch to fail. Other poisonous nicknames: "regular Navy," "damn professors." Lesson: artists favor artists, soldiers favor soldiers; a leader must genuinely respect both or one side leaves.

*3M and Six Sigma.* Under a Six Sigma CEO, 3M's innovation plunged: a regime that helps an assembly line suffocates an inventor.

*Nursery examples.* Early ideas arrive covered in warts: anti-angiogenesis, Bond with a monkey villain, *The Adventures of Luke Starkiller*. Soldiers pick at warts, so the nursery must exist before the idea is judged.

*The rubber-raft test.* Bush asked scientists: you are landing at night on a hostile coast; describe the weapon you'd want. Practical output, not clever ideas, is the goal even in the nursery.

**Evidence.** The radar timeline (1922, 1930, a five-year delay, Pearl Harbor) against Bush's 30-month result (95% reduction in shipping losses; U-boat withdrawal). Counterfactual built into the story: same Navy and engineers. Supporting anecdotes: 3M's innovation plunge, Apple's Mac failure, a long list of failed companies with shiny labs and no shipping. Caveat: the notes give outcomes but not the underlying data series.

**Key terms.** Bush-Vail Rules 1 and 2; artists and soldiers; phase separation; dynamic equilibrium; loonshot nursery; four-quadrant map; gardener, not a Moses; support from the top; field feedback loop; "luck is the residue of design" (Branch Rickey).

**So what.** Separating artists from soldiers (wide spans/loose controls/creative metrics vs. narrow/tight/quantitative) is necessary but not sufficient. The leader manages the transfer, not the technology; get top cover; hire into both camps with equal love. Four quadrants: stagnation (bottom-left), chaos (top-left; "the CEO must be the Chief Innovation Officer"), the Trap (bottom-right), Bush-Vail target (top-right). Do not legislate creativity everywhere (sometimes you need the phone operator to answer the phone), do not schedule "three good ideas on Wednesday."

## Chapter 3 - The Surprising Fragility of the Loonshot

**Central argument.** Every important breakthrough dies at least three times before it succeeds. The decisive skill of great inventors and investors is not invention but investigating failure: separating a True Fail from a False Fail. Survival requires LSC (Listen to the Suck with Curiosity) and champions who are different people from inventors.

**Full stories.**

*Akira Endo's statin.* Death 1: by the time he presented mevastatin, the "diet-heart" hypothesis had been declared dead and almost nobody attended his talk. Death 2: the drug had no effect in rats. He carried a resignation letter in his pocket and pleaded for time. A bar conversation with a colleague who was about to eat his research hens led him to test on chickens without formal approval: hens carry LDL like humans, and cholesterol fell by nearly half. (The rat failure was a False Fail: rats carry mostly HDL, so LDL-lowering drugs show nothing. This is the "species difference" Endo suspected.) Death 3: after the first patient's cholesterol dropped 30% and a 12-hospital study was launched, a dog safety study "showed cancer" (an artifact of ultra-high doses) and Sankyo killed the program after Endo had left. Merck, which had been given Endo's confidential data under a collaboration, terminated too, until outside physicians Brown and Goldstein proved the dog result was a false positive and pressured a restart. Mevacor was approved in 1987; statins now prevent about 500,000 US heart attacks and strokes a year. At each death, someone asked why instead of accepting the result.

*Beecham.* It quit statins on the rat result and forfeited a share of $300 billion.

*Friendster.* Users left because of crashes, not because social networks were a fad. Thiel and Howery extracted a contrarian answer with confidence from the retention data, which is the recipe for an attractive investment.

*The Comet jets.* The Comet explosions were metal fatigue from a window design, not jets per se (Trippe saw through this in Ch 4).

*Deak Parsons.* The Navy liaison who dragged radar out of neglect "with the persistence of a door-to-door salesman": the model of the bilingual champion, fluent in artist-speak and soldier-speak.

*Gleevec and Folkman.* Gleevec's 35-month record trial hides years of denied tenure and rejected papers (revisionist history). Judah Folkman's support kept him in research: the "Spouse Activation Factor" (SAF) and his line "you can tell a leader by counting the number of arrows in his ass." Black's beta-blockers took 7 years; Folkman 32; Endo 16.

**Evidence.** The statin case: rat, hen (cholesterol nearly halved), first patient (30% drop), 12-hospital study, dog study false positive, approval 1987, 500,000 events prevented annually. Beecham's $300B forfeiture. Gleevec's 35 months. Timelines of 16, 7 and 32 years. Quote from Sir James Black: "It's not a good drug unless it's been killed at least three times." Caveat: the notes use anecdotal case evidence; "at least three" is a rule of thumb.

**Key terms.** Three Deaths; False Fail; species difference; project champion; LSC; contrarian answer with confidence; revisionist history; SAF; counting the arrows.

**So what.** (1) Budget for three deaths; zero deaths means untested. (2) Before terminating, ask whether the idea or the test is flawed, what you would have to believe for the test to be flawed, and the cheapest check. (3) Appoint and train champions; do not leave promotion to inventors, and plan for what happens when a combined inventor-champion leaves (Sankyo collapsed after Endo retired). (4) After a rejection go to the rejecter and ask why (not to friends for reassurance). (5) Anger signals stubbornness; curiosity signals persistence. "I find it's when I question the least that I need to worry the most."

## Chapter 4 - The Two Types of Loonshots: Trippe vs. Crandall

**Central argument.** There are two kinds of loonshots: P-type (product/technology that no one thinks will work) and S-type (strategy/business model no one thinks will make money). Every innovator has a strong side and a blind side; Pan Am died of the one Juan Trippe could not see.

**Full stories.**

*Trippe's jet-age poker game.* Boeing would not redesign the 707 for nonstop transatlantic range, Douglas would not build a jet at all, and Pratt & Whitney would not release its experimental high-compression engine. Trippe opened talks with Rolls-Royce to spook Pratt, then ordered 120 engines directly: $40M, four times Pan Am's annual income, with no planes to put them in. Holding the engines, he got Douglas to commit to the DC-8 to his design, quietly, then ordered 20 inferior 707s from Boeing and timed both announcements for the same *Wall Street Journal* morning. Boeing's president "felt like an earthquake victim" and conceded the redesign. Brilliant, and exactly the skill that later blinded him.

*Crandall and deregulation.* After airline deregulation (1978) the same instinct led Pan Am to buy jumbo jets (the 747 order, then eight more, then a $100M terminal), while Crandall's unglamorous S-types won the war: two-tier pay (A-scale/B-scale) to escape legacy labor contracts; the Sabre reservation system given free to travel agents, producing placement advantage and, unexpectedly, yield management, the first big-data pricing engine ("thirty years before Big Data"). Startups added hub-and-spoke, secondary airports and 20-minute turnarounds. Pan Am lost money in 18 of its last 22 years and ceased operations in 1991.

*The pie-shop parable.* A device that lists every pie shop "fairly" but places yours at the top (Sabre-like placement), or a two-tier wage that lets you outgrow lower-cost entrants: how to spot S-type moves.

*IBM.* It won the P-type PC battle but missed that customers cared about the software standard and chip rather than the box, handing $1.5 trillion of value to Microsoft and Intel.

*Google.* A P-type ranking algorithm plus S-type advertising strategies: great ideas usually need both.

*Harry Lime and wars.* Bahcall frames the observation that wars accelerate P-types through Harry Lime's cuckoo-clock speech.

*Android free.* A "solar eclipse" S-type moment, alongside deregulation.

**Evidence.** Numbers: 120 engines, $40M, four times annual income, 20 inferior 707s; Pan Am's 18 of 22 loss years; ceased 1991; Walmart's three decades; $1.5T lost by IBM. Qualitative asymmetry: P-type deaths are quick and dramatic (streaming kills rentals), S-type deaths slow and hard to diagnose even in hindsight. Caveat: the notes do not provide a statistical test of the P/S asymmetry; it is argued through cases.

**Key terms.** P-type; S-type; watch your blind side (Rule 1c); dangerous virtuous cycle; Moses Trap (introduced); S-type moment; two-tier pay; Sabre; yield management; hub-and-spoke.

**So what.** (1) List your loonshots by type; if one column is empty, fund the other. (2) After any regulatory, pricing or platform shock, ask "which S-types just became visible?" (3) S-types are dim, rarely written about and much harder to copy; do not dismiss "nerdy" strategy changes. (4) Watch the virtuous cycle for the turn too many; do not bet the franchise on one more P-type after the music stops. (5) Winning the technology battle can lose the strategy war. (6) P/S is not Christensen's disruptive/sustaining (Ch 11).

## Chapter 5 - Edwin Land and the Moses Trap

**Central argument.** Edwin Land built a Nobel-caliber loonshot nursery (Rule 1) but stayed judge and jury of every idea, failing Rule 2. A Moses can will a loonshot into existence for a while, but the virtuous cycle eventually spins one turn too many (Polavision), and the blind side (digital's S-type economics) goes unseen even when the leader understands the technology better than anyone alive.

**Full stories.**

*The origin: herapathite.* At 19 Land found herapathite crystals in an obsolete-words dictionary entry and built the first synthetic polarizer. It was a textbook False Fail rescued from the graveyard of unexplained experiments. Polarization, a hidden property of light, became Polaroid's origin; every LCD pixel still uses Land's two-filter trick. A vectograph/3D imaging project that began in art-history photography was weaponized into 3D terrain maps.

*The rise (Stage 1).* Sepia prints (1947), black-and-white, auto-exposure, instant color (1963), SX-70 (1972); sales rose from $1.5M to $1.4B. His rule for choosing problems: "manifestly important and nearly impossible."

*The blinders (Stage 2).* Cameras earned little and film cartridges earned a lot, so only loonshots that spun the instant-film wheel were seen.

*Moses (Stage 3): Polavision.* Land personally led it for ten years and half a billion dollars. Instant movies appeared in 1977 and were technically miraculous but commercially dead within a year: a $2,500 camera, $30 per 3-minute cassette, not erasable. It was unveiled with mimes and dancers, and he told an analyst "the bottom line's in heaven." The result was a $200M write-off and Land forced out. In the warehouse of unsold machines he said, "I wanted you to see what hubris looks like." The notes' epitaph, later applied to NeXT: "much more scientific and aesthetic appeal than commercial significance."

*KH-11.* In April 1971 Land argued for digital (CCD) spy satellites before Sony or Kodak had started, and won against nearly the entire military leadership. He understood digital fully, and still dismissed it for Polaroid because "there's no film; there's no way that can make money." Polaroid's digital camera arrived in 1996; bankruptcy in 2001. He banned his own head of engineering from the ninth-floor lab. A colleague's description: "His research decisions would always be governing, never mine."

**Evidence.** The Polaroid revenue arc ($1.5M to $1.4B), Polavision economics ($2,500 / $30 / 3 minutes / $200M write-off / ten years / half a billion dollars), the KH-11 episode (1971, versus Sony/Kodak and the military), the 1996 and 2001 dates. The counterfactual: the same man persuaded Nixon to fund CCD satellites in the same years. Caveat: the notes assert what an equilibrium structure "would have" surfaced; it is an argument, not a measurement.

**Key terms.** Moses Trap (three stages); manifestly important and nearly impossible; franchise blinders; herapathite; Polavision; KH-11; "the bottom line's in heaven".

**So what.** (1) A leader's love for a loonshot is not evidence of its strategic strength. (2) CEO plus Director of Research is a structural warning sign. (3) Understanding technology is not seeing its S-type: ask "what new income streams does this enable?" (4) A brilliant walled-off lab (the bottom-right quadrant) delays but does not prevent decline. (5) When the wheel has spun many times successfully, check for one turn too many. (6) Do not wait a decade to enter a category you foresaw.

## Chapter 6 - Escaping the Moses Trap

**Central argument.** Steve Jobs 1.0 doubled down on the Moses Trap (NeXT, the Pixar Image Computer); watching Ed Catmull run Pixar taught him to mind the system rather than manage the project. That is Rule 3, system mindset, and Apple 2.0 was the result. Two traps flank the target: Moses (transfer over-forced) and PARC (under-forced).

**Full stories.**

*NeXT.* Jobs risked the company on optical drives ("we made a decision to risk our company") against Sun's McNealy, rejecting a software-licensing S-type. The Cube was $10,000; optical drives were slow; Businessland sold under 400 units. A Kasparov-style system analysis: one person was financier, product visionary and factory designer; the launch was modeled on Land's Polavision theater; the press crowned Jobs the P-type genius of his generation (an incentive to be Moses); excluded were employees and PC makers proposing a software-only strategy and the actual behavior of $10,000-machine buyers (Sun's data). Process fix: separate the roles (Catmull as CEO of Pixar, Jobs as deal-maker), install a peer feedback loop the leader cannot dominate, and weigh S-type options with the same rigor as P-types.

*Pixar and the braintrust.* Catmull's "ugly babies vs. the Beast": originality is fragile and ugly at first; the franchise Beast is a glutton but a necessary motivator. Pixar's directors' braintrust gives peer feedback; Jobs was kept out of early film feedback and prefaced late comments with "I'm not a filmmaker; you can ignore everything I say," since a leader's presence is gravity that crushes candor. Buzz and Woody: *Toy Story* plus a perfectly timed IPO made Jobs a billionaire and showed him the Bush-Vail rules working.

*Apple 2.0.* Ive's studio sealed off (separation); Cook as heir (loving soldiers); the iTunes store and closed ecosystem as S-types. IBM's OS/2 failure had been a False Fail; Jobs paid $100M+ to kill Mac clones while near bankruptcy. "The best innovation is sometimes the company, the way you organize" (Jobs to Isaacson).

*Newton, Jobs and synthesizers.* Each assembled others' pieces (Hooke/Raskin) into a system and each tried to bury the precursor.

*PARC and Kodak.* "Color is not part of the office of the future"; Kodak buried its 1975 digital camera for a decade; Xerox Texas could not make its typewriter numbers and launch the Alto because of its bonus structure. Pan Am's revolving CEOs are cited as a case of reading decline as a stale team when the structure had hardened. Art Levinson grilled junior technicians on data: ferocious precision is tailored to the creative phase.

*Win/loss illustrations for Kasparov's levels.* A 100:1 lottery with three tickets sold (good decision, bad outcome); the keeper slipped (bad decision, good outcome).

**Evidence.** NeXT numbers ($10,000 Cube, under 400 Businessland units, $100M+ to kill clones). Kasparov's three-level ladder as a framework. The cases of Pixar, Genentech and PARC as natural experiments in feedback and transfer. Caveat: the evidence is case narrative; the notes make Rule 3 the least quantitative of the four rules.

**Key terms.** Rule 3, system mindset; Level 0/1/2; outcome mindset; ugly babies vs. the Beast; braintrust/peer feedback; PARC Trap; synthesizer; love of loonshots vs. strength of strategy; closed ecosystem as S-type.

**So what.** (1) After every outcome, ask why, then how did we decide and what should change. (2) Analyze wins too. (3) Use a neutral outside mediator for uncomfortable analysis. (4) The leader minds the system; champions run projects. (5) Design the feedback loop so authority stays out. (6) Diagnose transfer on a dial from over-forced to under-forced and aim for the gardener's touch. (7) Do not double down on hardware because you are famous for hardware.

## Chapter 7 - Interlude + Phase Transitions I: Marriage, Forest Fires, and Terrorists

**Central argument.** Emergent behaviors (liquids flowing, markets clearing, teams innovating) are not fundamental laws; they can snap. Every phase transition is a tug-of-war between two forces, triggered when a control parameter crosses a threshold. Find the control parameters and you can manage the transition.

**Full stories.**

*Marble in an egg carton.* A model of binding energy vs. entropy, illustrated with Jane Austen's "single man in possession of a good fortune" vs. the roaming bachelor.

*The traffic phase diagram.* Forces: the driver wants cruising speed; the driver brakes to avoid the bumper ahead. Axes: average car separation (vertical) and average speed (horizontal). Stopping distance is about 6 car lengths at 30 mph and 30 at 80 mph, so the transition line slopes up-right. A brake tap on an empty road (above the line) dies out; in dense traffic (below the line) the delay doubles for each following car (2, 4, 8, 16 seconds) until it is a jam. Engineers move flow back above the line by lowering speed limits in heavy traffic, metering on-ramps to cap density, or banning truck overtaking to prevent local density spikes. The same procedure (name the forces, name two dials, draw the line, pick the cheaper dial) is what Ch 8 applies to organizations. Phantom jams were confirmed at the Nagoya Dome.

*Yellowstone.* Suppressing every small fire (low sparking rate) let density build past the threshold; 1988 burned 36% of the park. Controlled and prescribed burns back the forest away from the line.

*Greenspan and the invisible hand.* Greenspan's "notably rare exceptions" (2008) are the storms you most need to understand; Adam Smith's model was Boyle's emergent physics, not Newton's laws.

*Virtual terror cells.* Neil Johnson tracked 108,086 followers across 196 pro-ISIS pages. Clusters grow, merge, split and dissolve like fire clusters, and exponential cluster growth predicts eruptions weeks ahead, without analyzing any individual.

*Iron vs. ice.* Iron melts at 2,800 F, not 32 F, because its binding energy is about 100x stronger: multiple control parameters give leverage.

**Evidence.** Percolation theory (Broadbent and Hammersley, 1957); small-world networks (Watts and Strogatz): robust to random failure, fragile to attacks on influential nodes; power-law tails (twice as many 10-acre fires as 20-acre; terror-attack casualties with exponent about 2.5 across nine conflicts); Nagoya Dome traffic; the Yellowstone 36%; the ISIS dataset (108,086 followers, 196 pages). Caveat: this chapter is analogy-building; the notes do not claim the forest or traffic results prove anything about organizations, only that the method (two forces, control parameters, phase diagram) transfers.

**Key terms.** Emergent property; control parameter; phase diagram; percolation; small-world network; power law; symmetry-breaking transition vs. dynamic instability; phantom jam; Yellowstone effect; simple, but not simplistic; binding energy vs. entropy.

**So what.** Ask "what are the two forces, and what dial shifts their balance?" Use salt on the sidewalk: shift the transition point rather than fight the phase. Study clusters, not individuals. Do not suppress every small failure (the organizational Yellowstone effect). Do not model with 137 case studies' worth of detail, and do not treat efficient markets as laws. Power-law tails and clustering signal a system near its edge.

## Chapter 8 - Phase Transitions II: The Magic Number 150

**Central argument.** As a group grows, incentives shift from stake in project outcomes to the politics of promotion; above a critical size the Invisible Axe appears and loonshots get killed even though every individual favors innovation. That size is not hard-wired by the brain (Dunbar); it follows from four structural dials, and it can be raised.

**Full stories.**

*The last-hour choice.* At 4 p.m. each employee chooses between work that raises project value and lobbying or networking for promotion. Structure sets which is rational.

*The Pfizer thought experiment.* You are a middle manager at a 100,000-person, $50B company reviewing an early-stage drug you like. Option 1: champion it, with seven years of committee slogs, about 10% odds, a hit that adds about $500M (1%), and 99,999 colleagues who claim credit if it works and point at your "reckless" wart-ignoring if it fails. Option 2: belittle it wittily and propose a modest franchise step, agreeing with your boss, advertising judgment and opening a plausible path to the boss's job next year (+30% salary, double the influence, better outside offers). At 100,000 people the rational answer is Option 2. Shrink the company to 50 people, $0 revenue, meaningful equity: a hit changes revenue by an infinite percentage and makes everyone a millionaire and a hero; no boss's job is worth taking; Option 1 is rational. Somewhere between 50 and 100,000 the two curves cross, and that crossing is M, which the equation puts near 150.

*Dunbar's number.* Right observation (Brigham Young's companies averaged 150; Gore builds a new plant at 150 parking spaces), wrong theory (extrapolating from pumpkin-sized monkey brains).

**Evidence.** The equation M = E x S^2 x F / G (derived in Appendix B). Two worked calculations: E = 50%, F = 1, S = 6, G = 12% gives 0.5 x 36 / 0.12 = 150; S = 10, G = 33% gives 0.5 x 100 / 0.33 which is about 150 again. Brigham Young and Gore as empirical clusters near 150. Politics appears first in the lowest professional-manager layer. Caveats: M is not universal (teams transform at different sizes, like solids melt at different temperatures); the notes' defaults are "typical" structures. The Pfizer scenario is a thought experiment.

**Key terms.** Invisible Axe; design parameters (G, S, E); fitness parameters (project-skill fit, return-on-politics, F); M; soft equity; hard equity; rational politics; no-politics condition; Dunbar's number; organizational phase diagram.

**So what.** (1) Treat team size as temperature; nothing about people changes, yet the phase does. (2) Read M as a budget for how many people can share a mission. (3) Remember the square on S, the highest-leverage dial. (4) The transition line is angled, so a group past 150 can restore loonshot focus by changing structure instead of shrinking. (5) Count soft equity as equity. (6) Look for politics first in the lower-middle management layer. (7) Know your four dials before blaming culture.

## Chapter 9 - The Fourth Rule: Raise the Magic Number

**Central argument.** DARPA, a roughly 200-person nursery inside a two-million-person organization, shows how adjusting the dials in M = E x S^2 x F / G lets very large organizations keep nurturing loonshots. Six levers, each mapped to a variable.

**Full stories.**

*DARPA.* No career ladder: program managers serve 2-4 year terms with badges printed with expiration dates (lever 1). They are publicly known, choose projects and negotiate contracts (soft equity, lever 2).

*McKinsey.* Important promotions are evaluated by a senior partner from a distant office or practice who interviews up to two dozen colleagues and clients over about 3 months (lever 1).

*Open innovation.* Coors' cold-activated can, Kraft's melt-resistant chocolate and the Red Balloon Challenge deliver fresh ideas and peer recognition; external peers are impartial judges.

*Project-skill fit.* Undermatch: Bahcall's toothpaste project. Overmatch: a bored Frank Lloyd Wright on a coffee machine ("idle hands are the devil's workshop"). Training raises fit twice: better output and a desire to practice new skills instead of politicking.

*Fix the middle.* Steep equity-grant curves (100% bonuses at the top, under 10% in the middle) turn the dangerous middle into *Survivor*. Celebrate results, not rank; cut parking spots, executive cafeterias and Hawaii "workshops".

*Perverse incentives.* Dead Sea Scrolls paid per fragment, so shepherds tore scrolls into scraps. The Ford Pinto's "under $2,000, under 2,000 lb" stretch goal left no time for safety ("Goals Gone Wild"). The free-rider bonus: paying junior staff on company earnings they cannot move.

*Chief incentives officer.* A strategist in cognitive biases, hard and soft equity and perverse-incentive detection, not a rubber-stamp comp specialist; small firms hire part-time, like a fractional CFO.

*Spans.* Wide (15+) in loonshot groups: Bill Coughran at Google with 180 direct reports; Bob Taylor at PARC with 40-50. Narrow (5 or fewer) in franchise groups. "What's the right span?" is like "what's the right temperature for tea?"

*The Red Balloon Challenge.* A rotating Air Force test pilot with no DARPA tenure pitched a joke: ten red weather balloons in ten undisclosed parks, $40,000 to the first team to find all ten. He was funded like any program manager. Georgia Tech, with a three-week head start, promised winnings to charity and recruited 1,400 people. MIT heard of the contest four days before and built a recursive payout ($2,000 to the balloon spotter, $1,000 to whoever recruited the spotter, $500 to that person's recruiter, and so on), so people who never left home still had a stake. They recruited 4,400 people in 36 hours and found all ten balloons in 8h 52m. A reward giving every link a stake beats altruism; no ladder let a pilot's joke become a landmark network-science experiment.

**Evidence.** DARPA's structure (200 people, 2 million, 2-4 year terms); McKinsey's process (two dozen interviews, 3 months); research finding that wage dispersion lowers productivity and cooperation and raises turnover; the Red Balloon numbers (1,400 vs. 4,400 people; $40,000; 8h 52m); spans (180; 40-50; 15+; 5 or fewer). Caveat: the notes present most levers through example, not controlled comparison.

**Key terms.** Rule 4's six levers; dangerous middle; perverse incentive; free-rider bonus; wage dispersion; chief incentives officer; open innovation; peer review as structure.

**So what.** Map every HR policy to a variable: does it raise E, S or F, or lower G? If none, ask what it is for. Before launching any reward, run the Dead Sea Scrolls test. Treat organizational fitness like marathon training (you cannot order the body to run 26 miles without the weekly runs). Do not use company-earnings bonuses for people who cannot move earnings, steep grant curves, uniform span policy, or rewards on launches, trial starts, hours or sales without checking recalls, failed trials, padded hours, unprofitable customers.

## Chapter 10 - Why the World Speaks English: The Mother of All Loonshots

**Central argument.** The scientific method was the mother of all loonshots. It ignited in fragmented Western Europe, not in the far richer empires of China, India or Islam, because Europe was a loonshot nursery among nations, satisfying three conditions the empires never met: phase separation, dynamic equilibrium, critical mass.

**Full stories.**

*Kepler's eight minutes.* An error of eight arc-minutes (under 0.05%) in Mars's position that he refused to ignore, abandoning 2,000 years of circular-orbit assumptions: the template for truth judged by measurement rather than authority.

*Tycho vs. Shen Kuo.* Equal talent, equal loss of royal patronage. Tycho hopped to Prague and hired Kepler. Shen had nowhere to go (private astronomy was illegal) and spent his last decade under house arrest "with only my writing brush and ink slab to talk to." The Song emperor declared Shen's astronomical system "good enough" after seven years and fired his assistant.

*The Royal Society of London (1660).* The first successful loonshot nursery inside one nation: Boyle's air pump, Papin's piston (buried in a cookbook on digesting bones), Newcomen's steam engine (1712), then the *Nemesis* off the Chinese coast.

*Film and drugs.* Film after *US v. Paramount* (1948): Majors acquire and distribute franchises; hundreds of small production shops nurture loonshots; partnerships connect them. Drugs after genetic engineering: pharma Majors vs. hundreds of biotechs, linked by deals (Roche-Genentech more than $30B a year). In a nursery a killed project "floats around just until a new investor raises his hand" (immunotherapy survived in small biotechs after every big pharma passed); inside a Major it stays dead. Boston's 200+ biotechs with VCs, vendors and acquirers form a self-feeding cycle that Detroit's handful of firms does not.

*Haiti vs. the Dominican Republic.* Same island, so culture, climate and geography are ruled out for why some countries adopt science faster; institutions explain it.

*Scoring Song China and Europe.* Critical mass: China yes ("the first industrial miracle": steel, paper money, printing, cannons, canal locks); Europe yes, but by importing Chinese, Indian and Islamic technology and mathematics. Phase separation: China no (the emperor personally directed astronomy and could end it by decree); Europe yes (hundreds of independent city-states and courts; Copernicus's idea taught for sixty years at Wittenberg despite poor repute; Tycho found another patron). Dynamic equilibrium: China no; Europe yes (telescopes from the Netherlands, orbits from Germany, inertia from Italy, geometry from France, synthesis in England). The framework predicts the outcome. The counterfactual is a "Chinese Vannevar Bush" in the Song court.

*Minors become Majors.* Except in baseball (antitrust exemption): Disney, Amgen, England. The Qianlong emperor's "we have nothing to learn from strange or ingenious objects" is the franchise-phase tell for nations. Kerala-school precursors of calculus and heliocentrism never ignited without critical mass.

**Evidence.** Critical-mass arithmetic: at 1-in-10 odds, ten shots give 65% chance of at least one hit; two dozen give 92%. Needham's list of Chinese firsts; Kepler's 8 arc-minutes (under 0.05%); Roche-Genentech more than $30B/yr; 200+ Boston biotechs; the Haiti/DR natural comparison. Caveat: the three-condition scoring is a framework application to history, argued through cases; the notes flag that Eurocentric accounts ignoring dynamic equilibrium are wrong (no Indian math or Islamic astronomy, no Copernicus; no Chinese technologies, no European surplus).

**Key terms.** Loonshot nursery (three conditions); critical mass; Majors/Minors; loonshot life support; the Needham Question; first-appearance vs. adoption; lily pads.

**So what.** Ask of any ecosystem "separated? exchanging? enough shots on goal?" Aim for roughly two dozen shots at 10% odds. Keep killed ideas alive on the next lily pad. Do not use franchise projects as national strategy (Great Wall, Grand Canal, Taj Mahal: magnificent, the wrong phase), do not give a supreme ruler a veto over research direction, and do not expect a lone genius to ignite without critical mass. A Major that stops nurturing loonshots becomes a colony.

## Chapter 11 - Afterword: Loonshots vs. Disruption

**Central argument.** "Disruptive innovation" is a hindsight label about a technology's effect on a market; a loonshot is a real-time label about what leaders believe. Use P/S-type loonshots to steer in real time; use disruptive/sustaining to analyze history. The two are independent axes, like height and hair color.

**Full stories.**

*The transistor.* A better switch from the largest company in the country, priced at $20 vs. $1 for a tube, sold first to high-end military buyers: sustaining, and later disrupted everything.

*PageRank.* Incrementally better results than the other 17 search engines: sustaining.

*Walmart.* Sam Walton wanted a big-city department store; his wife refused to live anywhere over 10,000 people, and Bentonville offered four quail seasons.

*IKEA.* Every defining element (showroom, flat-pack, self-service warehouse, own designers, Polish suppliers) was a desperate response to being banned from trade fairs and cut off from designers and suppliers. Worked through both lenses: disruption lens (hindsight): a low-end entrant that improved and took incumbents' customers, true and useless to Kamprad in 1953. Loonshot lens: "customers won't buy furniture from a warehouse in the countryside" (S-type, showroom); "customers won't assemble furniture" (S-type, born from an employee removing table legs to fit a Volvo); "you can't source quality furniture outside Sweden" (S-type, Poland at half price). None was a technology; all were contrarian bets forced by a competitors' blockade.

*Interferon and EPO.* The "magic bullet" for viruses became a $6B multiple-sclerosis franchise; Amgen's tiny kidney-disease niche became a $100B company.

*Zero of 20.* A pharma CEO asked how many recent launches landed within 2x of the original forecast. None.

*Low-cost airlines.* Hundreds of "disruptive" low-cost airlines failed while an incumbent (American) won with sustaining S-types.

**Evidence.** The transistor price ($20 vs. $1), PageRank vs. 17 engines, interferon $6B, EPO $100B, zero of 20 within 2x, the airline failures. Caveat: the evidence is a set of cases against a theory; it is an argument that the disruption label has no steering value, not a statistical rebuttal.

**Key terms.** Loonshot (today) vs. disruptive (in hindsight); leaf in the tornado; sustaining; nurture loonshots to test beliefs.

**So what.** Classify by the belief challenged (P: "won't work"; S: "won't make money"). Treat market-size forecasts as order-of-magnitude guesses; fund portfolios. Don't refuse an idea because it is "merely sustaining"; don't require a credible market estimate before nurturing a loonshot; don't use disruption theory to predict. "Would you rather learn you were wrong in your own pilot or in a competitor's press release?"

## (b) The big ideas of the book (numbered)

1. **Innovation is structure, not culture.** Same people, different phase. Teams flip when a control parameter such as size crosses a threshold. Ch 1, 7, 8.
2. **Loonshots and franchises are different phases and need different tools.** Wide/loose/creative for artists; narrow/tight/quantitative for soldiers. Ch 1, 2, 9.
3. **Separate, but stay connected.** Phase separation without exchange is a trap; the target is life on the edge. Ch 2.
4. **The leader manages the transfer, not the technology.** The weak link is never the supply of ideas. Ch 2, 6.
5. **Breakthroughs die at least three times, often falsely.** Budget for it and triage the test, not just the idea. Ch 3.
6. **Rejection is information; investigate with curiosity.** LSC separates persistence from stubbornness. Ch 3.
7. **There are two kinds of loonshot, and everyone has a blind side.** S-types are slower, dimmer and harder to copy. Ch 4.
8. **Success sets the trap.** A virtuous cycle plus franchise blinders plus an all-powerful leader equals Moses. Ch 4, 5.
9. **Under-forcing is as deadly as over-forcing.** The PARC Trap mirrors the Moses Trap. Ch 6.
10. **Analyze decisions, not outcomes, and analyze wins.** The system mindset. Ch 6.
11. **Every phase transition is a tug-of-war tipped by a dial.** Find the forces; find the cheapest dial. Ch 7.
12. **Above the magic number, politics rationally beats projects.** M = E S^2 F / G, about 150 by default; the Invisible Axe is structural. Ch 8.
13. **You can raise the magic number.** Six levers: distant evaluators, soft equity, fit, fix the middle, an incentives officer, spans. Ch 9.
14. **Nurseries need separation, equilibrium and critical mass.** They keep killed ideas alive; empires bury them. Ch 10.
15. **Steer by beliefs; fund portfolios.** Forecasts for loonshots are reliably wrong. Ch 11.

## (f) Open questions or limits the notes flag

- The magic number is not universal: teams transform at different sizes. The notes note M = 150 is for typical structures (E 50%, S 6, F 1, G 12%).
- Models are "simple, but not simplistic": they predict macro patterns, not individual cases (patterns.md).
- False-Fail triage risks rationalizing a true fail; pair with LSC (patterns.md).
- Mandatory trade-offs: separation alone produces the PARC Trap; nurseries cost money before earning; exhaustive evaluations and matching staff are expensive; wide spans mean more failed experiments; S-types are unglamorous and hard to evaluate.
- Critical mass is a threshold: half-measures (Detroit) don't ignite.
- The notes for the Personal Loonshot Discipline (SRT) state "none stated" for trade-offs.
- The notes cover the book content only (Scope and Limits); they do not provide implementation detail, the full Appendix B derivation, or the text of the book itself.
- Evidence is mostly case-based; the notes do not claim controlled studies for the rules.
