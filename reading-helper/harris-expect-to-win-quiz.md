# Expect to Win Quiz Book
*Expect to Win: 10 Proven Strategies for Thriving in the Workplace* by Carla A. Harris

**How to use:** Cover the answers, answer from memory (out loud or in writing), then check. Anything you miss is a chapter section to reread. Each chapter has short-answer (SA), true/false (T/F), and multiple-choice (MC) questions. A cumulative 10-question final exam is at the end. Built only from the `harris-expect-to-win` skill notes, not the book's own wording.

## Chapter 1: Authenticity — The Power Is You

**Q1 (SA).** Why does Harris call your authentic self your competitive advantage?
**A:** You were hired because your specific combination of skills, personality, and values beat every other candidate ("someone else didn't get the job that day because you were the best candidate"). Impersonating someone else gives that advantage away, and bringing the real you is the source of the confidence that makes clients buy, colleagues trust, and bosses promote.

**Q2 (T/F).** Authenticity means wearing whatever you like regardless of the firm's culture.
**A:** False. Authenticity transcends the personality: it means choosing an environment whose written and unwritten rules (attire, expected skill profile, persona) you can honestly satisfy, then owning that choice. If meeting them would be a constant struggle, you are in the wrong seat.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the elevator speech and what is it built from?
**A:** A ready-at-all-times statement of who you are, what you are good at, and what you want in the organization, deliverable in an elevator ride. It comes from your strengths/weaknesses list and your answer to why you are in this profession, firm, and seat.

**Q4 (MC).** When preparing for a client, Harris says to ask:
a) What will my colleagues think of my delivery?  b) What does the client need, what would I worry about in their shoes, and do they need educating or convincing?  c) How can I fit in more slides?  d) How can I sound like the senior banker?
**A:** b. The aim is to turn a presentation into a conversation: "When you can turn a presentation into a conversation, you have won the battle of converting a client."

**Q5 (SA).** What did the stories of Marissa and Peter illustrate?
**A:** Marissa, a Spanish-speaking Latina, suppressed her identity so thoroughly that leadership never considered her for the Latin America role she was best qualified for. Peter was charismatic and influential outside the office but quiet and credit-avoiding inside; eighteen years in, he had no promotion in eight. Both show the cost of splitting or hiding yourself.

**Q6 (SA).** What is the "wrong building" diagnosis, and what did Anne do about it?
**A:** The ladder leaning against the wrong building: a fundamental mismatch between your inspirations and the firm's success model. Anne, a 17-year financial-marketing writer, kept her job, earned a journalism master's at night over 3.5 years, networked with publishers and editors, met an author needing a ghostwriter at an event hosted by her own firm, and became a freelance writer. The play: keep the paycheck, build transferable skills, network toward the new field, then jump.

**Q7 (T/F).** Preparing hard and then sitting silent in meetings is a safe way to avoid mistakes.
**A:** False. It reads as "does she even have a clue?" and stalls promotion; it was Carla's own early mistake.

## Chapter 2: Be the Architect of Your Own Agenda

**Q1 (SA).** What goes into the personal career agenda?
**A:** A written plan with (a) broad macro career goals, (b) specific micro goals for the current seat (skills, experiences, people), (c) compensation and promotion targets, and (d) a timeline, including how long you want to stay.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the agenda's main function when a setback or an offer arrives?
**A:** It is a decision filter that takes the emotion out of decisions (stay or quit after a missed promotion) and lets you self-motivate. The agenda fixes the goals; the strategy stays flexible.

**Q3 (MC).** Harris says industry norms such as 3–4 years to VP and 8–12 to MD should be treated as:
a) law  b) a guide, not law  c) irrelevant  d) a promise from HR
**A:** b. Comparative pay data should be used only to catch egregious inequality, not marginal differences.

**Q4 (SA).** What is the "they" syndrome and what replaces it?
**A:** Explaining a stalled career with "they did this, they won't…," which makes you a victim and gives away your power. Replace it with "What did I do to contribute?" (not enough exposure, too few supporting relationships, no homework on how the organization works, waiting for projects rather than pursuing them).

**Q5 (SA).** Walk through Sherry's mistake-recovery conversation.
**A:** After a costly naked-short error on an emerging-markets deal, with her boss repeatedly resurfacing it in meetings, she took him aside and (1) owned the mistake without hedging, (2) stated the lesson (never again make a solo judgment when she knew she needed help), (3) said she had learned it and it was time to move on, and (4) asked for his explicit support going forward. He affirmed her, offered coaching, and never mentioned it in a meeting again.

**Q6 (SA).** Explain "political maniac vs. workhorse" and the key rule about them.
**A:** Political maniacs work relationships and the system; workhorses follow rules, out-work everyone, and rise on merit. People value the opposite type but reward their own, so align with a boss of your type. "Being valued and being rewarded are two different things."

**Q7 (T/F).** An off-line role (no direct client contact or revenue) is generally a career dead end.
**A:** False. It can be the fastest route to senior exposure; Carla's operations-officer stint produced a future president of Morgan Stanley as her sponsor.

## Chapter 3: You Are the Captain of Your Career — The Ninety-Day Rule

**Q1 (SA).** What three things does an organization assess, and which is most decisive?
**A:** Can do (ability), will do (motivation, teamwork, follow-through), and fit. Fit is the most subjective and most decisive.

**Q2 (SA).** What are the three deliverables of the ninety-day rule?
**A:** (1) Learn the basic skill needed to perform the job, (2) learn the unspoken rules of the game (the informal politics), (3) identify the pertinent players and the key relationships you must develop.

**Q3 (T/F).** "No one taught me" is an acceptable reason for a skills gap in your first year.
**A:** False. The organization never accepts it; it reads as lack of initiative. Training is offered, not pushed, so seek it internally, externally, or teach yourself.

**Q4 (MC).** Which is the best way to open a day-91 check-in?
a) "How am I doing?"  b) "I've attained proficiency in A, B, C; next quarter I plan D, E, F. Is there anything you'd add or change?"  c) Wait for the annual review.  d) Ask for a raise.
**A:** b. Simply asking "How am I doing?" signals insecurity.

**Q5 (SA).** What is the halo effect and what is the flip side?
**A:** A strong first-quarter impression means your work is assumed right until proven otherwise and mistakes get the benefit of the doubt. A weak one means every assignment becomes an exercise in proving yourself and you are excluded from the meaty, developmental projects that create stars.

**Q6 (SA).** What did Carla do about the group's social rhythms and hours?
**A:** She adopted the group's hours (late nights in M&A, 7 a.m. starts in capital markets) because that is where rapport is built. When the group's conversation was sports, she phoned her sports-fan father every Sunday to learn enough to open Monday's conversation. If not invited to lunch or drinks, invite yourself.

**Q7 (SA).** What question should you ask the decision-maker, and why?
**A:** Ask your manager what, in their mind, is required for you to advance. It signals your intention to advance and gives you a concrete map from a decision-maker.

## Chapter 4: Perception Is the Copilot to Reality

**Q1 (SA).** State Harris's perception formula and which half to change.
**A:** Perception = self-presentation + the baggage of the beholder. Change self-presentation (behavior, speech, dress), because it is infinitely easier to change.

**Q2 (SA).** What are the "three adjectives" and the two constraints on choosing them?
**A:** The words you want people to use to describe you when you are not in the room (because every major decision is made without you). They must be true of you and valued by the organization's business model.

**Q3 (SA).** How do you find which adjectives your firm values?
**A:** What recruiters said they were hiring for; the annual report, marketing literature, and website; and asking "How does my company make money? What skills does it value? Why am I relevant to the product/profit equation?"

**Q4 (SA).** Describe Carla's "tough" campaign.
**A:** After being told "I don't know if you're tough enough," she worked *tough* into nearly every sentence and acted congruently for about three months ("You'd better make sure it's right, because you know I'm tough!") until a VP was overheard warning his team to double-check assumptions because "Carla Harris is so tough."

**Q5 (MC).** The fan/non-fan feedback audit involves:
a) 1 fan  b) 2–3 non-fans and 2–3 fans, with non-fans met over coffee as equals  c) only your boss  d) an anonymous survey
**A:** b. Whose perception to fix first: the boss's, because it is the hardest to change and the most influential.

**Q6 (T/F).** The associate who wanted VP was promoted mainly because she dressed like the women officers.
**A:** False, and Carla warns you can't dress your way to success. Her objective record was already strong; dressing like the officers and holding a pre-meeting with her boss before group meetings supplied the missing subjective evidence of officer-level behavior.

**Q7 (SA).** Name some behaviors that create a negative perception.
**A:** Always soliciting opinions before deciding (indecisive), always saying why things won't work (not commercial), belittling others in meetings (a bully), and running from people who don't like you (the misperception hardens).

## Chapter 5: The Mentor, the Sponsor, the Adviser

**Q1 (SA).** Define adviser, mentor, and sponsor.
**A:** Adviser: answers isolated career questions without knowing your broader agenda. Mentor: gives tailored developmental advice in the context of your strengths, weaknesses, and agenda, and hears the good, bad, and ugly. Sponsor: uses internal political and social capital to move your career, arguing your case behind closed doors ("carry your paper into the room").

**Q2 (SA).** What is the test for whether someone is a mentor or only an adviser?
**A:** If you are not comfortable sharing all the facts with them, they are an adviser. A company-assigned "mentor" is really an adviser until trust is earned.

**Q3 (MC).** A good sponsor must have:
a) seniority only  b) a seat at the decision table in your functional area, political and social capital, and willingness to spend it on you  c) a friendly manner  d) membership in the same ethnic group
**A:** b. Ideally it is your boss; if not, your boss can be a bridge to someone who is.

**Q4 (SA).** What is the sponsor ROI?
**A:** Sponsors spend capital and expect a return, chiefly the credit and expanded power base of being associated with a star. Reciprocate with information, relationships, and support, without compromising your integrity.

**Q5 (SA).** What did Carla do before her 1999 managing-director promotion?
**A:** She realized she could not name who would passionately argue her case on the committee. She asked a senior MD with a seat on the committee directly, saying she needed someone to argue for her, specifically him, and that she would give him the strongest package. He agreed. She also recruited a second advocate without a seat but with influence over those who had one. She was promoted.

**Q6 (T/F).** Asking someone directly to sponsor you has no real downside.
**A:** True per Harris: a yes gets you an advocate, and a no gets you the reason the organization might not back you.

**Q7 (SA).** What should you secure when joining a firm or switching divisions, and how many?
**A:** Explicit sponsor commitments from at least two people, asked of those selling you hardest ("Who will sponsor me? Will you be a member of my success team?"). Your old sponsorship stays behind when you change divisions.

## Chapter 6: Leverage Your Voice

**Q1 (SA).** What are the three functions of voice?
**A:** (1) Creates perception, (2) creates a level of expectation of your contribution, (3) creates the mechanism to articulate your expectations of the organization.

**Q2 (SA).** Explain the 50/50 rule and the fix.
**A:** Before you speak, a first-time observer gives you 50/50 odds of being smart or not. Silence lets the coin land on "stupid." Say something in every meeting; if you have nothing to add, paraphrase what someone else said.

**Q3 (SA).** How should you answer when you're unsure in front of a client or senior?
**A:** Confidently, with room to adjust: "In my judgment the answer is X, Y, Z, but I'll double-check a couple of sources and get back to you." Speak audibly, since a drop in volume is the tell of uncertainty. Visible doubt destroys credibility.

**Q4 (MC).** The two-thirds evaluation rule says a review should be:
a) all about last year  b) one-third lessons from last year and two-thirds next-year aspirations, needs, and timelines  c) two-thirds complaints  d) skipped
**A:** b. Goals carry timelines (3–6 months for most items), and you should ask for next year's promotion this year.

**Q5 (SA).** What was the "eureka year"?
**A:** In a tough year with cutbacks, Carla worked hard, said nothing about wanting promotion, and wasn't promoted. Her boss said peers had been pleading their cases for months and she had been silent. Lesson: in marginal decisions, the person who voiced expectations wins the tie.

**Q6 (SA).** What was the "yes, uh-huh" discipline and its result?
**A:** Told that her boss hated giving her feedback because "she has an answer for everything," she responded in the next review only with acknowledgments ("yes," "uh-huh," "oh really? okay"). The feedback became specific, and the relationship evolved from manager to adviser to sponsor.

**Q7 (T/F).** Keeping your head down is a safe strategy.
**A:** False: "Keeping your head down doesn't keep you from getting shot; it only increases the chances that you won't see the bullet coming."

## Chapter 7: Have a Penchant for Taking Risks

**Q1 (SA).** State Harris's success equation.
**A:** Achieving success and moving ahead = performing extremely well + aligning with internal politics + taking risks.

**Q2 (SA).** Distinguish latent risk from forward risk.
**A:** Latent risk is out of your control and untied to your performance (takeover, division sold, sponsor leaving abruptly, a mismatched role). Forward risk is a chosen action with an uncertain outcome that leaves you better off regardless (skills, experience, finances, positioning). Stacking forward risks builds a platform to navigate latent shocks.

**Q3 (MC).** Which risk type fits "I know the outcome I want but not how I'll get there"?
a) Calculated  b) Studied  c) Step-out-on-faith  d) Latent
**A:** c. Reserve it for when you are the primary person affected and repercussions on others are very small.

**Q4 (SA).** What are the three "test the waters" questions, and the decision rule?
**A:** (1) Will it give me skills and experience I wouldn't get staying in my seat for the next twelve months? (2) Will it expose me to people and relationships I wouldn't otherwise reach? (3) Will it create new branches on my decision tree? Three yeses means take it.

**Q5 (SA).** When should you take a studied risk?
**A:** When the action is clear but reactions can't all be anticipated, the upside must be markedly greater than the downside. (Example: Carla recommended postponing an oversubscribed Internet IPO; the next day NASDAQ fell over 100 points, and the client returned months later and priced successfully.)

**Q6 (T/F).** Declining an opportunity carries no risk.
**A:** False. Declining is also a risk (annoying the boss, losing an accelerator). Do it with a solid rationale ready and willingness to accept repercussions, as Carla did when she declined the new retail-distribution effort in her MD year and was promoted.

**Q7 (SA).** What does F-E-A-R stand for and what is the "predecessor rule"?
**A:** False Evidence Appearing Real. Predecessor rule: "If you're doing your job the way your predecessor was doing it, you, too, will likely soon find yourself a predecessor."

## Chapter 8: Power in the Network

**Q1 (SA).** Name the four relationship types.
**A:** Upward (boss and higher), lateral (peers and the assistants of senior people), downward (junior people), and external (nonprofit boards, other-firm colleagues, entrepreneurs, headhunters, clergy, and so on).

**Q2 (SA).** Where does a network's power come from?
**A:** Relationship strength, how you manage it, and the role you play (connector, information gatherer, or helpful person), not the number of business cards.

**Q3 (MC).** The recommended cadence for your most important relationships is:
a) one e-mail a year  b) one face-to-face plus four voice-to-voice contacts a year  c) daily calls  d) only at promotion time
**A:** b. Other relationships get e-mail updates that include what you are doing and your future goals.

**Q4 (SA).** Why does Harris emphasize assistants?
**A:** The assistant is closest to a CEO/CFO/COO, controls access, knows the mood and the best moment to approach, and can frame your request favorably. Ignoring or being rude to one is a career derailer. Honor everyone's power.

**Q5 (SA).** How did Carla produce her first CD through her network?
**A:** She took a banker-musician colleague to lunch; he mapped the steps and introduced a colleague with a recording studio, who introduced a Broadway musical director who agreed to produce after an audition. A senior internal sponsor walked her to a music-label CEO for distribution. Every link was a relationship she already had.

**Q6 (T/F).** You are competing with your peers, so you should avoid asking them for help.
**A:** False. Asking a peer for help starts a long-term relationship; you compete with yourself ("there is plenty of room at the top"); "don't hate, gravitate" toward the peer who is ahead.

## Chapter 9: Balance Is a Necessity

**Q1 (SA).** How does Harris redefine balance?
**A:** Not work versus family, but integrating your passions and giving back into your professional life.

**Q2 (SA).** What happens without something outside the job that makes you feel alive?
**A:** "Your life becomes a function of somebody else's day" (the boss's mood, a deal's outcome, a Friday disagreement). Professional life will never consistently fulfill you.

**Q3 (MC).** To "calendarize," Harris suggests:
a) quitting email  b) mapping a typical day, taking two lunches a week for something joyful, and batching reading and e-mail into fixed blocks  c) working later  d) delegating everything
**A:** b. This is "congregate like actions": e-mail only in several fixed half-hour slots.

**Q4 (T/F).** Honoring your passions at work is a distraction that hurts your career.
**A:** False. In Carla's case, the gospel CDs and Carnegie Hall concerts produced a Sunday New York Times Business feature (CD released 2000) and a half-page Wall Street Journal story (2006), raised her standing with clients, and opened new relationships.

**Q5 (SA).** Why is giving back part of the success equation?
**A:** It is a duty of gratitude and its own reward, but it also refuels you, keeps you humble, reminds you why you work hard, and unplanned builds client relationships and visibility ("You never know who may be sitting in the chair next to you at that nonprofit board meeting").

## Chapter 10: Expect to Win

**Q1 (SA).** Contrast "know," "maybe," and "hope."
**A:** At eighteen Carla *knew* she'd get into Harvard, HBS, and a top Wall Street firm. After bad deals and disappointing pay, her language slid to *maybe* and *hope*: "mediocre thoughts, mediocre results." The fix is to tell yourself daily that you know it will happen, and act "as if" until you believe it.

**Q2 (SA).** List the five principles of the winner's lens.
**A:** (1) Identify the best outcome early and devise a strategy; (2) fearlessness: either the goal is achieved or a valuable lesson is learned; (3) assume everything works out for your good; (4) goals can't be reached alone, so engage in interdependent relationships; (5) acquire the skills to be the best, a positive outlier.

**Q3 (SA).** What are the four sheets of the annual reflection?
**A:** Who have I been this year? Who do I want to be? What lessons have I learned this year? What do I want to accomplish in the next year? Include weaknesses; write in third person if it's hard.

**Q4 (MC).** When you feel low, the lift-someone-up reset says to:
a) wait it out  b) ask what you can do today for somebody else to lift them up  c) quit  d) vent to peers
**A:** b. Posture and attitude follow, and power is reclaimed.

**Q5 (T/F).** Winners are motivated mainly when things are going well.
**A:** False. "Selectively motivated" (working hard and smart only when things go well) is the trait Harris says winners lack.

**Q6 (SA).** What is the sign of complacency and the response?
**A:** Work has become rote and effortless. Time for a new challenge or a new place; return to the agenda and ask whether you are getting the experiences, people, and lifestyle you came for.

## Chapter 11: My Most Precious Pearl

**Q1 (SA).** What is the "eleventh pearl"?
**A:** Know not only who you are but whose you are. For Harris it is her Christian faith ("I am a child of God"); for any reader, something over and above the job to turn to for strength, guidance, and comfort.

**Q2 (SA).** What is "lesson or blessing"?
**A:** Every career event is one or the other, and both are to be leveraged to keep moving forward and upward ("Consider it all good").

**Q3 (SA).** What should you do if you can't give your best?
**A:** Identify the problem (not challenged, not motivated, treated unfairly), apply your tools (intelligence, experience, faith, the pearls), find the solution, and hand the remainder over.

**Q4 (MC).** In Harris's mapping, Romans 8:28 stands for:
a) No challenge is insurmountable  b) Even when things look bad, they will work out for my benefit  c) I need not fight alone  d) Don't worry about anything
**A:** b. (Philippians 4:13 is the insurmountable-challenge verse, Hebrews 13:5 is "no battle alone," and Philippians 4:6–8 is "don't worry.")

**Q5 (SA).** How did the faith practice begin?
**A:** As a second-year associate under an insecure manager uninterested in training her, she was told by a close friend's mother to read and pray Psalm 91 and Psalm 4 daily. These became a mantra, then "armor," and the start of using faith deliberately as a tool.

**Q6 (T/F).** Hard work guarantees the assignment, promotion, or platform on your timeline.
**A:** False. That is the belief her early career dismantled; things like timing and others' decisions are beyond control, so you need a resource for them.

# Cumulative Final Exam (10 questions)

**F1 (SA).** Harris says no organization is a true meritocracy. Why, and what does she add to hard work and intelligence?
**A:** Because people evaluate people. She adds strategy, relationships, politics, tenacity, and faith.

**F2 (SA).** Give the success equation and say which chapter's ideas map onto each term (performance, politics, risk).
**A:** Moving ahead = performing extremely well + aligning with internal politics + taking risks. Performance is supported by Ch 3 (ninety days, skills); politics by Ch 2 (ecosystem), Ch 3 (unspoken rules), and Ch 5/8 (sponsors, network); risk is Ch 7.

**F3 (MC).** Which pair correctly names Ch 3's and Ch 4's key tools?
a) Ninety-day rule; three adjectives  b) Four-sheet reflection; 50/50 rule  c) Elevator speech; test-the-waters  d) Calendarize; lens
**A:** a.

**F4 (SA).** Compare the "they" syndrome (Ch 2) with the "selectively motivated" trap (Ch 10). What do both give up?
**A:** Both forfeit power and agency: blaming others makes you passive and a victim; working hard only when things go well makes motivation conditional. The cure in each is to focus on what you control (what did *I* contribute; show up with your best self every day).

**F5 (T/F).** The three adjectives you choose should be whatever the firm values most, even if untrue of you.
**A:** False. They must be true of you and valued by the business model; untrue adjectives lead to burnout and an inconsistent perception.

**F6 (SA).** How do silence and the 50/50 rule (Ch 6) connect to the "smart or stupid?" perception (Ch 1 and 4)?
**A:** Submerging yourself out of fear creates the perception "I don't know if she's smart or stupid." Silence lets the 50/50 coin land on "stupid," so speaking in every meeting (even a paraphrase) shapes perception.

**F7 (SA).** What are the sponsor's three criteria and the two-commitment rule?
**A:** A seat at the decision table in your functional area, political and social capital, and willingness to spend it on you (ideally your boss, or a bridge to someone). Get explicit commitments from at least two people before joining or switching divisions.

**F8 (MC).** An offer pays the same as your current job but is bigger. Per the cheatsheet, you should:
a) refuse because pay is equal  b) take it as a platform to argue for pay and authority later  c) ask permission first  d) consult no one
**A:** b. (Similarly, a better-paid move at another firm is compensated risk; take it.)

**F9 (SA).** Name three ways Carla's outside passions (singing, faith, giving back) helped her career, across Ch 1, 8, 9, and 11.
**A:** Passions are relationship bridges (Ch 1); her network helped produce her first CD, which became a conversation piece with clients (Ch 8); the CDs and Carnegie Hall concerts, with proceeds to two schools, earned New York Times and Wall Street Journal coverage and rising standing with clients (Ch 9); faith is her steady constant through uncontrollable events (Ch 11).

**F10 (SA).** Give four timing/number rules from the book.
**A:** Any four of: 90 days to prove value, with day 91 advertising points on the board; 3 adjectives; about 3 months to install or overwrite a perception; 2–3 fans plus 2–3 non-fans in an audit; one-third lessons and two-thirds aspirations in an evaluation; check-ins every 2 months, report card done by month 6; 8–10 mentor touches per year; 1 face-to-face plus 4 calls a year for key contacts; 2 sponsor commitments; 2 lunch hours a week for passions; perform at the next level 6–12 months before you want the title.
