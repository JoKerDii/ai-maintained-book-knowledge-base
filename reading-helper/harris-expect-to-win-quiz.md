# Expect to Win: Quiz Book

*Carla A. Harris, "Expect to Win: 10 Proven Strategies for Thriving in the Workplace" (2009).*

> **Source note.** This quiz reflects the `harris-expect-to-win` skill's chapter notes (SKILL.md, glossary, patterns, cheatsheet and the eleven chapter files), **not the book's own wording**. Names, numbers and stories are only those that appear in those notes. If the notes are silent on something, it is not quizzed.

## How to use this quiz

1. Work one chapter section at a time, right after reading that chapter (or its notes). Cover the answers (everything under **A:**) and write or say your answer first.
2. Question types are mixed: short answer (SA), true/false (T/F), multiple choice (MC), "explain in your own words" (EXPLAIN) and scenario/application (SCENARIO). Every section has at least two scenarios.
3. Score yourself honestly: full credit if you got the idea and the key number or term; half if you got the idea only. Below 70% in a section means re-read that chapter's notes.
4. Finish with the 20-question cumulative final exam at the end. Chapter numbering follows the skill: Ch 1 to Ch 11 (the book's "eleventh pearl" is Ch 11).
5. Quick reference: the **Success Equation** is *Moving Ahead = Performing Extremely Well + Aligning with Internal Politics + Taking Risks* (Ch 7).

## Chapter 1: Authenticity, the Power Is You

**Q1 (SA).** What does Harris say is your only durable competitive advantage, and why?
**A.** The specific combination of skills, personality and values that is *you*. You were hired because that combination beat every other candidate ("Someone else didn't get the job that day because you were the best candidate"), so impersonating someone else throws away the very thing that won you the seat.

**Q2 (T/F).** Authenticity means dressing and behaving however you like, even if the firm's code is different.
**A.** False. "Authenticity transcends the personality" means *choosing* an environment whose written and unwritten rules (attire, expected skill profile, persona) you can honestly satisfy and then owning that choice. If meeting the industry's expectations would be a constant struggle, you are in the wrong seat, not being inauthentic.

**Q3 (MC).** Which of these is NOT one of the four steps of the Ch 1 strengths/goals approach? (a) List strengths and weaknesses; (b) answer *why* you are in this profession, firm and seat; (c) copy the style of the most senior colleague; (d) lead with strengths, schedule work on weaknesses, and be ready to explain why you deserve the seat, promotion and top-tier bonus.
**A.** (c). Copying a senior colleague's style is the anti-pattern the chapter warns against: acting a role spends mental capacity you should use to learn products, adapt commercially and read the political environment.

**Q4 (SA).** What is the elevator speech and where is it derived from?
**A.** A ready-at-all-times statement of who you are, what you are good at and what you want in the organization, deliverable in the time of an elevator ride. It is derived from the strengths/goals inventory and rehearsed until automatic. Use it for unplanned encounters with senior people, networking events and promotion season.

**Q5 (EXPLAIN).** In your own words, explain "presentation to conversation" and what Harris asked herself to prepare.
**A.** Instead of preparing to impress colleagues with delivery, prepare around the client: *What information do they need? What would I worry about in their shoes? Do they need educating or convincing?* "When you can turn a presentation into a conversation, you have won the battle of converting a client." Evidence: after "flat and rote" feedback, Carla used this for a Fortune 500 IPO meeting with the CEO, CFO, COO and head of HR (a trusted colleague spent political capital to get her in the room), and the CFO called the senior banker next day with rave reviews.

**Q6 (SA).** What happened to Marissa, and what is the lesson?
**A.** A Spanish-speaking Latina who suppressed her identity so thoroughly that leadership never considered her for the Latin America role she was best qualified for. Lesson: erasing distinguishing traits to fit in can cost you assignments; your background and outside traits are relationship bridges, not liabilities. (The SKILL.md summary calls her a Latina consultant.)

**Q7 (SA).** What is Peter's story and what pattern does it show?
**A.** Charismatic and influential outside the office, but quiet and credit-avoiding inside; eighteen years in, no promotion in eight. It is the "outside/inside split": your outside self is strong and your inside self silent, which stalls a career.

**Q8 (SCENARIO).** A mid-level analyst has strong reviews but no passion for her work, and notices she lights up when writing about nonprofits and spirituality. She is tempted to quit tomorrow. Which Ch 1 story applies, and what is the plan?
**A.** Anne's "wrong building" story: a 17-year financial-marketing writer with good reviews but no passion, who kept her job, spent 3.5 years earning a journalism master's at night, networked relentlessly with publishers and editors, and at an event hosted by her own Wall Street firm met an author who needed a ghostwriter. Now a fulfilled freelance writer. The play: keep the paycheck, build transferable skills, network toward the new field, then jump; Harris says the fix takes a deliberate 12-24-month plan.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A junior banker's boss is anxious, second-guesses everyone and keeps smart staff on edge; the banker has gone quiet in meetings and feels her confidence draining. What concepts apply and what should she do?
**A.** The insecure boss / submerging pattern: an insecure boss preoccupied with being "found out" is a common cause of lost voice, and silence produces the perception "I don't know if she's smart or stupid." Treat the self-doubt as a *signal to reorient*, not who she has become: spend the next week deliberately showcasing strengths, and ask "Am I showing them?" before blaming the organization. (Carla herself lost her voice under an insecure boss and reclaimed it.)

**Q10 (T/F).** Hiding your hobbies, faith and outside interests at work protects you.
**A.** False. They are legitimate relationship bridges; hiding them can cost assignments (Marissa). Harris's own singing and faith later became connection points with clients (see Ch 9).

## Chapter 2: Be the Architect of Your Own Agenda

**Q1 (SA).** What does a personal career agenda contain?
**A.** Four elements: broad (macro) career goals, specific (micro) goals for the current seat (skills, experiences, people, timeline), compensation and promotion targets, and a timeline. It is built by answering in writing why you joined, which skills and experiences you want, which people you want exposure to, your timeline for projects and promotion, and how long you want to stay.

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** The agenda "fixes goals but keeps strategy flexible." Explain, using the off-line example.
**A.** The destination is fixed but the route can change. Harris took an off-line (non-revenue) operations-officer role; it bought exposure to senior executives and produced her future sponsor, a future president of Morgan Stanley. A rigid plan might have rejected the unglamorous role.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the "comparative agenda" and how should you use it?
**A.** Industry norms (the notes give examples of 3-4 years to VP and 8-12 to MD) and comparative pay data (HR salary bands; headhunter bands as you get senior). Use them as a guide for rhythm, and act on pay data only to catch *egregious* inequality, not marginal differences.

**Q4 (MC).** The "they" syndrome is best replaced by which question? (a) Why are they against me? (b) What did *I* do to contribute? (c) Who can I blame? (d) When will they change?
**A.** (b). Blaming "they" makes you a victim and gives away power. Usual contributing factors: too little exposure to the right people, too few supporting relationships, no homework on how the organization works, waiting for projects instead of pursuing them.

**Q5 (SA).** Give the four-part mistake-recovery conversation (Sherry's story).
**A.** Sherry, largely self-taught, guessed on an emerging-markets deal because she did not understand share allocation and the naked short; the stock rose after pricing and covering the short cost the firm revenue, and her boss resurfaced it in meetings for weeks. In one private conversation she (1) owned it without hedging, (2) stated the lesson (never again a solo judgment when she knew she needed help), (3) said the repeated call-outs showed it was time to move on ("don't you agree?"), and (4) asked for his explicit support going forward. He affirmed her ability, offered coaching and never mentioned it in a meeting again.

**Q6 (T/F).** "Being valued and being rewarded are the same thing."
**A.** False. The ecosystem concept: political maniacs and workhorses each *value* the opposite type but *reward* their own. Line up in your reporting line with a boss of your own type.

**Q7 (SA).** What is a chit / IOU, and why use it?
**A.** When you are passed over, you can engage decision-makers constructively and extract an organizational obligation for the next cycle. It is far cheaper than restarting political capital at a new firm ("political start-up costs"). Spend chits on what is on your agenda, not on a colleague's marginal six-month head start.

**Q8 (EXPLAIN).** Why are Gina's and Hazel's stories cautionary?
**A.** Gina rejected a small new product, judging by current revenue rather than skill-building and first-mover upside; she waited six more years for a now-mature product while the person who took it was promoted in three. Hazel resented Curtis's earlier promotion although he made sacrifices (Hong Kong, uprooting family) that she chose not to make: their choice, their timeline. Measure yourself against your agenda, not a peer's report card. (The notes give these in brief only.)

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A senior associate is passed over for promotion and is drafting a resignation. What does Ch 2 advise?
**A.** Return to the written agenda: Am I living my life the way I want? Am I learning what I came to learn? If yes, a few months' delay in a decades-long career is noise. Rage-quitting forfeits chits, track record and support and usually lengthens the road. Engage decision-makers to create a chit, and stay if the goals remain reachable.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A manager hears her company is being acquired and the floor is full of gossip. What does the chapter suggest?
**A.** "Corporate chaos as opportunity": while others gossip, look for where you can excel and build relationships with the incoming people. Anchor to the agenda rather than the rumor mill.

## Chapter 3: Captain of Your Career, the Ninety-Day Rule

**Q1 (SA).** What three things does an organization assess, and which matters most?
**A.** *Can do* (ability), *will do* (motivation, teamwork, follow-through) and *fit*. Fit is the most subjective and most decisive: do you catch on to formal and informal rules, do people like you, do you mesh or act like a fish out of water.

**Q2 (SA).** State the three deliverables of the ninety-day rule.
**A.** (1) Learn the basic skill needed to perform; (2) learn the unspoken rules of the game (informal politics); (3) identify the pertinent players and the key relationships to develop. Keep a "maniacal calendar" scheduling skills, relationships and product knowledge by hour, day, week and month.

**Q3 (T/F).** You typically have a year to get up to speed in a new role.
**A.** False. By the six-month look you should be on par or ahead; at the one-year mark you must be on par or ahead of peers, since catching up later takes a long time.

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** What are "points on the board" and the halo effect, and how do they connect?
**A.** Points on the board are early accomplishments you record and advertise from day 91 so people do not form impressions on their own. A strong first quarter creates a halo: your work is assumed right and mistakes get the benefit of the doubt. A weak first quarter (the "curse of mediocrity") means proving yourself on every assignment and exclusion from meaty developmental projects.

**Q5 (MC).** Which opening is recommended for the day-91 conversation? (a) "How am I doing?" (b) "I've attained proficiency in A, B, C; next quarter I plan D, E, F; is there anything you'd add or change?" (c) Wait for the review. (d) Ask for a raise.
**A.** (b). "How am I doing?" signals insecurity. If the start was rocky, acknowledge the steeper learning curve, name what you have mastered, and ask how to move forward most effectively.

**Q6 (SA).** Why does the notes say "no one taught me" never works?
**A.** The organization hears it as lack of initiative. Training is offered, not pushed: seek it internally, externally, or teach yourself.

**Q7 (SA).** Describe Carla's presentation-skills sprint.
**A.** Two years into banking she realized she lacked the board-room presentation experience VP required because she had never put a time frame on the goal. She ran the ninety-day rule twice: quarter one, internal reps (campus recruiting presentations, shadowing strong presenters); quarter two, external reps (finance/treasury committee leadership at nonprofits, presenting numbers compellingly). By the VP decision presenting was no longer an impediment.

**Q8 (SCENARIO).** A new associate's group goes out for drinks every Thursday and nobody has invited her; they also talk sports, which she does not follow. What applies?
**A.** Invite yourself: often no one is invited, it is habit, or it is informal hazing in up-or-out environments that you pass by showing up. Treat lunch, coffee and the Thursday bar as relationship infrastructure. For the sports gap, build a bridge as Carla did: she phoned her sports-fan father every Sunday to learn enough to open Monday's conversation.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** An associate keeps being staffed on low-profile deals. What does Ch 3 tell her?
**A.** Learn how assignments really get made. The assignments associate nominally matches deals to development needs but in practice assigns whoever is requested or free, so *request* the specific transactions and senior people you need. Passive acceptance builds an experience deficit.

**Q10 (SA).** What should you ask your manager about advancement, and why?
**A.** Ask directly what, in their mind, is required for you to advance. It signals intent to advance and gives you a concrete map from a decision-maker, creating an informal understanding. Harris notes women and women of color often do not know what it takes. Also find published skill requirements by level and perform at the next level 6-12 months before you want the title.

## Chapter 4: Perception Is the Copilot to Reality

**Q1 (SA).** Give the perception formula.
**A.** Perception = Self-Presentation + Baggage of the Beholder. Self-presentation (behavior, speech, dress) is infinitely easier to change, so start there.

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** What is the three-adjective exercise and its two constraints?
**A.** Answer "How do I want people to describe me when I am not in the room?" with at least three adjectives, then walk, talk and behave consistently with them. They must be (a) genuinely true of you and (b) valued by the organization's business model. It matters because every major decision on pay, promotion and assignments is made when you are not in the room.

**Q3 (SA).** How do you find the adjectives your firm values?
**A.** What recruiters said they were hiring for; the annual report, marketing literature and website; and the questions "How does my company make money? What skills does it value? Why am I relevant to the profit equation?" If there is no intersection, you are in the wrong seat/firm/industry: spend 12-24 months harvesting and leverage it into the next move.

**Q4 (SA).** Describe the "tough" campaign.
**A.** Carla was told "I don't know if you're tough enough." She assumed it might be true and shared, and for about three months worked *tough* into nearly every sentence ("You'd better make sure it's right, because you know I'm tough!") and acted congruently, until a VP was overheard warning his team to double-check assumptions "because Carla Harris is so tough." Perceptions can be rewritten with repeated self-description plus matching behavior.

**Q5 (SA).** How does the 4-6 person feedback audit work?
**A.** Pick 2-3 non-fans and 2-3 fans; ask each for your strengths and what to work on. Meet non-fans over breakfast or coffee, as equals, not across a desk. It surfaces the true perception, opens dialogue and often recruits the non-fan onto your success team simply because you asked.

**Q6 (T/F).** Fix the perception of your peers first, since they are most numerous.
**A.** False. Fix the boss's perception first: it is hardest to change (fewer, less frequent interactions), most influential, and propagates formally and informally.

**Q7 (MC).** The associate who wanted VP was promoted after two changes. Which? (a) Dress like the women officers and hold a pre-meeting with her boss before each group meeting; (b) Change firms and ask for a raise; (c) Present more slides; (d) Stay silent.
**A.** (a). Her boss began to perceive she wanted and could handle an officer role and let her run meetings. Carla's caveat: you cannot dress your way to success; the objective evidence was already there, the subjective evidence was missing.

**Q8 (SCENARIO).** A high performer is always asked to prepare background assumptions but never asked to present the substance when seniors are present. Which concept applies and what are two actions?
**A.** Behaving upward and perception management: seniors treat her as an associate because she presents as one. Dress, speak and run meetings like the next level, and hold a pre-meeting with the boss to propose how the meeting runs and what part she will play.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A woman of color finds one colleague consistently cool toward her and has started avoiding him. In a small firm, his view is spreading. What does Ch 4 advise?
**A.** Do not run: avoidance lets the misperception harden (Harris sees this often among women and women of color). Meet him over coffee as an equal and ask for feedback; asking alone changes views. Also treat any bias that precedes you as "baggage of the beholder": remember he has no firsthand data, refuse to carry his baggage, and make the experience so positive it changes how he treats the next person who looks like you.

**Q10 (EXPLAIN).** How should the adjectives change as you rise?
**A.** Junior: eager, resourceful, analytical. Mid-level: gets things done, mobilizes others, strategic potential. Senior: visionary, commercial, client-focused, authority. Refresh them as what is valued shifts; Ch 10's annual reflection is where you do it.

## Chapter 5: The Mentor, the Sponsor, the Adviser

**Q1 (SA).** Define adviser, mentor and sponsor in one line each.
**A.** Adviser: answers isolated career questions without knowing your broader agenda. Mentor: gives tailored developmental advice in the context of your strengths, weaknesses and agenda, and hears the good, the bad and the ugly. Sponsor: spends internal political and social capital to move your career, arguing your case behind closed doors.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the test for whether someone is a mentor?
**A.** If you are not comfortable sharing *all* the facts with them, they are an adviser, not a mentor. Company-assigned "mentors" are really advisers until trust is earned (they aim at integration, not career maximization).

**Q3 (EXPLAIN).** What does "carry your paper into the room" mean and what is the sponsor test?
**A.** Decisions are made behind closed doors, and someone must argue for you there. The test: "Who is carrying my paper into the room?" If you cannot name the person passionately committed to arguing your case, your promotion is at risk regardless of credentials; stop working and go recruit one.

**Q4 (MC).** Which is NOT a sponsor selection criterion? (a) A seat at the decision table in *your functional area*; (b) political/social capital; (c) willingness to spend it on you; (d) being the most senior person in the firm regardless of area.
**A.** (d). A respected voice in asset management cannot carry an investment-banking promotion. Ideally the boss, or the boss as a bridge to someone who qualifies.

**Q5 (SA).** Describe Carla's 1999 MD promotion move.
**A.** In a crowded room of advocates, she could not name who would passionately argue her case. She approached a senior MD with a committee seat: it is important I'm promoted this year; I need someone on the committee to argue for me; specifically you, because you've seen my work and the client feedback; I'll make sure you walk in with the strongest possible package. He agreed. She also recruited a second advocate without a seat but with strong influence over those who had one. She was promoted.

**Q6 (SA).** How many sponsor commitments should you secure before joining a firm or changing divisions, and how do you ask?
**A.** At least two explicit commitments. Ask the people selling you hardest: "Given everyone I've met, who can I count on to be committed to my success? Who will sponsor me? Will you be a member of my success team?" When changing divisions your old sponsorship stays behind, so ask again.

**Q7 (T/F).** Asking directly for sponsorship has a downside risk, so wait to be chosen.
**A.** False. A yes gets you an advocate; a no gives you the reason the organization might not back you. Women and women of color are less often chosen along lines of commonality, so finding mentors and sponsors is your responsibility.

**Q8 (SA).** What is "sponsor ROI" and how do you handle it?
**A.** Sponsors spend capital and expect a return, chiefly the credit and expanded power base of being associated with a star. Reciprocate with information, relationships and support, without compromising integrity; if asked for something you do not believe in, decline and find another way to add value. Evidence: a former mentee close to a client decision-maker gave Carla's team post-pitch feedback, vouched for their integrity, and they were named book runner.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A mentor tells a mentee to take a step that would benefit the mentor if it works and leave them unscathed if it fails; the mentee also notes the mentor would never do it themselves. What concept applies?
**A.** "Agenda of one: Y-O-U." Advice must be free of the mentor's bias. Downgrade the person to adviser but keep the relationship. Also distinguish discomfort from danger: if uncomfortable because it stretches you, follow it; if it could seriously backfire, trust your gut, tell them honestly and own the consequence.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A manager has an adviser she trusts and wants more; the adviser has helped for a year. How does she convert, and what if the answer is no?
**A.** Thank them for advice so far, say they could be extraordinarily helpful over the next couple of years, ask if they would act as mentor, and state the expectation of frank, confidential, honest counsel. Accept a no gracefully (good mentors take few mentees) and keep them as an adviser. Then maintain 8-10 touches a year (Carla every six to eight weeks), follow through on advice, and close the loop with outcomes.

## Chapter 6: Leverage Your Voice

**Q1 (SA).** What are the three functions of voice?
**A.** (1) It creates perception; (2) it creates a level of expectation ("every time she speaks it's value-add"); (3) it is the mechanism for articulating what you expect from the organization (promotions, pay, assignments).

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** Explain the 50/50 rule and Harris's fix for having nothing to add.
**A.** Before you speak, a first-time observer gives even odds you are smart or not; silence lets the coin land on "stupid." If you have nothing to add, paraphrase someone else's point ("move a preposition and a noun") to get your voice into the room.

**Q3 (SA).** Give two examples of word-framing.
**A.** To seem analytical: "There are three reasons..." or "I used five key assumptions..." To seem creative: "This called for creative thinking, so I..." The listener interprets you inside the frame you set.

**Q4 (SA).** What is Harris's adaptation of "frequently wrong, but never in doubt"?
**A.** The lesson is not that being wrong is fine, but that visible doubt destroys credibility. When unsure, give a confident answer with room to adjust: "In my judgment the answer is X, Y, Z, but I'll double-check a couple of sources and get back to you." Keep volume up; dropping volume is the tell.

**Q5 (SA).** State the two-thirds evaluation rule.
**A.** One-third of a review covers last year's lessons; two-thirds covers your aspirations for the next twelve months, what you need from the organization, and a timeline (3-6 months for most items). Never set a goal without a timeline. The output is a report card.

**Q6 (SA).** Walk through the eureka year.
**A.** In a tough year with cutbacks, Carla worked hard, said nothing about wanting the promotion and assumed it would come. It did not. Her boss said he had been wondering when she would come in; her peers had been pleading their cases for months and tough marginal choices had to be made. Lesson: at the margin, the person who voiced expectations wins the tie. She stopped being angry and began asking.

**Q7 (SA).** What is the listening turnaround?
**A.** A colleague said her boss hated giving her feedback because "she has an answer for everything." On a mentor's advice she responded in the next review only with "yes," "uh-huh," "oh really? okay." The boss found her open, feedback became specific, and the relationship moved from manager to adviser to sponsor; he began discounting false claims about her.

**Q8 (T/F).** Keeping your head down is the safest approach in a hostile environment.
**A.** False. "Keeping your head down doesn't keep you from getting shot; it only increases the chances that you won't see the bullet coming."

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** At month three of the year, an associate wants a promotion next year but has a gap in client experience. What should she do?
**A.** Ask for next year's promotion this year, early enough to close the gap; a yes confirms the track, a no gives the reason. Then run the every-two-months check-in ("You said A, B, C, D; A and B done, C and D by [date]; anything else? anything to make your job of sponsoring me easier?") and have the report card done by month six.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** In a meeting, a colleague falsely says "she doesn't know the convertible product," and another executive scowls at the manager. How should she respond?
**A.** Do not argue: assert, invite the test, then make sure she can pass it. Treat the scowl as an intimidation game rather than a verdict: find a supporter to look at, or stare back and keep talking. A reputation for speaking up deters people from trying to put one over on you. (Strategic silence only works once you have an established voice.)

## Chapter 7: Have a Penchant for Taking Risks

**Q1 (SA).** State the success equation.
**A.** Achieving Success and Moving Ahead = Performing Extremely Well + Aligning with Internal Politics + Taking Risks. Information and technology have leveled the knowledge landscape; willingness to take risk now differentiates.

**Q2 (SA).** Distinguish latent and forward risk.
**A.** Latent: out of your control and unconnected to performance (takeover, division sold, sponsor leaving abruptly, a role not matching your skills). Forward: chosen actions with uncertain outcomes that leave you better off regardless. Stack forward risks to build a platform from which latent shocks can be navigated.

**Q3 (MC).** Which risk type fits "the action is clear but not all reactions can be anticipated; upside must be markedly greater than downside"? (a) calculated; (b) studied; (c) step-out-on-faith; (d) not smart.
**A.** (b) studied. Calculated has predictable consequences (take as many as possible); step-out-on-faith has a known outcome but unknown path (use only when you alone bear the consequences).

**Q4 (SA).** What are the three "test the waters" questions, and the decision rule?
**A.** Will it give me skills/experience I would not get by staying 12 months? Will it expose me to people I otherwise would not reach? Will it create new branches on my decision tree? Three yeses means go. It says nothing about pay: a bigger job at the same pay is a platform.

**Q5 (SA).** Explain Carrie's calculated risk.
**A.** Two years past ready for SVP and unpromoted. Objective: make her boss and his boss understand she deserved and expected promotion that year. Action: meet the boss's boss first, then tell her own boss what she did and why. Consequences mapped in advance (boss angry or respectful; senior boss annoyed or impressed; prepared to search externally if nothing moved). Result: promoted.

**Q6 (SA).** Explain Carla's studied risk in the Internet IPO.
**A.** At the end of the first tech bubble, the book was oversubscribed but investor feedback was thin and conviction absent. Her gut said postpone; downside was an angry client, internal criticism for deferring revenue and a competitor stealing the mandate. She recommended waiting for the fall. Next day NASDAQ fell over 100 points and most Internet IPOs priced that day traded below issue. The client returned months later and priced successfully.

**Q7 (SA).** What is "the risk of saying no", with Carla's example?
**A.** Declining is also a risk. The year she was up for MD, her boss asked her to launch a new retail-distribution effort. She judged a long syndicate track record would carry promotion better than six to nine months in a new role, declined with her rationale ready, accepted the risk of an annoyed boss and a missed accelerator, and was promoted with his sponsorship intact.

**Q8 (T/F).** Compensated risk: if a rival firm offers more money but you fear its politics, decline.
**A.** False. You are being paid to absorb the political and cultural risk; usually worth taking. Likewise refusing a bigger job at the same pay forfeits the platform.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A junior employee wants to launch a no-precedent initiative; colleagues say she should ask permission first. What principle applies?
**A.** "Beg forgiveness, don't ask permission": asking permission for something with no precedent empowers the asked person to say no, and if you cross a line someone will tap you on the shoulder. Title never limits the risks you may take; self-imposed limits do. Ask "How do I make my role better? My job bigger? My platform bigger?"

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A manager considers a "step-out-on-faith" move in a large company with many interdependent players. Advice?
**A.** Avoid it there: too many permutations of reactions to model. Reserve faith risks for when she is the primary person affected and repercussions on others are very small. Prefer calculated risks, and separate genuine risk from "not smart" (acting without considering consequences you would be unprepared for). Also remember F-E-A-R is False Evidence Appearing Real, failure delivers experience, and nobody regrets a well-thought-out risk but almost everyone regrets one not taken.

## Chapter 8: Power in the Network

**Q1 (SA).** What are the four relationship types?
**A.** Upward (boss and above), lateral (peers inside and outside the firm plus the assistants of senior people), downward (juniors), external (nonprofit boards, other firms, entrepreneurs, headhunters, clergy, etc.).

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** Where does network power come from?
**A.** Relationship strength, how you manage it and the role you play (connector, information gatherer, helpful person), not the number of business cards. A network is a series of relationships connected to *you*; they need not connect to each other.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the maintenance cadence?
**A.** For the most important relationships: one face-to-face per year plus four voice-to-voice contacts. Everything else: e-mail updates once or twice a year that include what you are doing and your future goals.

**Q4 (SA).** What are the four roles of the lateral network?
**A.** Access to relationships, skills transfer, information transfer, and inspiration/camaraderie/affirmation.

**Q5 (SA).** What is the town-hall play?
**A.** Attend, sit in the front row, prepare a few precise, articulate questions, introduce yourself afterward, try to arrange a one-on-one, find the common thread (school, sport, hobby), and join the task forces and volunteer projects they support.

**Q6 (T/F).** An executive assistant is a minor figure in your network.
**A.** False. The assistant is the gatekeeper: controls access, knows the mood and best moment to approach, and can frame a request favorably. Rudeness to an assistant is a career derailer; build the relationship by being of service without expecting return. Everyone has power.

**Q7 (SA).** Retell the first-CD story and its lesson.
**A.** In 1999 Carla wanted to record a gospel album to raise money for two schools but knew nothing of licensing, producers or distribution. A musician-banker colleague mapped the steps and introduced one with a recording studio, who introduced a Broadway musical director; after an audition between shows he agreed to produce and sourced studio, engineer, mastering, manufacturer and musicians. A senior internal sponsor walked her to a music-label CEO, who connected her to the president of the independent distribution arm; press contacts landed a Sunday Business feature. Lesson: every link was a relationship she already had, leveraged one step at a time.

**Q8 (SCENARIO).** A new associate sits among former analysts who share a ready-made network and information flow. What did Carla do and what should the associate do?
**A.** Carla targeted two people (a classmate with ties into the group and a former analyst she knew), invested in lunches, dinners and drinks, asked directly for help accessing the information flow, and offered her external network in return; soon she had the same information as peers. Apply: ask peers for help (the start of a long-term relationship) and offer value first.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** An ambitious manager feels intimidated by a peer who is clearly ahead. What does Ch 8 say?
**A.** "Don't hate, gravitate": get closer to the one who intimidates you and learn from them. You compete with yourself; there is plenty of room at the top. Hiding failures from peers out of competitiveness forgoes solutions they have already found.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A busy executive says she has time only for work and family. What network risk does this create?
**A.** No time to build the external ring (boards, volunteer work). Treat external board and volunteer work as investment time and restructure the calendar. Also check for the anti-patterns of networking only with people who look like you and underusing the network (Harris observes women often do not ask for help or make themselves useful to their networks).

## Chapter 9: Balance Is a Necessity

**Q1 (SA).** How does Harris redefine balance?
**A.** Not work vs. family, but integrating your passions and giving back into your professional life. Without something outside the job that makes you feel alive, "your life becomes a function of somebody else's day."

**Q2 (SA).** What does "calendarize it" mean here?
**A.** Map a typical day, find where time leaks and capture it. Lunch is 5-10 hours a week; take two of those hours for what brings joy (museum, workout, singing lesson, noon lecture, lunch at your child's school).

**Q3 (SA).** What does "congregate like actions" mean?
**A.** Batch reading into an uninterrupted hour or two with calls held, and answer e-mail only in several fixed half-hour slots instead of all day. Harris says this recovers several hours a day; continuous e-mail is the single biggest time leak she has seen.

**Q4 (T/F).** Professional life, with a high enough title, will consistently fulfill you.
**A.** False. Professional life will never consistently fulfill you, no matter the title; you need people or activities you can rely on to feel good in your daily life.

**Q5 (EXPLAIN).** Why does giving back belong in the success equation?
**A.** It is a duty of gratitude and its own reward, but also refuels you, keeps you humble, reminds you why you work so hard and, unplanned, builds client relationships and visibility ("You never know who may be sitting in the chair next to you at that nonprofit board meeting"). Do not give *in order* to get.

**Q6 (SA).** What professional dividends did Carla's gospel recordings and Carnegie Hall concerts bring?
**A.** She directed all proceeds to two schools (one in Harlem, one in Jacksonville). After twelve years as a banker with no press mention, her first CD in 2000 brought a Sunday New York Times Business-section feature, and in 2006 a half-page Wall Street Journal story on her first Carnegie Hall concert. Colleagues congratulated her, client standing rose overnight, and clients thanked her for speaking about her faith.

**Q7 (MC).** "After a fourteen-hour day there's only time for family." Harris's response? (a) Agree; (b) Audit lunch and interruptions first; (c) Work more; (d) Quit.
**A.** (b). If you want something you find the time; "no time" is a scheduling problem.

**Q8 (SCENARIO).** A banker says she cannot sing in a choir because meetings never stop. Which tools apply?
**A.** Calendarize (take two lunch hours weekly), congregate like actions (batch e-mail into fixed slots, reading into held-call blocks). Colleagues may need to adjust to her unavailability windows. Ask "What is your passion? How can you get more of it into your day?"

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** An analyst keeps her church work and volunteer service secret from clients to seem "serious." Advice?
**A.** Community service is what clients are also doing with free time; talking about United Way, church or scouting work is a relationship bridge. After the Journal piece, clients Carla had never known shared her faith with her. Hiding them forfeits the bridge (the same principle as Ch 1).

**Q10 (SA).** How does Ch 9 link to other chapters?
**A.** Ch 1 (bring the whole self), Ch 8 (nonprofit boards are the external ring), Ch 10 (refueling sustains a winner's expectation through bad years) and Ch 2 (balance serves the agenda).

## Chapter 10: Expect to Win

**Q1 (EXPLAIN).** Explain "know vs. maybe vs. hope."
**A.** At eighteen Carla *knew* she would get into Harvard, HBS and a top Wall Street firm. After deals went badly and pay disappointed, her language slid to *maybe* and *hope*: "mediocre thoughts, mediocre results." The fix is telling yourself daily that you *know*; if you do not yet believe it, act "as if."

**Q2 (SA).** List the five principles of the winner's lens.
**A.** (1) Identify the best outcome early and devise a strategy; (2) fearlessness: either the goal is achieved or a valuable lesson is learned, both are wins; (3) assume everything attempted works out for your good; (4) interdependence: goals can't be reached alone; (5) acquire the skills to be the best in your area, the positive outlier, instead of relying on luck or politics.

**Q3 (SA).** What are the four sheets of the annual reflection?
**A.** Who have I been this year? Who do I want to be? What lessons have I learned? What do I want to accomplish next year? Include weaknesses; write in third person if stuck; use the lists to guide conduct.

**Q4 (T/F).** "Selectively motivated" people are winners.
**A.** False. Working hard and smart only when things go well is the trait winners lack.

**Q5 (EXPLAIN).** What is the lift-someone-up reset?
**A.** When low, ask "What can I do today for somebody else, to lift them up?" Make the call, send the e-mail, do the research. Posture and attitude follow, and power is reclaimed by acting on what is under your control.

**Q6 (SA).** Retell the Cleveland hotel-room story.
**A.** Mid-career, after bad deals and disappointing pay, Carla's self-talk was *maybe*. The night before her birthday on a business trip she ran the reflection she first did at thirteen (when she decided to stop being the girl who knew everyone's gossip and become a trustworthy friend). She wrote who she had been (good friend, trustworthy, accessible) and who she wanted to be professionally (known as a professional and expert at her craft). She realized she could choose her focus: money not made, or the agenda and a plan. She chose the plan and to "knock the cover off the ball."

**Q7 (SA).** What does the syndicate-desk habit show?
**A.** Often meeting a CEO and CFO only two hours before a road show she would run and price over ten days, she walked in expecting to build trust, "and in almost every case, it worked." Expectation changes body language, speech and mentality.

**Q8 (SCENARIO).** A senior analyst's work has become rote and effortless; she feels bored. What does Ch 10 say?
**A.** Complacency: time for a new challenge or place. Return to the agenda and ask whether she is getting the experiences, people and lifestyle she came for. Yes: focus on what she controls. No: adjust the agenda. Staying in a mastered role dulls relevance.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A manager starts each week afraid of making another mistake after a bad quarter. Advice?
**A.** That attracts the very experience feared. Apply the second principle (fearlessness: result or lesson) and the reflection; do not let past mistakes define the perception you expect. Decision-makers read doubt: if you think "maybe I'm good enough," they think "maybe we should choose someone else."

**Q10 (SA).** Explain "not wedded to specifics."
**A.** Promotions arrive on different timelines and opportunities look different from the script; winners expect the surprise to hold an advantage rather than ignoring wins that do not look as imagined. Also "change as guarantee": welcome it and create it.

## Chapter 11: My Most Precious Pearl

**Q1 (SA).** What is the eleventh pearl?
**A.** Knowing not only who you are but *whose* you are. For Harris it is her Christian faith ("I am a child of God"); generalized, you need something above the job (the notes list God, Buddha, Allah or the power of the Universe) for strength, guidance and comfort.

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** Why does Harris say faith is a "steady constant"?
**A.** Against market and political volatility, a belief that outcomes, even painful ones, will ultimately be in your favor supplies the energy, discipline and tenacity to forge ahead after major disappointment.

**Q3 (SA).** What is "lesson or blessing"?
**A.** Every career event is one or the other; both are valuable and both can be leveraged to keep moving forward and upward. "Consider it all good."

**Q4 (SA).** What is "give your best, then diagnose"?
**A.** Because she believes she receives the best each day, she must give her best. If she cannot (not challenged, not motivated, treated unfairly), she identifies the problem, applies her tools (intelligence, experience, faith, the pearls), finds the solution and hands over the remainder.

**Q5 (MC).** Which mapping is correct? (a) Philippians 4:13: no challenge at work is insurmountable; (b) Isaiah 54:17: always ask permission; (c) Hebrews 13:5: work alone; (d) Romans 8:28: expect bad outcomes.
**A.** (a). Others: Isaiah 54:17 means those who try to derail your agenda will not prevail; Hebrews 13:5, no battle need be fought alone; Romans 8:28, even bad-looking events work out for your benefit. Also Philippians 4:6-8 (don't worry), Isaiah 41:10 (no one to fear).

**Q6 (SA).** Retell the second-year associate story.
**A.** Working for an insecure manager neither able nor interested in training her, and worried about her job, she told a close friend's mother, who told her to read and pray two psalms daily (Psalm 91 and Psalm 4). They became mantra, armor, comfort and motor, and marked the start of using faith deliberately as a tool for success.

**Q7 (T/F).** Hard work guarantees the assignment, promotion or platform on your timeline.
**A.** False; that belief is what her early career dismantled. Some things (timing, other people's decisions, a sponsor leaving, illness) are beyond control.

**Q8 (SCENARIO).** A manager who has prepared perfectly is passed over because a power broker left overnight. How does Ch 11 reframe it?
**A.** Separate what preparation controls (hard work, product knowledge, politics) from what it does not (timing, others' decisions) and have a resource for the second category. Treat the disappointment as a lesson (or blessing) rather than proof she controlled nothing.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A colleague of a different or no faith asks if Ch 11 applies to him. Answer.
**A.** Yes, in generalized form: have something above and beyond the job to turn to, whatever you believe in. Harris herself has never made a professional judgment without calling on what she considers a higher wisdom; the transferable model is a consistent decision ritual for high-stakes, unclear calls.

**Q10 (EXPLAIN).** How does Ch 11 underwrite earlier chapters?
**A.** Ch 10's fearlessness and expectation of good are rooted here; "no person or situation I should fear" underwrites her risk-taking (Ch 7); sharing faith at work is Ch 1's authenticity at fullest disclosure; faith and service refuel her (Ch 9).

# FINAL EXAM: 20 Cumulative Questions

**F1 (SA).** What is Harris's premise about organizations, and what does it imply for strategy?
**A.** No organization is a true meritocracy because people evaluate people. Hard work and intelligence are necessary but not sufficient; add strategy, relationships, politics, tenacity and faith.

**F2 (SA).** Write the success equation and name which chapters supply each part.
**A.** Performing Extremely Well (the base, Ch 1-3 skills and fit) + Aligning with Internal Politics (unspoken rules Ch 3, perception Ch 4, sponsors Ch 5, network Ch 8, ecosystem Ch 2) + Taking Risks (Ch 7). Equation itself is stated in Ch 7.

**F3 (T/F).** Fit is the most objective of the can do / will do / fit trio.
**A.** False. Fit is the most subjective and most decisive (Ch 3).

**F4 (MC).** Which time frame is correct? (a) 30-day rule; (b) 90-day rule with a day-91 check-in; (c) 180-day rule; (d) one-year rule only.
**A.** (b).

**F5 (SA).** Give three numbers/thresholds from the notes for maintaining relationships.
**A.** Mentors 8-10 touches a year (Carla every six to eight weeks); key network contacts one face-to-face plus four calls; others one to two e-mails a year including goals. Also two sponsor commitments before joining.

**F6 (EXPLAIN).** Link the three adjectives (Ch 4), word-framing (Ch 6) and the sponsor (Ch 5).
**A.** The adjectives define the perception you want; word-framing is the verbal channel that installs them; the sponsor repeats them in the room where decisions are made without you. Feed your sponsor the adjectives and a package.

**F7 (SCENARIO).** A new VP-track associate wants to be seen as senior. Combine two chapters' tools.
**A.** Ch 4: behave upward, dress like the next level, pre-meeting with the boss; Ch 6: ask for next year's promotion this year with the two-thirds evaluation, check in every two months. Also Ch 3: ask the decision-maker what advancement requires and perform at the next level 6-12 months early.

**F8 (SA).** Name the five risk categories on the cheatsheet and rule for each.
**A.** Calculated (take as many as possible); studied (only if upside markedly exceeds downside); step-out-on-faith (only when you alone bear the consequences); "not smart" (don't); latent (cannot prevent, offset with forward risks).

**F9 (SA).** Which two stories show the cost of silence, and how do they differ?
**A.** Ch 1: Carla prepared hard but sat silent and stalled (and Peter, quiet inside); Ch 6: the eureka year, where saying nothing about the promotion cost her at the margin. The first is perception ("smart or stupid?"), the second is the failure to state expectations.

**F10 (T/F).** Mistakes should be apologized for repeatedly to show sincerity.
**A.** False. Own it once, state the lesson, ask for support, move on (Ch 2). Repeated apologies keep it alive.

**F11 (EXPLAIN).** Why can't you rely only on a mentor, and what extra does a sponsor add?
**A.** A mentor advises; a sponsor spends capital in the room. Long-term advancement without a sponsor is nearly impossible; you need both plus advisers on a diverse board of directors.

**F12 (SCENARIO).** You are offered a bigger job at the same pay in a division where you lack a sponsor. What do you do?
**A.** The cheatsheet says take the bigger same-pay job (a platform to argue for pay later), and it must pass the three test-the-waters questions (Ch 7). But secure two explicit sponsor commitments first since changing divisions leaves old sponsorship behind (Ch 5).

**F13 (SA).** What is "wrong building" and the prescribed fix?
**A.** Ladder against the wrong building: a fundamental mismatch between you and the firm's success model (Ch 1, Ch 4). Fix with a 12-24-month plan: harvest transferable skills, network toward the target, then jump (Anne).

**F14 (EXPLAIN).** How does the winner's lens (Ch 10) reuse earlier ideas?
**A.** Fearlessness = Ch 7's "failure is experience"; interdependence = Ch 5 and 8; mastery = Ch 3 and the go-to person; expectation of good is rooted in Ch 11.

**F15 (SCENARIO).** A team member has strong results but is never promoted; she is a workhorse under a political boss. Diagnosis and actions?
**A.** Ecosystem mismatch (Ch 2): valued but not rewarded. Align toward a workhorse boss or build a sponsor with a seat at the table (Ch 5), speak up (Ch 6), and cultivate relationships (Ch 8).

**F16 (SA).** Why is the assistant important, and how does this connect to "everyone has power"?
**A.** Assistant controls access and timing (Ch 8). Honor power in the assistant as much as in the CEO; relationships are reciprocal.

**F17 (MC).** Which is the best use of non-fans? (a) Avoid them; (b) coffee as equals to ask for strengths and one area to work on; (c) report them; (d) ignore.
**A.** (b) (Ch 4 audit; cheatsheet: asking alone changes their view).

**F18 (SCENARIO).** You just started a job in an up-or-out firm. Lay out your first 90 days using at least three chapters.
**A.** Ch 3: three deliverables, calendar, adopt group hours, invite yourself; Ch 4: pick three adjectives; Ch 5: ask who will carry your paper, identify advisers; Ch 8: map key players, assistant relationships; Ch 6: speak in every meeting; day 91: advertise points on the board and ask what advancement requires.

**F19 (EXPLAIN).** Why does Harris pair balance (Ch 9) with winning (Ch 10)?
**A.** Refueling through passions and service sustains the expectation through bad years; without it your mood is hostage to others' days. Authenticity also expands the career unplanned (press features, client bonds).

**F20 (SYNTHESIS).** If you could keep only three practices from the book, which and why, based on the notes' priorities?
**A.** Open answer, but strong answers include: (1) write the agenda (Ch 2) as the decision filter; (2) recruit a sponsor and say what you want (Ch 5, 6); (3) know whose/who you are and expect to win (Ch 1, 10, 11). Grade on whether reasons cite the notes (marginal decisions, "who is carrying my paper into the room?", know vs. maybe).
