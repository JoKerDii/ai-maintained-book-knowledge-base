# Range: Key Arguments, Stories & Evidence by Chapter
*David Epstein, Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World*

Each chapter has: **Argument** (the claim), **Stories & evidence** (what the book uses to support it), **Key numbers**, and **So what** (the lesson). Built from the full text of the chapter files in the `epstein-range` skill, so it reflects that summary of the book, not the book's own wording. Chapter numbering follows the skill (Introduction, Ch 1-12, Conclusion).

---

## The Big Arc in One Paragraph
The Introduction sets up the Roger vs. Tiger contrast: elite performers usually sample widely and specialize late, and the head-start story is an unrepresentative template. Ch 1 supplies the master distinction (kind vs. wicked learning environments) that tells you when narrow early drilling works, and Ch 2 explains how the modern world became wicked and why conceptual, transferable thinking pays. Ch 3-4 show breadth and desirable difficulties as the mechanism of durable, flexible learning. Ch 5 gives the tools for novel problems (distant analogies, the outside view). Ch 6-7 turn to careers: optimize match quality, quit when informed, and test possible selves rather than plan. Ch 8-9 show outsiders and broad-with-depth people innovating. Ch 10-11 show narrow expertise failing in forecasting and organizations, with foxes, incongruence, and sensemaking as remedies. Ch 12 and the Conclusion defend protected inefficiency and tell you not to feel behind.

## Introduction: Roger vs. Tiger
**Argument:** Eventual elites in most domains do not front-load narrow technical practice. They pass through a sampling period of varied experience and specialize late, and the pattern extends beyond sports to careers, invention, and science. The ten-thousand-hours story is a real but unrepresentative sample.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Tiger vs. Roger:** one boy handed a putter before he could talk, coached in media answers at three, called "the Chosen One"; the other played squash, skiing, wrestling, badminton, basketball, handball, and soccer, had "pully" rather than pushy parents, asked to be moved back to a younger training group, and was still ranked number one in his mid-thirties.
- **More common Roger path:** German World Cup winners who did not play more organized soccer than amateurs until age 22 or later; a two-year study where 11-year-olds who played more sports and nonorganized soccer improved more by 13; Ester Ledecká winning Winter Olympic gold in two sports; Lomachenko crediting four years of Ukrainian dance.
- **Deliberate practice** as defined in the source study has four conditions (explicit instructions, individual supervision, immediate feedback, repeated similar tasks), which many domains can't supply.
- **Tillman Foundation veterans** read their nonlinear LinkedIn profiles as failures despite inimitable leadership experience.
- **Hyperspecialization and silos:** oncologists specializing by single organ; each group taking its most reasonable local action can produce a catastrophic whole ("system of parallel trenches").
- **British Olympic officials** call late-recruited athletes "slow bakers."

**Key numbers:** a 50-year-old tech founder is nearly twice as likely as a 30-year-old to start a blockbuster company; the fastest-growing start-ups had founders averaging 45.

**So what:** Sample before you specialize and treat that period as integral. Judge a domain before importing its training model. Expect to look behind early. Be skeptical of advice from people whose interests the head-start story serves.

## Ch 1: The Cult of the Head Start
**Argument:** Whether experience produces expertise depends on the domain. Kind learning environments reward early narrow repetition; wicked ones punish it. Chess, golf, and the Polgar experiment are exceptions, and most of what we want to learn is closer to "Martian tennis."

**Stories & evidence**
- **Hogarth's typhoid diagnostician:** a hands-on tongue exam kept "correctly" predicting fever because he was spreading it. Experience reinforced the wrong lesson.
- **Kahneman-Klein accord (2009):** Klein's naturalistic decision making (firefighters, naval commanders, grandmasters) and Kahneman's heuristics and biases were both right, depending on domain.
- **The Polgars:** Laszlo Polgar chose chess because it is objective and easy to measure. Susan started at four with "pawn wars" and a hand-cut card catalog of 200,000 game sequences; Judit became the youngest grandmaster ever at fifteen.
- **Chunking (Chase and Simon, 1973):** National Geographic showed Susan a 28-piece midgame diagram from a truck; she reproduced it, but barely any of the random arrangement.
- **Deep Blue and centaurs:** Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997. In freestyle chess, amateurs with three ordinary computers beat supercomputers and grandmaster-plus-computer teams; in 2014 a team captained by Anson Williams, an engineer with no chess rating whose skill came from *Command & Conquer*, won.
- **Cognitive entrenchment (Erik Dane):** experienced bridge players adapted worse than nonexperts to altered play order; experienced accountants did worse than novices under a new tax law.
- **Schwartz's light-bulb puzzle:** students new to the puzzle found the rule behind all 70 solutions; of those paid to repeat one working solution, only one did.
- **Argyris's consultants:** brilliant at well-defined problems, brittle and defensive when the first solution failed (single-loop learning).
- **Career streams (Connolly):** successful transitioners "traveled on an eight-lane highway."

**Key numbers:** Nobel laureates are at least 22 times more likely than other scientists to be amateur actors, dancers, magicians, or other performers; Polgar's catalog held 200,000 sequences.

**So what:** Classify the environment first. In wicked domains treat your first familiar solution as a suspect and import an outside analogy as a circuit breaker. Expect codifiable pattern recognition to be automated; invest in strategy and integration.

## Ch 2: How the Wicked World Was Made
**Argument:** Modernity rewired thinking from concrete, experience-bound reasoning toward abstract classification, which is why range pays now. Yet education responds by pushing narrow specialization rather than teaching transferable conceptual tools.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Luria in Uzbek and Kyrgyz villages (1931):** collectivization created a natural experiment with untouched remote villages as a control. Remote villagers refused to sort wool by color ("None of them are the same"), grouped tools with the log, insisted the boy stay in a group of adults, refused syllogisms about white bears in the Far North ("only someone who was there"), and weren't fooled by the Ebbinghaus illusion. Modern-work exposure brought eduction and curiosity (nineteen-year-old Siddakh asking why the rich are rich).
- **Flynn effect:** IQ gains across the century largest on Raven's Progressive Matrices and abstract vocabulary; the trait tied to exposure to self-directed, nonrepetitive work was cognitive flexibility, across six industrializing nations.
- **Fermi-izing:** decomposing a problem into estimable pieces to reach the right order of magnitude; independent errors partly cancel.
- **Jeannette Wing's computational thinking** (abstraction and decomposition) as a candidate part of the conceptual Swiss Army knife.
- **Epstein's own statistical misstep:** he ran a standard test on a big database without understanding its limits, got an almost certain false positive, and earned a master's degree.
- **Flynn's critique of universities:** correlation between broad conceptual thinking and GPA about zero; on the causation question, students performed worse than random.

**Key numbers:** about 3 IQ points per decade, in more than 30 countries; three-quarters of American college graduates go into a career unrelated to their major.

**So what:** Acquire a small kit of broadly transferable reasoning tools rather than more facts in one field. Use Fermi-izing on numbers you can't check. Judge training by whether it transfers.

## Ch 3: When Less of the Same Is More
**Argument:** Even in classical music, the most golflike, blueprint-driven skill domain, exceptional performers spread effort across several instruments, started later, and took fewer early structured lessons. Breadth of training predicts breadth of transfer.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Figlie del coro of Venice's ospedali:** the Pietà raised abandoned infants (surrendered through the scaffetta). From them came Europe's most celebrated musicians; Anna Maria della Pietà was declared the premier violinist in Europe, Vivaldi spent four months' salary on a violin for her and wrote 28 concertos in her leather notebook, and Rousseau was shocked to see the disfigured performers behind a grille. About an hour a day of music early on, lessons three days a week, but every instrument the institution owned.
- **Sloboda's boarding-school study:** exceptional students came from less musically active families, did not start younger, took fewer lessons, practiced less, but spread effort across three instruments; those with heavy early structured lessons all fell into the "average" group. A follow-up two decades later at a conservatory: nearly all had played at least three instruments, more than half four or five.
- **Quitters:** in a study of 1,200 young musicians, mismatch between desired and played instrument was the pattern; Chua's daughter Lulu: "You picked it, not me" (quit at thirteen).
- **Improvisers:** Berliner on learning by osmosis; Suzuki's method modeled on language acquisition; Limb's MRI work showing self-criticism areas turn down during improvisation; Cecchini: jazz students "all sound the same" when never required to solve problems themselves, and it is easier for jazz musicians to learn classical than the reverse.
- **Low prior restraint (Adam Grant):** creative children's households averaged one rule versus six.
- **Versatility as insurance:** Pelegrina switched from oboe to violin when her teeth fell out; Django reinvented his fret hand after losing two fingers.

**Key numbers:** about three instruments; 1 rule vs. 6 rules; 1,200 young musicians.

**So what:** Spread early effort across about three tools before narrowing, let the learner choose the specialty from experience, delay heavy instruction, and cut rules rather than effort when you want creativity.

## Ch 4: Learning, Fast and Slow
**Argument:** Durable, flexible learning is produced by conditions that make short-term performance worse: struggle, delay, mixing, and error. Because we judge by before-your-eyes progress, we choose the methods that feel best and teach least.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Bjork's desirable difficulties:** generation, testing, spacing, and making-connections questions and interleaving; fluency "produces misleadingly high levels of immediate mastery."
- **Oberon and Macduff:** rhesus monkeys learning ordered image lists under four hint conditions did well in practice, then collapsed when hints were removed on day four (Macduff scored zero on lists practiced with automatic hints), while no-hint lists improved to nearly three-quarters (Oberon) and about half (Macduff).
- **Air Force Academy calculus:** instructors who most boosted their own students' exam scores and evaluations harmed performance in later courses.
- **Classroom hints:** about 20% of questions began as making-connections problems and 0% survived the interaction; the Japanese bansho tracks conceptual connections on the board.
- **Fadeout:** across 67 early-childhood programs, closed skills were taught instead of open skills.
- **Interleaving example:** shuffling Cezanne/Picasso/Renoir flashcards; Kornell's "make it easy to make it hard."
- **Learning hacks:** brain-training apps and supplements crowd out spacing, testing, and making-connections questions.

**Key numbers:** 20% / 0% of making-connections questions; 67 programs; four days of the monkey study.

**So what:** Never read current performance as learning; measure delayed, unaided performance. Force an attempt before supplying the answer, space repetitions further than feels right, interleave, and build evaluation systems that look at downstream outcomes.

## Ch 5: Thinking Outside Experience
**Argument:** For problems you have never seen, direct experience is a trap. The tool that works is a volume of distant analogies from unrelated domains, which surfaces deep structure and forces the outside view. Intuition does the opposite: it grabs one surface-similar case and stays inside the details.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Kepler:** after five years he discarded his Mars model over eight minutes of arc in Brahe's data, then reasoned by analogy (smell, heat, light, magnets, a whirlpool, boatmen) to a theory of bodies pulling one another and the moon's influence on tides.
- **Dunbar's labs:** the most productive labs offered a new analogy every four minutes from varied base domains; the E. coli-only lab produced no new findings.
- **Gentner's studies:** business students given a single same-industry example preferred it, but those given Nike and McDonald's generated more strategic options than those given Apple and Dell; the movie-forecasting study showed a single most analogous film collapsed predictive power; Ambiguous Sorting Task of 25 cards sorted by deep structure.
- **Priming:** Stanford students primed with WWII chose war, with Vietnam chose diplomacy, on an identical scenario.
- **Outside view:** Kahneman and Tversky; the Edinburgh tram costed at £320 million from the inside and headed toward £1 billion; Netflix predicts by analogizing you to similar customers.
- **Radiation problem:** most solvers summoned more medical knowledge rather than reaching outside the domain.
- **BCG intranet** of exhibits sorted by discipline, concept, and strategic theme.

**Key numbers:** about 90% of major infrastructure projects go over budget, by 28% on average; eight minutes of arc; a new analogy every four minutes; 25 cards.

**So what:** Generate many analogies, including unrelated ones, before solutions. Distrust the first analogy. Build the reference class and estimate that first. Staff teams for variety of base domains.

## Ch 6: The Trouble with Too Much Grit
**Argument:** Persistence toward a goal chosen before you knew yourself is not a virtue. Finding a goal with high match quality is the harder, more valuable problem, and quitting is the mechanism that finds it. Grit predicts well only in narrow, preselected, goal-given contexts, which is where most evidence comes from.

**Stories & evidence**
- **West Point:** the Grit Scale beat the Whole Candidate Score at predicting Beast dropouts, but from a filtered population (restriction of range). Since the mid-1990s about half of graduates leave active service at the first legal opportunity and three-quarters before the pension mark, the highest attrition of any officer route; retention runs inverse to investment (OCS longest, then unfunded ROTC, then scholarships, West Point last).
- **Army's retention fixes:** a $500 million bonus failed (taken by those already staying, refused by those leaving); the Officer Career Satisfaction Program, trading extra service years for choice of branch or post, got 4,000 cadets to extend in four years.
- **Van Gogh:** dealer, teacher, bookseller, would-be pastor, itinerant preacher; 40th percentile on the Grit Scale when his biographer filled it out.
- **Multi-armed bandit (Robert Miller):** sample high-information, fast-feedback paths early.
- **Duckworth's caution** about "high-stakes character assessment" and preselected samples limiting external validity.
- **Churchill's maxim** continues "except to convictions of honor and good sense."
- **Teachers who switch schools** become more effective (Kirabo Jackson); Scottish students forced to study broadly for two years switched careers less and caught up in income quickly.
- **Sunk costs:** conmen ask for several small investments first.

**Key numbers:** 71 of 1,218 plebes dropped out; about half leave at five years; three-quarters before twenty; $500 million; 4,000 cadets; 85% of workers globally not engaged or actively disengaged.

**So what:** Separate "am I persisting enough?" from "am I in the right thing?" Write quit conditions in advance. Prefer early paths with fast, unforgiving feedback. Leaders should build matching mechanisms rather than pay retention bonuses.

## Ch 7: Flirting with Your Possible Selves
**Argument:** You can't introspect your way to the right career because the person you're planning for doesn't exist yet. Match quality is discovered by doing. Treat identity as a hypothesis to test with short experiments, then revise.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Frances Hesselbein:** four professional positions, none applied for. She led Girl Scout Troop 17 for six weeks (stayed eight years), chaired the United Way, took her first professional job at 54 for six months (stayed four years), and at 60 went to a national CEO interview only to decline. As CEO for thirteen years: minority membership tripled, a quarter million members added, cookie business past $300 million, called the best CEO in the country by Drucker. "I was unaware that I was being prepared."
- **Dark Horse Project (Rose and Ogas):** 45 of the first 50 apologized for a path that was actually the norm; dark horses ask what best matches now.
- **End of history illusion (Gilbert):** people know they've changed but expect not to.
- **Ibarra:** "Test-and-learn, not plan-and-implement"; quizzes and strengths-finders "give people license to pigeonhole themselves."
- **Paul Graham:** work forward from promising options; "premature optimization."
- **Context principle:** traits are if-then; mismatches are often context problems.

**Key numbers:** 45 of 50; Hesselbein's 6 weeks to 8 years, 6 months to 4 years, 13 years as CEO; quarter million members; $300 million.

**So what:** Ask which possible self to explore now, run cheap reversible experiments, say yes to filler roles, and reflect and rewrite your story after each. Change the context before changing your self-assessment.

## Ch 8: The Outsider Advantage
**Argument:** The further a problem sits from a solver's expertise, the more likely they are to solve it, because decisive knowledge is usually not on anyone's resume. Accelerating specialization increases rather than shrinks the outsider's opportunity.

**Stories & evidence**
- **InnoCentive (Bingham, Lakhani):** seekers post challenges that stumped specialists; just over a third were completely solved, and the more diverse the solvers, the more likely a solution. "The further the problem was from the solver's expertise, the more likely they were to solve it."
- **Eli Lilly's confidentiality reflex** vs. Bingham's low-risk counter.
- **Swanson and Arrowsmith:** eleven neglected connections between magnesium deficiency and migraine literatures; by 2012 the American Headache Society and American Academy of Neurology said magnesium should be considered a common migraine treatment, on evidence as strong as ibuprofen's.
- **Jill Viles:** a substitute teacher with muscular dystrophy matched a *Muscle and Nerve* paper to her father's "Popeye arm" and her own posture; a Des Moines neurologist refused to look; she insisted on cardiac monitoring (her father's pulse dropped into the twenties; emergency pacemaker); the LMNA mutation was confirmed in 1999 by an Italian group. Recognizing missing fat in Olympic hurdler Priscilla Lopes-Schliep, she theorized the same partial lipodystrophy, confirmed by Dr. Abhimanyu Garg; Priscilla had three times normal blood fat. Cold-contacting Etienne Lefai changed his team's path.
- **Distant analogies:** a slushy, chewing gum on a leg, tear gas; NASA's initial resistance to Cragin's answer "because it was using a different methodology."
- **Domingos:** knowledge is a double-edged sword.

**Key numbers:** just over one-third of challenges solved; eleven connections; 1999 confirmation; three times normal blood fat.

**So what:** When specialists are stuck, change the solver population, not the technique. Frame problems for a wide range of professions, look across literatures that don't cite each other, and take outsiders seriously when the method is unfamiliar.

## Ch 9: Lateral Thinking with Withered Technology
**Argument:** You needn't work at the frontier to invent. Deliberately retreat from it and find new uses for cheap, well-understood, mature technology. Breadth beats depth where uncertainty is high, and the broad-with-one-deep-area profile beats both.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Gunpei Yokoi at Nintendo (joined 1965):** the Drive Game flopped as too advanced and fragile; then the Love Tester (a transistor and a store-bought galvanometer), the single-channel Lefty RX at a tenth of the cost, Game & Watch (commoditized LCDs; dots embossed with tweaked hanafuda printing machines), and the Game Boy (1970s-era processor, four grayscale shades, rivals from Sega and Atari). Told a competitor had a color screen: "Then we're fine."
- **Virtual Boy:** his greatest failure, when he abandoned his own tenets for experimental technology.
- **Ouderkirk at 3M:** specialists, generalists, and polymaths; polymaths most likely to succeed and win the Carlton Award. Consensus that something is impossible means being first is likelier.
- **Dyson's birds and frogs;** Jayshree Seth's T-shaped people consulting I-shaped people.
- **Abbie Griffin's serial innovators** (tolerance for ambiguity, systems thinkers, peripheral knowledge, repurposers, many hobbies) said they would not be hired under current practices.
- **Gruber's network of enterprise** (Darwin); the candle problem and functional fixedness.
- **Melero and Palomeras:** in high domain uncertainty, high-breadth team members mattered most. **Comics study:** repetition hurt, experience had no effect, publisher resources didn't help.

**Key numbers:** Game & Watch sold 43.4 million units; Game Boy sold 118.7 million (bestselling console of the twentieth century); Lefty RX at one-tenth the cost; Game Boy launched in 1989.

**So what:** Ask what cheap, universally understood technology could serve an unconsidered purpose. Compete on the barrier users actually face (complexity, price), build a polymath profile, staff for breadth as uncertainty rises, and welcome unqualified ideas.

## Ch 10: Fooled by Expertise
**Argument:** In wicked domains, narrow expertise makes forecasts worse: specialists bend events to one big theory and get more dogmatic with more information. Accuracy comes from integrators who gather many perspectives, treat beliefs as hypotheses, and update hard on surprise.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Ehrlich-Simon bet:** Ehrlich bet five metals would rise; in 1990 Simon collected $576.07. Both claimed vindication; Ehrlich would have won 62% of ten-year windows since 1900; commodity prices were a bad proxy.
- **Tetlock's twenty-year study:** 82,361 forecasts; the average expert did terribly, "impossible" events happened 15% of the time, and "sure things" failed over a quarter of the time. Integrators who held Gorbachev was a genuine reformer and that the USSR had lost legitimacy outside Russia were the only ones to see the collapse coming.
- **Good Judgment Project:** ordinary volunteers beat intelligence analysts with classified access; an hour of foxy training improved accuracy; superteams became about 50% more accurate individually and beat crowd wisdom and prediction markets.
- **Baron's active open-mindedness;** two-thirds of one study's participants declined payment rather than read counterarguments; Kahan's science curiosity vs. science knowledge.
- **Scott Eastman and Ellen Cousins:** deep-study areas were the weakest; take facts, not conclusions, from experts.
- **Anti-patterns:** claiming total victories and near-miss defeats; updating in the wrong direction after wild misses.

**Key numbers:** $576.07; 62%; 82,361 forecasts; 15%; over 25%; about 50% accuracy gain for superteam members.

**So what:** Ask whether you are reasoning from one big idea or many, build the reference class first, score yourself especially on misses, recruit for range and curiosity, and practice "extremely polite antagonism." If you can't say what would change your mind, you're a hedgehog on that topic.

## Ch 11: Learning to Drop Your Familiar Tools
**Argument:** Under pressure in unfamiliar situations, expert groups regress to what they know best and cling to tools that define their identity, even fatally. The organizational antidote is incongruence, a deliberately mixed message between formal process and informal individualism, not a stronger consistent culture.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Carter Racing / Challenger:** a case with seven engine failures in 24 races, decision at 40 degrees F; the lesson taught for thirty years is "incomplete data." Epstein's twist: 17 flights had no O-ring exposure, so carried no information; of seven exposure flights, five showed erosion and two showed blow-by. Allan McDonald: looking only at relevant data supported NASA's launch position. Boisjoly's real evidence was qualitative (gray soot streak at 75 degrees, jet-black at 53); asked to quantify, he could only say "away from goodness." NASA's strong quantitative culture made it inadmissible; others stayed quiet without numbers.
- **Weick's tools:** Quentin Rhoades and his saw; the firefighter Paul Gleason's sensemaking; "can do" vs. "make do."
- **Patil, Tetlock, Mellers:** incongruence experiments across 334 institutions (congruence had no measurable effect on success); each injection turned non-learners into learning machines.
- **Captain Tony Lesmes at Bagram:** dropped going-with-the-team, floated it as a hypothesis, got seven wounded out in one trip.
- **Geveden's rule;** von Braun's Monday Notes, killed when Lucas made them upward-only forms; Kranz's twice-heard hunch.
- **Oculostenotic reflex;** Toynbee: "no tool is omnicompetent."

**Key numbers:** 7 of 24 races with failures; 17 of 24 data points irrelevant; 99.4% failure probability from the case's regression at 40 degrees; seven soldiers; 334 institutions.

**So what:** Ask whether this is the data you need for the decision. Count relevant data points. Build an admissibility path for qualitative evidence, push against your dominant culture, separate chain of command from chain of communication, and state plans as hunches held lightly.

## Ch 12: Deliberate Amateurs
**Argument:** Breakthrough discovery depends on protected inefficiency: time to graze shallow, wander outside your field, and pursue curiosities with no defensible application. Systems that train and fund specialists optimize that time away, and the discovery rate pays for it.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Oliver Smithies (Saturday, 23 January 1954):** stuck on insulin and moist paper, he remembered starch congealing on his father's shirt collars from age twelve, made a starch gel, and invented gel electrophoresis ("Very promising!"). He never found the insulin precursor, switched from medicine to chemistry, took a sabbatical in his fifties, published his most important paper around sixty, and won the Nobel as a geneticist. Colleagues' label for broken equipment: NBGBOKFO.
- **Andre Geim:** Friday night experiments; frog levitation (an Ig Nobel) to gecko tape to graphene and a Nobel; "I do not dig deep, I graze shallow"; Novoselov looked like he was wasting his life.
- **Tu Youyou:** "professor of the three no's"; a fourth-century Chinese wormwood recipe for artemisinin.
- **Uzzi's 18 million papers:** hits combine mostly conventional work with one atypical combination; novel combinations are one in ten papers and likelier to be top-1% cited fifteen years on.
- **Stagnant teams:** 1920s Broadway had a 90% flop rate with repeat collaborators.
- **Casadevall:** parallel trenches, the guild system, R3 Initiative, and a black-mold project sparked by a Chernobyl robot news story. **Bill Gore's** dabble time.
- **HIV:** retrovirus knowledge was on the shelf, but still fifteen years from 1981 to effective therapy.

**Key numbers:** 18 million papers; 1 in 10 novel combinations; 90% Broadway flops; 15 years to HIV therapy; Smithies' key Saturday in 1954; across 10,000 researchers no standard experience-to-contribution relationship.

**So what:** Schedule a recurring unjustified exploration block, read outside your field daily, take your skills to a new problem rather than becoming a clone of your adviser, and pick ecosystems with porous boundaries. Don't read a late start or "spread thin" as being behind.

## Conclusion: Expanding Your Range
**Argument:** There is no tidy prescription for breadth; experimentation is inefficient, high-variance, and disorderly. Epstein's one sentence of advice is: don't feel behind.

**Stories & evidence**
- **Howard Scott Warshaw:** *Yar's Revenge* (bestselling original 2600 title) and *E.T.* (pronounced the biggest commercial failure in video game history, blamed for Atari's collapse) in the same year, same method.
- **Simonton:** the more work eminent creators produced, the more duds and the higher the chance of a supernova success. Edison's thousand-plus patents, Shakespeare's *Timon of Athens* between *King Lear* and *Macbeth*, and Rachel Whiteread winning both the Turner Prize and the "Anti-Turner Prize."
- **Michael Simmons:** truncated (baseball) vs. untruncated outcome distributions. **Bingham:** breakthrough and fallacy look alike at first.
- **Late starts:** Richter began formal piano lessons at 22; Nash got a basketball at 13 and won two NBA MVPs. Caesar wept at a statue of Alexander, then peaked early and was murdered.
- **Michelangelo and marble;** Ibarra's "plan experiments, not plans."

**Key numbers:** in baseball the best swing scores four, elsewhere an at-bat can score a thousand; 1,000+ Edison patents; 2 NBA MVPs; Richter at 22; Nash at 13.

**So what:** Stop benchmarking against others' ages, convert anxiety into scheduled experiments, budget for duds, treat direction changes as information, and judge a path by its distribution of outcomes. Specialization isn't the enemy; premature specialization sold as a shortcut is.
