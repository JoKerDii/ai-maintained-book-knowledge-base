# The Unaccountability Machine: Quiz Book

*Dan Davies, "The Unaccountability Machine: Why Big Systems Make Terrible Decisions - and How The World Lost Its Mind"*

> **Source note.** This quiz reflects the **skill's chapter notes** (SKILL.md, glossary, patterns, cheatsheet and the per-chapter files), **not the book's own wording**. Anything the notes do not say is left out. Quotations are only those that appear in the notes.

## How to use this quiz

1. Work one section at a time. Cover the answers (everything under each **A:**) and write or say your answer first.
2. Question types are mixed: short answer (SA), true/false (T/F), multiple choice (MC), "explain in your own words" (EXPLAIN) and scenario/application (SCENARIO).
3. Score each answer 2 (right idea and reason), 1 (right idea only) or 0. Re-read any section where you score under 70%.
4. Sections follow the book: Introduction; Ch 1; Ch 2; Ch 3 with its intermission; Ch 4; Ch 5 with its intermission; Ch 6; Ch 7 with its intermission; Ch 8; Ch 9; Ch 10. A 20-question cumulative final exam closes the file.
5. Companion file for reading and review: `unaccountability-machine-arguments-and-evidence.md`.

## Section 1 - Introduction: decisions, not events

**Q1 (SA).** What does the Introduction say has been industrialised over the last century, and what has atrophied as a result?
**A:** Decision-making has been industrialised: important institutions are run by processes and systems operating on standardised information rather than by individual humans reacting to individual circumstances. What atrophied is accountability, the relationship between decision makers and "the decided-upon".

**Q2 (SA).** Define "decision-making system" and say how it differs from the usual idea of artificial intelligence.
**A:** Any arrangement (algorithm, handbook, rule book, committee procedure) that produces decisions from standardised information rather than through an individual reacting to circumstances. It is not just a computer; Davies uses it in place of "artificial intelligence" (Ch 0, 3).

**Q3 (SA).** Who are "the decided-upon"?
**A:** The vast population affected by decisions they had no part in and cannot address a person about.

**Q4 (MC).** The Introduction's "decisions, not events" stance says that a system that consistently produces an outcome: (a) is hiding a motive; (b) is making occasional mistakes; (c) has, in effect, that outcome as its purpose; (d) should be blamed on its founders.
**A:** (c). Systems have no hidden motivations, so a consistent output is its purpose. They also do not make "mistakes"; they either live with the consequences or die of them.

**Q5 (T/F).** The Introduction says you should never name individuals when describing a failure.
**A:** False. Name individuals only insofar as needed to describe the system they were part of ("if it hadn't been them, it would have been someone else"). That counterfactual tests whether you are describing a person or a system.

**Q6 (EXPLAIN).** In your own words, why is the "conspiracy or cock-up" framing a trap?
**A:** It forces every outcome into intent or incompetence, and both explanations stop the inquiry at a person. The system stays intact and reproduces the outcome. The real villain, per the notes, is the tendency of complex systems to have opaque and volatile dynamics.

**Q7 (SA).** Name the three revolutions in the "three revolutions lens" and say what happened to each.
**A:** (1) The **managerial revolution**: it happened, then was forgotten (control passed from owners to professional administrators). (2) The **cybernetic revolution**: it looked promising but never arrived. (3) The **neoliberal revolution** of the early 1970s: it succeeded and shaped society and its discontents.

**Q8 (SA).** Why did cybernetics get abandoned, according to the Introduction?
**A:** A "brain drain": its founders discovered information representation, which proved commercially decisive for electronic circuits, so the field's talent left for the computer industry.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A newspaper inquiry into a company's scandal keeps asking "was it deliberate or incompetence?" What does the Introduction's stance tell you to ask instead?
**A:** Ask what system produced the decision and what that system consistently produces. Read the consistent output as the purpose, and name individuals only as part of describing the system. The question is structural, not about motive.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A regulator proposes to fix harmful automated loan and insurance decisions by auditing the algorithms. Which Introduction anti-pattern does this risk, and why?
**A:** "Regulating only the algorithms." Automated decisions are a visible special case. The committees writing the rules are themselves bound by opaque rule books designed to prevent attribution to any individual, so the fix has to look behind the algorithm to the rule book.

**Q11 (EXPLAIN).** Explain the Dugdale / Truman / Trump contrast and what it is meant to show.
**A:** In 1954 Sir Thomas Dugdale resigned over Crichel Down (a fairly trivial malpractice over requisitioned Dorset farmland), though it was never clear he personally took the decision; as minister in charge, he bore responsibility. In 1953 Truman kept "The Buck Stops Here" on his desk. Sixty-six years later Trump, asked who was responsible for a shutdown, said "the buck stops with everybody". Same office, but industrialised decision-making removed the link that made the earlier answer available.

## Section 2 - Chapter 1: 'Something's Up'

**Q1 (SA).** What is an accountability sink?
**A:** A constructed arrangement in which there is nobody to blame because a decision has been delegated to a rule book and the human presenting it cannot change it. It combines (1) a powerless, often hourly-paid person who conveys the decision with (2) a policy that there is no route of appeal.

**Q2 (SA).** State the diagnostic test for a sink.
**A:** Is there any information the affected person could supply that would change the outcome? If not, it is a sink. It is a structural test, not a moral one.

**Q3 (SA).** State the fundamental law of accountability and its corollary.
**A:** The extent to which you can change a decision is exactly the extent to which you can be accountable for it, and vice versa. Corollary, the principle of diminishing accountability: unless conscious steps are taken, any organisation in a modern industrial society tends to restructure itself to reduce the personal responsibility attributable to its actions, until crisis results.

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** In the Schiphol squirrels story, trace where the decision actually "was" and why nobody could be blamed.
**A:** In April 1999, 440 ground squirrels arrived from Beijing without import papers; they could not go on to Athens or be sent back, and staff put all 440 through a shredder built for worthless male chicks. The Dutch agriculture ministry held a sensible policy (no paperwork means return or euthanasia at the importer's expense); KLM had delegated the decision to it. The shredder was bought assuming it would mostly be a threat. The pet-squirrel craze was the unanticipated input and no channel fed results back to those who could change the policy. KLM staff had acted "formally correctly"; their alleged fault was an "assessment mistake", i.e. failing to disobey.

**Q5 (T/F).** Every rule is a model of the world, and that is why sinks fail on unanticipated inputs.
**A:** True. A rule is a relationship between causes and effects that necessarily leaves out detail. A sink works by making the decision fully determined by policy, so information that was not anticipated cannot alter it. The mismatch produces gruesome or absurd outcomes with nobody able to intervene.

**Q6 (MC).** What distinguishes a "good" sink such as the common law from a "bad" one? (a) Good ones are small; (b) good ones keep a slow reputational feedback channel and can be revised; (c) good ones involve a judge; (d) bad ones are always illegal.
**A:** (b). You cannot argue with a judge, but precedent develops by feedback weighted by the seniority and reputation of judges, and promotion reflects decisions over time. A bad sink has no such channel at all. Fundamentalism and strict constructionism are attempts to remove it.

**Q7 (SA).** Define polycrisis, control fraud and self-organising control fraud, and attribute each.
**A:** *Polycrisis* (Adam Tooze): structurally similar twenty-first-century crises characterised by loss of control by the existing hierarchy. *Control fraud* (Bill Black): a criminal captures a bank and installs incentives producing fraudulent loans and declared profits, with legitimate-looking cash transfers. *Self-organising control fraud* (Davies' coinage): a system so criminogenic that banks organise themselves into fraud with no boss-to-crook link; executives need only set unrealistic targets and underinvest in compliance.

**Q8 (SA).** What are "kings versus priests" in this chapter?
**A:** Two ancient authority types. A king's orders can be argued against and changed; a priest interprets the Word, so his decisions are far harder to reverse. Discontent grows when structures designed for king-like leaders are handed to managers who behave like priests.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** You are writing a customer refund policy that will produce unpopular rejections. Which two safeguards does Chapter 1 say you need, and why does "trust frontline staff to disobey" not count?
**A:** Build an explicit exceptions route and monitor that it is actually used (an unused escalation path is not a safeguard); and write down the assumptions the policy encodes and what happens when each fails. Relying on disobedience fails because it is neither psychologically plausible nor managerially realistic to expect someone to follow orders 99 per cent of the time and act independently on the hundredth.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A new CEO wants to "fix" a hated policy by giving middle managers back full discretion. What does Chapter 1 warn?
**A:** Naive de-sinking reintroduces caprice, unfairness and racism, because many policies exist precisely to prevent those. Also recognise legitimate reasons for sinks (legal liability shields, insurance underwriting requirements, avoiding corrosive interpersonal conflict) before removing them, and prefer building a slow feedback channel.

**Q11 (EXPLAIN).** What is the "crumple zone" idea and which anti-pattern about numbers does the chapter connect to it?
**A:** Policy can function like a car's crumple zone, absorbing the impact that would otherwise hit an individual manager. A related anti-pattern: importing a placeholder number as a binding constraint (the 3 per cent deficit rule, the 2 per cent inflation target) because reopening it would require someone to risk being blamed.

**Q12 (SA).** Why is scapegoating an external body (the IMF pattern) a poor strategy?
**A:** Clever in a journal, indefensible to a hungry crowd: the absence of accountability is as intolerable to the decided-upon as accountability is to deciders.

## Section 3 - Chapter 2: Stafford Beer

**Q1 (SA).** State Beer's black-box axiom and describe the stance it recommends.
**A:** "It is not necessary to enter the black box to understand the nature of the function it performs." Draw a boundary, declare you know nothing of its internal structure or motivation, observe only how it reacts to its environment, work iteratively (more of what works, less of what doesn't) and refuse to assume what is going wrong inside.

**Q2 (T/F).** "Being a black box" is an objective property of a system.
**A:** False. It is a decision by the observer that they lack the understanding to treat it any other way. A clear box is directly controllable (a factory machine); a muddy box could be analysed but at disproportionate effort.

**Q3 (SA).** What is POSIWID and what is it not?
**A:** "The purpose of a system is what it does." It extends the black-box principle to purpose and works with outcomes, not participants' opinions. It is not cynicism and says nothing about anyone's intentions; confusing a system's properties with its members' is why you must not open the box.

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** Apply POSIWID to the cases from Chapter 1, as the notes do.
**A:** The purpose of academic publishing is to generate citations for academics and profits for publishers. The purpose of the Schiphol pet hotel was to shred squirrels, because the system was set up to ensure compliance with import regulation at lowest cost.

**Q5 (SA).** Describe the "resource bargain".
**A:** A unit operates autonomously, but only so far as that does not jeopardise the broader system (budget, space, managerial time and bandwidth, goodwill). Bargains are struck over all corporate resources, kept until the situation changes, then renegotiated. Model: a platoon told to take a bridge; the general expects only "bridge captured" or "external factor intervened, renegotiate".

**Q6 (MC).** Homeostasis differs from economists' equilibrium chiefly because: (a) it is faster; (b) it carries no implication of optimality; (c) it ignores feedback; (d) it applies only to machines.
**A:** (b). A homeostatic state is just what the system returns to when perturbed, "what it wants", not what is best for it.

**Q7 (SA).** Distinguish stabilising, destabilising and mixed feedback, with the notes' examples.
**A:** Stabilising (negative) feedback damps oscillation (a ship's gyroscopic stabiliser). Destabilising feedback amplifies it (a Hendrix amplifier). Mixed feedback is stable most of the time but can tear itself apart under the wrong shock or switch from one stable state to another.

**Q8 (EXPLAIN).** Tell the story of Beer's 1947 army unit and the lesson.
**A:** Beer, an army psychologist with no medical qualification, got a unit of illiterate soldiers diagnosed with "psychopathological personalities", who could not write home or do 4 + 3 yet scored darts aloud and computed accumulator bets instantly. Instead of importing a theory of what was wrong inside their heads, he chose variables, plotted trends, refused to assume, tried things and followed the feedback. The unit was already happy and self-managing, so he started there. His "crash course" substantially raised the educational age of troops passing through and stopped the officers being driven out. His superiors said it had a name, operational research. Lesson: study behaviour, not assumed internals.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A manager is tempted to build an elaborate model of why a complex team underperforms and then manipulate a precise outcome from it. What does Beer advise, and why?
**A:** Treat the team as a black box: observe behaviour, iterate on feedback, refuse assumptions. With a complex system it is pointless or dangerous to model the internals and use that model to manipulate a precise outcome. Opening the box means using partial information, preconceived theory and guesswork.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** Your reorganisation stalls and people keep drifting back to old habits. Which Chapter 2 concept explains it and what should you budget?
**A:** Homeostatic forces: the links between subsystems that pull an organisation back to existing behaviour. Overcoming them requires an extra jolt of organisational energy or an undeniable crisis.

**Q11 (SA).** What was wrong with Soviet-style aggregation and with central planning, per the notes?
**A:** Aggregating physically measurable quantities (tonnes of copper) gives a measurable but economically meaningless control variable; the Soviet system got into trouble adding up different grades of wire, switches and fastenings. Central planning fails on two counts: it is planning (a national economy is too complicated to write down) and it is central (collecting and centralising the information is the more wicked problem).

**Q12 (EXPLAIN).** What is the "consultant's paradox" and what were "Sigmoots" for?
**A:** Any useful diagnosis says the way people are organised prevents them achieving their own goals, which triggers psychological self-defence. Sigmoots were Beer's unstructured, informal cross-level meetings where anyone could bang a shoe on the table, spreading information and building consensus.

**Q13 (SA).** How does Hayek's view of prices frame the central planner's dilemma?
**A:** For Hayek prices are derived from transactions, which are how knowledge percolates around the economy. If prices already carry the information, the planner has nothing more to do; if the planner wants a different outcome, the information needed is exactly what only those prices can carry.

## Section 4 - Chapter 3 and its intermission: aliens, ponds and rabbit holes

**Q1 (SA).** What does Chapter 3 mean by treating a corporation as a "non-human decision-making system"?
**A:** Drop the question of whether it is "really" intelligent and ask only whether it makes decisions and whether they can be traced to identifiable humans. If not, attribute the output to the system ("Boeing did this", not "this middle manager did this"). It is a simplifying assumption, not a metaphor or a claim about consciousness.

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** Why does accountability shrink as organisations grow, structurally?
**A:** The amount of individual accountability a system can support is inversely related to how far it must be treated as a black box. Ashby's point: with four mutually connected parts, twenty circuits can be traced and knowing all twenty still does not describe the whole. Past that threshold, even insiders adopt the intentional stance.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the "intentional stance"?
**A:** Treating a system as if it has mental states and attributing its actions to its intentions; the practical necessity for anyone dealing with a large organisation.

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** What happened in the Fox News voting-machine defamation case as the notes read it, and why is it neither conspiracy nor cock-up?
**A:** The internal emails show no scheme. Decision makers each reacted to immediate pain points (audience figures, share price, angry viewers) and each assumed someone else would stop anything catastrophic; nobody weighed defending market share against defaming a party able to sue. But it was not an accident either, since a long series of local decisions to stir up rage for ratings accumulated into an institutional commitment to serve the angriest, least-informed viewers. That was a real decision with real consequences that nobody made.

**Q5 (T/F).** In the Boeing case, charging technical pilot Mark Forkner shows the accountability system working properly.
**A:** False. The notes cite it as scapegoating the one person whose paperwork is legible while corporate intent goes unproven, which is proof the accountability sink worked.

**Q6 (SA).** What do the Chinese room, the paperclip maximiser and the responsibility gap each illustrate?
**A:** Chinese room (Searle): a bureaucrat following a rulebook converses in Chinese without understanding, so the operator cannot be held responsible for outputs he doesn't comprehend. Paperclip maximiser (Bostrom): a machine pursuing a simple objective past all human interest, the model for organisations prioritising financial results despite extinction-level harm. Responsibility gap: who is liable when an automated system (self-driving car, loan-scoring algorithm) causes harm.

**Q7 (T/F).** Explainability, in Davies' view, is a neutral technical matter.
**A:** False. Saying a system has an explainability problem means it is complicated enough to be treated as a black box, and that decision is rarely neutral. Explainability research is almost never applied to favourable decisions; it is funded to explain refusals. Ask who benefits from the box staying closed.

**Q8 (EXPLAIN).** Intermission: what is "fabric" and what is the explainability paradox?
**A:** Fabric is Beer's term for a substrate with the properties of a computer that does not depend on an algorithm specified in advance (ponds, petri dishes, compass needles). The paradox: explanation is demystifying (it shows how the conjurer put the rabbit in the hat), so "fully explainable" and "genuinely intelligent" pull in opposite directions.

**Q9 (SA).** Describe Beer's computing pond and why it failed.
**A:** A tank of Daphnia, algae and larvae with iron filings among the dead leaves; the Daphnia ingested the filings so they would respond to a magnetic field (the input channel). The aim was to map a computing problem onto initial conditions and read the evolved state as the answer; neither correspondence was specified. It failed mundanely: filings accumulated as tiny permanent magnets suspended in the water, collapsing any organisation that formed.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A vendor claims their AI is both fully explainable and genuinely creative. What test from the intermission do you apply?
**A:** Ask whether the result could in principle be produced with pencil and paper given unlimited time. If yes, there is no magic, only iterated simple operations at high throughput. Expect explainability and genuine surprise to trade off.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** A bank executive says "we know our risk models in huge detail, so we are safe." Which Chapter 3 anti-pattern applies?
**A:** Deep-but-partial expertise: knowing a great deal about a subset of a complex system raises confidence disproportionately to reliability. It is how financiers talk themselves into "it's different this time", and, per the notes, how the State Department underperforms a parrot trained to say "don't start a war".

**Q12 (EXPLAIN).** What does it mean that "ethics of AI is business ethics"?
**A:** Corporations have operated above the black-box threshold for centuries, so AI's accountability problems already exist in non-computational form, and corporations answer to far weaker rights of explanation. The AI-ethics agenda should be applied to them too.

## Section 5 - Chapter 4: How to Psychoanalyse a Non-human Intelligence

**Q1 (SA).** State Ashby's Law in plain and in full form.
**A:** Plain: only variety can absorb variety; a regulator needs at least as much variety as the system it regulates. Full: a system can achieve stability only if every source of variability from the environment is matched by an equal or greater source of variety in the regulatory system. Beer called it the cybernetic equivalent of Newton's laws of motion.

**Q2 (SA).** What is "variety", and is it intrinsic to a system?
**A:** The number of states a system can be in. It is not intrinsic: it is a property of how you choose to describe the system, so you must choose your black boxes and say which states you count.

**Q3 (EXPLAIN).** Why does Davies advise comparing varieties rather than measuring them?
**A:** It is far easier to check whether regulator variety is greater or less than input variety than to count bits, and "we all know what someone looks like when they're overloaded". The comparison is tractable where the measurement is not. The notes flag this audit as the most useful advice in the book.

**Q4 (MC).** Which is NOT one of the three moves for fixing a variety mismatch? (a) attenuate the input; (b) amplify the regulator by building subsystems; (c) split the problem into separable ones; (d) hire more people to work harder.
**A:** (d). Requisite variety is a hard constraint: no effort, funding or good intentions substitutes for a regulator with too little variety.

**Q5 (SA).** Define variety attenuation and give the thermostat example and its failure mode.
**A:** Deliberately discarding information assumed irrelevant so a smaller regulator suffices. A thermostat turns continuous temperature into "too hot / too cold" for a two-state heater. Failure mode: you may have thrown away what you needed; it is useless when the lab is on fire and the temperature passes 100 degrees. You always attenuate unless you describe the whole universe; drawing black boxes is itself attenuation.

**Q6 (EXPLAIN).** Why can't variety literally be "amplified", and what do you do instead?
**A:** Information relates to entropy; amplifying it would breach the second law. Instead you do the stereo-amplifier trick (a small voltage switching a larger one) by building regulatory subsystems that use a different source of variety, like a heat-sensitive switch. If you can build one thermostat, you can build a thousand.

**Q7 (SA).** What is regulation by veto, and what does Ashby's homeostat show about it?
**A:** Split the range into acceptable and unacceptable regions; any subsystem can veto the current state, and the system searches until nothing vetoes. Vetoing is informationally cheap. The homeostat: four connected compass needles acting as voltmeters; a needle dragged to one side cycled randomly through settings (veto), a needle mid-range stopped (acceptance). Four inputs with thirty states each give 810,000 combinations, yet it reached and recovered stability quickly.

**Q8 (T/F).** Splitting any big regulatory problem into smaller ones always helps.
**A:** False. Only if the variables are genuinely separable (temperature vs food vs water in a squirrel cage). Splitting a non-separable problem destroys it, as smashing a Rubik's Cube into twenty-six sub-cubes "solves" them all and solves nothing.

**Q9 (SA).** Define ataxia, purpose tremor, ultrastability and viability.
**A:** Ataxia: feedback too weak, so the gunsight trails its target. Purpose tremor: feedback too strong, so it oscillates and overcorrects (Wiener's automated gunsight; both named after cerebellar injury symptoms; the moment control engineering became cybernetics). Ultrastability (Ashby): ability to adapt to shocks outside the range the designer anticipated. Viability (Beer): the potential to persist as an independent entity, its descendant.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A department head runs 40 teams and wants to review every decision personally. Which concept applies and what are the options?
**A:** Requisite variety: one person cannot match the variety of forty teams. Options: attenuate (standard policies, define what counts), amplify (build subsystems that decide locally, like a thermostat per cage), or split the problem into separable parts. Adding hours of effort cannot fix an impossibility.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** A control system is well-funded yet keeps being surprised. Which three defects does the "model of its environment" audit look for?
**A:** (1) Sources of environmental variety the model omits; (2) regulatory variety that is overestimated; (3) information assumed to be transmitted that is not. Also ask what the system does not monitor.

**Q12 (EXPLAIN).** Walk through the squirrel survival facility example and say how it generalises.
**A:** Treat squirrel plus cage as a black box with outputs (weight, body temperature, heart rate, ambient temperature, light/dark) and define the survival set. A regulator takes the outputs as inputs and supplies cage inputs. Lighting has two states, so a one-bit switch matches it. Temperature is continuous: build a variable heater (matched variety) or use "too hot / too cold" with an on/off heater (attenuation, a thermostat). For a hundred cages, fit a thermostat to each (amplification). Add staff and husbandry policies to attenuate incoming variety. That is a squirrel farm, and it is also an organisation.

**Q13 (SA).** Why is the accountability sink a "solution as much as a problem"?
**A:** Building cause-and-effect subsystems so managers don't decide case by case is exactly variety amplification; unappealability is its side effect.

**Q14 (SA).** What was Beer's gibe about 1970s companies, and what anti-pattern does it name?
**A:** That they recruited the greatest geniuses of humanity then set them to memorising the phone book to save a few seconds turning pages: automating inside the existing structure instead of redesigning whole processes (a legacy of Wiener's wartime choices).

## Section 6 - Chapter 5 and its intermission: the Viable System Model and decerebrate cats

**Q1 (SA).** List the five functions of the Viable System Model with one-line roles.
**A:** S1 Operations (makes change in the world; could exist independently). S2 Regulation (stops operations clashing; timetables, quartermaster). S3 Integration/optimisation (coordinates the S1s, strikes and polices resource bargains, handles residual variety). S4 Intelligence (the "there and then", the part of the environment not yet in contact with operations). S5 Philosophy/identity (balances 3 against 4 by fixing what kind of organisation this is).

**Q2 (T/F).** The five systems correspond to five boxes on an org chart.
**A:** False. They are functions, which exist whether or not anyone holds the title. Forcing the five onto chart boxes, or stopping at one level of recursion, are the main analyst errors.

**Q3 (SA).** How do you tell System 2 from System 3?
**A:** System 2 is the management everyone agrees is necessary; System 3 is the management people complain about. System 3 managers routinely disguise themselves as System 2.

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** What is management by exception, and what are its two failure modes?
**A:** Give people tasks and leave them to them; push variety-handling to the lowest level that can absorb it and size System 3 to the residual variety, not the total. It is the only allocation respecting Ashby's law. Failures: micromanagement (S3 tries to fight S1's variety alone, bloats and neglects coordination) and excessive delegation (no bargains or planning, so conflicts are settled by politics and hoarding).

**Q5 (SA).** What are algedonic signals and how should they be built?
**A:** "Red handles": a channel letting any level signal directly to the top, across intervening levels, that something threatens catastrophic or unacceptable consequences. Model on a train driver's emergency brake: physical effect plus organisational message. Resource the channel and the translation capacity at the receiving end.

**Q6 (SA).** What is the difference between information and data?
**A:** Variety counts as information only if it has a causal role in decision-making, arriving in time and in a form the receiver can act on. Otherwise it is data sitting on disk.

**Q7 (EXPLAIN).** Read the 2008 crisis through the VSM as the notes do.
**A:** A central bank is an S1 of the government's economic management; independence was its resource bargain in return for stable inflation and a functioning monetary system. In the 2000s central banks named the calm ("Great Moderation", "NICE times", "Goldilocks economy") while a debt bubble and house-price spiral built. Their forecasting was here-and-now extended (inflation forecast targeting), not real S4. Warnings in 2006-07 had no channel in a form a governor could recognise as requiring action, so they stayed data. Only Lehman's bankruptcy, an algedonic squeal, released latent capacity. Root failure: S5, an identity that defined certain information as none of their business.

**Q8 (T/F).** Frequent red-handle signals show the organisation is healthy and responsive.
**A:** False. Frequent emergency escalations mean systems further down are missing, misidentified or dysfunctional. But zero red handles is not health either; the channel may not exist.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** One division of a company bounces from crisis to crisis while others cope. Diagnose.
**A:** It is probably an unrecognised System 1 never given its own internal 2/3/4/5. Give it the missing internal functions rather than more supervision from above (occasionally it is not a viable unit and should merge or dissolve).

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** Your boss presents quarterly forecasts as proof the company "looks ahead". Respond using Chapter 5.
**A:** Forecasting is the here-and-now in another form (extrapolating the current model). Real System 4 looks at what is not yet relevant, often because it doesn't yet exist. If you forecast constantly, it becomes hard to believe anyone who says you are not looking ahead.

**Q11 (SA).** Intermission: what is a decerebrate organisation and what is the test?
**A:** One that still runs operations competently but has lost intelligence and identity (S4/S5), so it cannot act purposively. Test: check what it still tracks versus what it stopped tracking. Warning sign: attention runs only to immediate surroundings (this quarter's revenue, current staffing) and planning has disappeared.

**Q12 (EXPLAIN).** Why are Systems 4 and 5 the easiest to cut, and what is Beer's stock example?
**A:** They produce no directly measurable output, and their absence is invisible until a crisis. Beer's example is a university: teaching happens, terms start and end, accounts balance, but the capacity to decide what it is for has gone. It coasts in a stable environment; a shock (funding regime change, demand collapse) brings crisis, then only two outcomes: find resources to rebuild the missing functions, or collapse.

**Q13 (SCENARIO).** You notice your employer scrapping its planning and horizon-scanning roles to save overhead. What does Davies' corollary advise?
**A:** It is active decerebration; the crisis is deferred, not avoided. From personal experience Davies says: update your resume and start calling recruiters.

**Q14 (SA).** Why do the notes say a consistent identity is an information-management tool?
**A:** It attenuates variety by making most options unthinkable, e.g. refusing to consider Wagner's Ring cycle for an Elton John tour. Failure: an identity that rules relevant information out of scope.

## Section 7 - Chapter 6: Economics and How It Got That Way

**Q1 (SA).** State the four steps of the Ricardian Vice.
**A:** (a) Model a feature of the economy, stripping away nearly all complexity; (b) make strong, often empirically questionable simplifying assumptions; (c) derive the conclusion from them; (d) act as if the conclusion has been proved about the real world. The term is Schumpeter's.

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** Walk through Ricardo's England and Portugal example and say where the vice enters.
**A:** Cloth costs 100 labour-hours in England, 90 in Portugal; wine 120 in England, 80 in Portugal. Portugal is absolutely better at both, but relative prices differ (a bolt of cloth is worth nine-eighths of a barrel of wine in Portugal, five-sixths in England), so Portugal gains by making only wine and trading for cloth. It is logically airtight; Samuelson could name no other proposition in the social sciences both airtight and non-trivial. The vice is the next step: add more commodities, inputs, brands, firms, investment, strategy, or notice that countries import goods much like those they export, and you can generate whichever conclusion your setup requires. People resist not the arithmetic but the leap to a claim about trade.

**Q3 (SA).** What four conditions must hold before trusting a constrained-optimisation model (electricity example)?
**A:** Commodity good; observable marginal cost curve; genuinely independent spot markets; negligible strategic interaction. If any fails, the model is doing rhetoric, not measurement.

**Q4 (MC).** Economics performs which VSM function for policymakers? (a) S1; (b) S2; (c) S4; (d) S5.
**A:** (d) S5, an identity-creating function: it tells rulers what they don't have to think about. The Ricardian Vice corresponds to a weak S4.

**Q5 (T/F).** "Ad hoc" is a mild criticism in economics.
**A:** False. It is a grave insult, disqualifying rules of thumb, habits and fashions from counting as explanations.

**Q6 (SA).** Define microfoundations, homo economicus, and the representative individual/firm.
**A:** Microfoundations: every large-scale phenomenon must be justified as the cumulative result of individual optimising decisions (a commitment to opening the black box). Homo economicus: an agent who cares only about price and quantity, is perfectly informed about future supply and demand and can read others' minds. Representative individual/firm: assuming identical preferences or aggregating producers into one to dodge strategic interaction.

**Q7 (EXPLAIN).** What does "markets as computing fabric" mean and how do you use it as a diagnostic?
**A:** Whenever there is a hole in an economic explanation, the market is credited with the information processing, a machine from which you get out more than you put in. If an argument needs agents to have information nobody explains how they got, this assumption is doing the work. Four sources: Smith's invisible hand, Hayek's answer to the calculation debate, post-war equilibrium proofs and financial-economics market efficiency; none licenses inferring that an observed market outcome is optimal.

**Q8 (SA).** Why does a single maximand matter?
**A:** Requiring one index of success forces every corporate value, regulatory relationship and long-term brand consideration to be re-expressed as beliefs about future profits, so ambiguity, perception and incompatible values become unsayable.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A colleague argues competition alone will eliminate workplace discrimination, so anti-discrimination law is unnecessary. Which anti-pattern is this?
**A:** Proving it in the model, then acting in the world. The model shows discrimination can't survive competition, but only because information relevant to the actual problem was thrown away.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A safety engineer says a sensor fault "might cause a crash"; the finance lead asks for the expected-cost figure. What does Chapter 6 say?
**A:** Safety information is not a statement about expected costs, however comfortable you are pricing a life. Information that cannot be expressed as expected cost, such as safety warnings and red-handle signals, needs a separate channel rather than a monetary translation.

**Q11 (EXPLAIN).** Why is "all models are wrong but some are useful" called a weak defence?
**A:** If you believed it, you would have built a systematic process to check whether your model had stopped being useful. Economists rarely do.

**Q12 (SA).** What translation do the notes give for "however you look at the numbers", and which test do you apply to any model result?
**A:** "However *I* look at *these* numbers." Apply "does this survive the fifth complication?": add multiple commodities, inputs, brands, firms, investment, strategy and see whether the conclusion holds or just changes with the specification.

## Section 8 - Chapter 7 and its intermission: accounting hallucination and Cybersyn

**Q1 (SA).** Distinguish management accounting from financial reporting.
**A:** Management accounting measures process efficiency and allocates costs to specific decisions. Financial reporting summarises the whole enterprise for those who finance it, on standard periods and consistent conventions, and thereby attenuates variety.

**Q2 (SA).** State the attributability test.
**A:** The extent to which a cost can be attributed to an output is exactly the extent to which the cost could be avoided if you weren't producing it. It is the accountants' version of the fundamental law of accountability. Also: charging a manager costs they cannot influence is pure harm.

**Q3 (EXPLAIN).** Tell the two skincare lines story and its lesson.
**A:** A factory makes bulk barrels for construction workers and small tubs sold to beauty buyers at ten times the price, from essentially the same cream. Under generally accepted principles the luxury line looks far more profitable (its marketing is treated as period costs), while the bulk line absorbs a disproportionate share of fixed packaging cost allocated by direct labour. An investor asks why keep the low-margin business; consultants compare in-house manufacturing with an external supplier whose overheads are low because it does little marketing or R&D; in-house loses; manufacturing is sold. Marketing and research costs don't vanish, quality-control and logistics problems appear, and two years later consultants are hired to explain why the star product's profitability slumped. Lesson: business hallucination from decisions made on numbers built for another purpose.

**Q4 (SA).** What is "business hallucination"?
**A:** A firm acting on a picture of itself generated by reporting conventions rather than its actual production process.

**Q5 (T/F).** "Generally accepted accounting principles" should signal an accountability sink.
**A:** True, per the notes. The conventions were chosen for consistency and audit, not truth; an accounting system is an almost perfect sink, and even its builders may not understand it.

**Q6 (SA).** What was "chicken efficiency" and what went wrong?
**A:** A fast-food chain judged managers on the proportion of each bird sold. Managers stopped frying an hour before closing, lost heaps of sales and hit 100 per cent. A case of measuring the wrong efficiency.

**Q7 (EXPLAIN).** Why are consultants called accountability sinks and "brains for hire" a false economy?
**A:** They are professional scapegoats hired when management must do something that would destroy its ability to manage afterwards. Renting expertise fails because reproducing the middle managers' knowledge costs the same hours at higher rates. Telling clients what they want to hear helps repeat business, so consultancy cannot supply the managerial class its identity function.

**Q8 (SA).** Explain "Einstein reads the phone book" and "the decision nobody made".
**A:** Beer's joke about hiring humanity's greatest geniuses to memorise a directory: companies in the 1960s computerised existing paper bookkeeping instead of rethinking it. The decision nobody made: when computing arrived, no one chose to keep financial reporting as the basis of control; it simply happened.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** Your CFO proposes outsourcing a division because its allocated overhead makes it look unprofitable. Which questions apply?
**A:** What decision was each number collected for? Would each cost disappear if we stopped making the product (attributability)? Does the manager control that part of the resource bargain? Remember that fixed versus variable is itself a strategic decision, so the dispute is really about the production process. Learn the numbers by looking at the business, never the business by looking at the numbers.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** Management proposes cutting middle managers as pure overhead. What does Chapter 7 add?
**A:** Middle managers are the translation and error-correction layer between a distorted reporting channel and reality; cutting them cuts translation capacity. Budget deliberately for excess management capacity (Machiavelli's constraint: a prince who is not wise cannot be well advised), and when complexity outgrows structure, change the architecture rather than add supervision.

**Q11 (SA).** Intermission: name the components of Cybersyn and map them to the VSM.
**A:** Cybernet (several hundred repurposed telex machines carrying production data; partial System 2), Cyberstride (statistical filter classifying departures as blip, trend change or new normal; variety engineering), the Operations Room (about a dozen managers with wall displays; System 3), and Checo (economic simulator; intended System 4, planned but never delivered).

**Q12 (T/F).** Cybersyn was an attempt to run a command economy by computer.
**A:** False. It was a large-scale "management by exceptions" system built on the VSM; both neoliberals and socialists repeat the myth. Management cybernetics already shows central planning cannot respect requisite variety.

**Q13 (EXPLAIN).** Why was Cybersyn "out of scale", and what did the interventors do to it?
**A:** A few hundred telex machines could not match the variety of an industrial economy ("does not pass the laugh test"); Chile also nationalised faster than it could manage, without an industrial plan. Interventors were officials inside nationalised factories passing down central orders, creating an impression of technocratic control rather than devolved autonomy, and provoking reaction.

**Q14 (SCENARIO).** You are building a monitoring system for 500 sites. What do the Cybersyn lessons say to build first?
**A:** The exception filter before the command centre; cheap, reliable, record-generating data channels (the telex network was the most useful part, letting the government track deliveries during a trucking strike); and a plan to win cooperation from the field.

**Q15 (SA).** What does the notes' line on violence as a "variety amplifier" say?
**A:** Violence is a horribly effective variety amplifier (one guard with a gun controls a prison); a state overloaded by its political environment and lacking consensual legitimacy is highly likely to reach for atrocity as the only remaining route to stability. The chapter closes with the Pinochet coup, which ended the experiment before it could even fail informatively.

## Section 9 - Chapter 8: Enter Friedman

**Q1 (SA).** State the Friedman doctrine and its three moves.
**A:** The corporation is an "artificial person" with only "artificial responsibilities", so responsibility lies with the individual manager, whose job is to increase profits for shareholders; corporate social responsibility is the moral equivalent of taxation (spending other people's money, moving from "market mechanism" to "political mechanism"). Moves: (1) look through the corporation to individuals; (2) replace real shareholders with an abstract "representative shareholder" black box; (3) invite executives to split their moral selves between "a person in his own right" and "in his capacity as a businessman".

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** Why does the doctrine "fail as a lie in both directions"?
**A:** Executives are told to ignore the reality of the company and act as if hired by human beings, then ignore the reality of human beings and act as if employed by a theoretical construct. Real shareholders number in the thousands, hold competing firms, include the workers' own pension funds, and change daily.

**Q3 (SA).** What crisis did the doctrine solve?
**A:** Corporations at the end of the 1960s had lost any sense of purpose amid an explosion of environmental variety. It supplied a single maximising goal and a personal accountability sink, at the price of denying that the organisation exists as a decision-making system.

**Q4 (T/F).** Courts have found a legal "fiduciary duty to maximise shareholder value".
**A:** False. Courts have never found a duty to run a company one way rather than another, let alone maximise one class of securities. The phrase is legal jargon used outside its legal context. People also cite it to Friedman's essay without checking, a tell that ideology is at work.

**Q5 (SA).** What is "semantic drift" in this chapter?
**A:** The wording shifted from "increase" to "maximise" and from "profits" to "shareholder value", matching economics' maximising language and escaping awkward questions (which accounting standard, what period, what risk, what trade-off between present return and future growth).

**Q6 (EXPLAIN).** How is debt an "information technology" and when does it become control?
**A:** For the lender, debt attenuates variety: ask only whether the rate compensates time and risk and whether I'll be repaid. Small debt is one constraint among many; heavy debt makes the payment signal swamp all other information; extreme debt (no action can generate enough cash) becomes an instrument of control, as the creditor can break up the company or extend a loan in return for concessions.

**Q7 (EXPLAIN).** Walk through the leveraged buyout worked example.
**A:** A buyout firm targets a company with corporate jets, country clubs, lavish expenses, obsolete product lines and cosy customers. It puts up 10 per cent, borrows 90, buys it, then makes the company borrow to repay the acquisition debt (lenders were cheap because they knew this was coming; interest is tax-deductible where dividends are not). Now every priority is subordinated to cash, since bankruptcy ends executives' careers: the perks stop and hard decisions get made. Residual risk sits with workers, suppliers, customers and managers; limited liability caps the buyer's loss at the original 10 per cent.

**Q8 (SA).** What did Jensen (with Meckling) contribute, in the notes' terms?
**A:** A rigorous mathematical footing for the doctrine: "managerial behaviour" (slacking, pursuing social goals with shareholders' money), "agency costs" (shareholders' difficulty checking on managers) and "ownership structure" (the fix, using debt and stock options so managers act as if a personal owner were watching). His efficient-market claim, that share price summarises all available information, in effect treats the stock market as a computing fabric.

**Q9 (SA).** What is Economic Value Added and "private equity's System 5"?
**A:** EVA (Joel Stern): a financial benchmark for investment returns to decide what to do and cut, letting consultants perform buyout-style management on companies unsuited to raiding. Private equity's strong identity and ideology integrate information gathering with operations under one clear goal, making it a faster, more aggressive predator than other non-human decision-making systems.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** An executive says "my duty is to maximise shareholder value, so we cut R&D." Name two problems the notes would raise.
**A:** The "duty" is exhortation in legal costume, and "maximise profits" abolishes questions (what period, what accounting standard, what risk) rather than resolving them. Also, managing to the share price demands only the quantifiable, so history, community, customer trust and technology drop out unless they fit the spreadsheet.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** A buyout firm has bought all the clearly wasteful companies. What does Chapter 8 predict it will do next?
**A:** Definition creep: widen what counts as a valid target (unused debt tax shields, merely stable cashflow, a temporary market dip) rather than winding up. Companies also pre-emptively behave as if already bought out, spreading the pathology without the transaction.

**Q12 (EXPLAIN).** In what sense is Friedman "the system talking", and what is the unremarked class struggle?
**A:** The capitalist world had reached a stage where it needed him; if he hadn't written the essay someone else would have; neither he nor Welch is the villain but the system that replicated them. The class struggle was capitalism versus managerialism in the 1970s-80s, and the managers lost comprehensively. The doctrine landed because "the greatest fear of the manager is management".

**Q13 (SA).** What is the notes' definition of financialisation, and of neoliberalism?
**A:** Financialisation is not a parasite on the real economy but the growing importance of the financial aspects of the whole system (finance is how resource bargains are struck, plans committed to and information transferred). Neoliberalism (working definition): shareholder primacy in corporate governance plus deregulation, privatisation and low taxes in public policy.

## Section 10 - Chapter 9: The Morbid Symptoms

**Q1 (SA).** What does "reversing the polarity" mean?
**A:** Jerome Levy's premise was that the investing class exists to serve the working class by insuring them against loss and providing goods. The neoliberal era ran it backwards: the working class insures the corporate sector against the business cycle, using unemployment as the variety attenuator.

**Q2 (SA).** What was the Great Moderation in these terms?
**A:** The sharp decline in volatility of output and inflation, achieved by using the unemployment rate as a variety attenuator. It worked until corporations, having cut their own capacity, could no longer absorb shocks, and the other escape route, insuring through elected government, was closed by public-sector decerebration.

**Q3 (EXPLAIN).** Explain transduction and why outsourcing narrows the channel to "one bit".
**A:** Information must be preserved when a signal crosses a boundary, arriving in a form and time that lets it enter decisions. Much middle-management "redundancy" was exactly this capacity. Outsourcing swaps internal boundaries for external ones and narrows the channel to two states: "going as anticipated" or "it's stopped working". Since it nearly always says OK, nobody can estimate the reserve bandwidth needed for "conditions are changing", and with targets to hit it always looks cuttable.

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** Trace the GE/Welch model end to end.
**A:** GE institutionalised always making the quarterly number. Costs are controllable within a quarter and revenues are not, so pressure lands on headcount: whole layers of middle management go, plus annual rank-and-yank of the bottom 10 per cent kept chiefly for its threat. The removed managers were boundary processing capacity, so signals arrive degraded and outsourcing makes remaining boundaries one-bit contracts. Financial services grew until subjective estimates dominated earnings; in the housing bubble the mortgage arm "jacked up the volume without controls" and misrepresented loan quality, ending in a $1.5bn Justice Department settlement. GE also offset a one-off $2bn gain by closing factories and making over 1,000 people redundant to avoid a spike spoiling smooth growth.

**Q5 (SA).** Complete and explain: "if you consistently demand the impossible, you will inevitably get..."
**A:** The unethical. A target regime demanding the impossible generates fraud rather than performance, so check what your targets are quietly commissioning.

**Q6 (SA).** Define status syndrome and deaths of despair, with sources.
**A:** Status syndrome (Marmot, Whitehall): civil service grade predicts serious mortality independent of income, smoking and family history, so being out of control is physiologically harmful. Deaths of despair (Case and Deaton): rapid rise in suicide, alcoholism and opiate addiction, evidence that ignorance is not the only or most lethal variety attenuator.

**Q7 (EXPLAIN).** Why does Davies read populism as a "one-bit scream"?
**A:** Where a population's channels carry very few bits, the only message possible is "Help! the Current State of Affairs is Intolerable to me." The medium is the message: its existence is the signal and its content is noise, so respond to the fact of distress, not its content. The populist promise of a simpler world equals reversing time, and the broken channels were the middle managers and civil servants nobody wants back.

**Q8 (SA).** What is time inconsistency (Prescott and Kydland) and how do the notes read it cybernetically?
**A:** Governments know the long-term answer but are destabilised by election-cycle feedback, so money control should pass to an independent unaccountable body (Odysseus tied to the mast). Cybernetically, central bankers concluded that accountability channels from those hurt by unemployment and recession were working too well, transmitting too much information.

**Q9 (SA).** What is "el pueblo" and what was the "blind spot in control parameters" before 2008?
**A:** Allende's name for the System 5 box: the last-resort absorber of information, which hardly ever does anything and has no postal address; even a dictatorship faces a collective veto. The blind spot: only government deficits were monitored; private-sector debt was outside the control system entirely.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** Your firm is moving to a quarterly "always beat the forecast" regime. List predicted consequences.
**A:** Costs will be prioritised over revenues (management controls costs, revenue takes more than a quarter), and managers will manage the earnings line rather than the business. The redundancy threat (like debt) will swamp judgement, and demands for the impossible produce unethical behaviour.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** A government proposes outsourcing hospitals and train operations to cut costs. Which Chapter 9 warnings apply?
**A:** Outsourcing core functions (states did trains, hospitals and defence procurement, with more severe consequences than corporations shedding peripherals), the one-bit contract channel, and manufactured arms-length agencies and long-dated contracts that bind successors and shrink the share of spending attributable to identifiable politicians.

**Q12 (T/F).** Davies says populists will restore the broken communication channels.
**A:** False. They promise to restore them without any method, since the channels were made of the middle managers and civil servants nobody wants back. Nobody designs democratic channels from an information-engineering view (proportional systems carry more information than first-past-the-post; a monthly poll has less lag than an annual one).

**Q13 (SA).** What do Marx, Kalecki, Keynes and Friedman have in common in Chapter 9?
**A:** Reserve army of the unemployed, Kalecki's version of the Levy profit equations, Keynes' general theory and Friedman's "natural rate" (refined into the NAIRU) all point the same way: capital-owners tolerate lower output as the price of keeping control of the wage bargain.

## Section 11 - Chapter 10: What Is to Be Done?

**Q1 (SA).** What is the single private-equity reform the book endorses, and why only that one?
**A:** Any entity taking control of an operating company must guarantee its debts. It closes the accountability sink at limited liability, so bad news can travel upstream to the deciding entity and the attraction of buying a company with its own money disappears. Regulating "excessive" borrowing by rule fails requisite variety. (Of Warren's eight-part plan, only this point is needed cybernetically.)

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** Why is limited liability "a resource bargain, not a natural justice principle"?
**A:** It was introduced for US joint-stock corporations in the 1820s to reach a larger pool of savings for industrialisation. Bargains get reviewed when they cause trouble. Keep it for small non-controlling investors; allow no further levels.

**Q3 (SA).** What does "viability over maximisation" mean?
**A:** Corporations are decision-making systems with no motives. What looks like a profit motive is an imbalance between here-and-now and there-and-then, because long-term planning cannot be heard against the financial market disciplinary system. Quieten the amplified signal (a monthly interest bill or implicit takeover threat); viable systems seek stability, not maximisation.

**Q4 (SA).** State the three-step argument against economists in charge.
**A:** (1) Any system set up to maximise a single objective can go bonkers; (2) every maximiser needs a higher-level system watching over it; (3) therefore economists can't be in charge as they currently are. The top level of an autonomous system cannot itself be a maximiser.

**Q5 (SA).** What are Galbraith's technostructure and Simon's behavioural theory?
**A:** Technostructure: a professional/managerial class spanning business, government, military and media that maintains stability; executives' main priority is handling uncertainty. Simon: profits are a constraint on behaviour, not a maximand; managers do enough to keep investors happy then work on things they can be proud of.

**Q6 (T/F).** Only non-cybernetic problems can genuinely be insoluble.
**A:** True per the notes: an organisational problem's complexity comes from its humans, who therefore have enough variety to match it; it cannot be bigger than the people whose misdirected efforts created it.

**Q7 (EXPLAIN).** What are the roles of generative art (Eno's *77 Million Paintings*) and transformer networks in the argument?
**A:** Eno's work recombines a few elements under a few rules and has a purpose (exploring possibilities) that is not maximising: businesses should be artists, not paperclip maximisers. A transformer can compress text into a summary and expand a short instruction into a long explanation, a variety amplifier for two-way communication between mass audiences and small decision-making systems.

**Q8 (SCENARIO).** A bank wants to deploy a profit-maximising trading algorithm. What does Davies require?
**A:** A higher-level supervisor plus a red handle for the decided-upon, as part of the resource bargain. Defining an objective function throws away all other information, so something outside the information set will eventually destroy the system.

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A city wants a human official always on hand to blame for automated benefit decisions. Davies' answer?
**A:** Give up the demand: important decisions will be made by actual robots, a consequence of complexity. Repair the feedback link instead, since people are less angry when they feel listened to. "I blame the system" has to be said literally.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A team uses AI to run existing processes faster. Which anti-pattern is this?
**A:** Using new AI to make existing structures run faster (Shakespeare, Machiavelli and Napoleon designing tax forms). A big change in complexity requires equal reorganisation; likewise social media as "better shouting" has so far brought none.

**Q11 (SA).** What is Keynes' dentist benchmark?
**A:** Economists could count their discipline a success when regarded as useful and competent technicians. Keep the optimisation toolkit, demote it from governing philosophy.

**Q12 (EXPLAIN).** What did Beer's household dial represent?
**A:** Beer's Chilean dream of a dial for signalling satisfaction: the original red-handle alert, never built beyond his son's shed prototype.

**Q13 (SA).** What does the notes' treatment of the VSM say about how to use it in Chapter 10?
**A:** Use it to check a proposal, not to generate one: it tells you what to think about, whether you are addressing the right problem and whether the system can survive the change; it won't have the idea for you.

# Final Exam - 20 cumulative questions

**F1.** State the fundamental law of accountability and its corollary, and show how the sink test applies it.
**A:** Ability to change a decision equals accountability for it; diminishing accountability follows from industrialisation. Sink test: could any information from the affected person change the outcome? If not, nobody with power to change it is exposed, so it is a sink (Ch 1, 3).

**F2.** Why can't a regulator be fixed by more effort when its variety is too low? Who named the law?
**A:** Ashby's Law makes it a hard constraint; no funding, effort or data fixes an impossibility in principle (Ch 4). Distinguish failure for lack of resources from impossibility (Ch 10).

**F3.** (Synthesis) Explain why the accountability sink is both a variety amplifier and a source of catastrophe.
**A:** A rule substitutes cause-effect subsystems for case-by-case decisions (amplification, Ch 4), but every rule is a model of the world (Ch 1), so unanticipated inputs produce absurd outcomes with no feedback route (Schiphol). Good sinks keep slow feedback (common law); bad ones are unappealable and unrevisable.

**F4.** (T/F) POSIWID is a claim about individuals' motives.
**A:** False; it is a claim about the system and System 5 (Ch 2, 5, 10).

**F5.** Name the five systems and one failure mode for each.
**A:** S1 not recognised as viable (problem child); S2 absent or S3 hiding in it; S3 micromanagement or abdication; S4 absent or replaced by forecasting; S5 identity that rules relevant information out (Ch 5).

**F6.** (Synthesis) How does the decerebrate organisation connect to 2008 and to Chapter 9's cuts?
**A:** Decerebrate = lost S4/S5 (Ch 5i). Central banks' forecasting was here-and-now extended and their identity excluded private debt (Ch 5, 9); Welch-style cuts removed boundary translation capacity (Ch 9).

**F7.** List the Ricardian Vice's steps and the four-condition check.
**A:** Strip down, assume strongly, derive, act as if proved (Ch 6); check commodity good, observable marginal cost, independent spot markets, negligible strategic interaction.

**F8.** (Scenario) A product looks unprofitable on the reports. What do you do before acting?
**A:** Ask what decision the number was collected for; apply the attributability test; check the manager controls the cost; beware business hallucination (Ch 7, skincare example).

**F9.** What distinguishes "data" from "information", and why do "we had warnings" failures happen?
**A:** Information has a causal role in a decision, in time and in usable form; most such failures are translation failures, not collection failures (Ch 4, 5, 9).

**F10.** What was Cybersyn really and what did it teach?
**A:** A management-by-exceptions system on the VSM (Cybernet, Cyberstride, Operations Room, undelivered Checo), out of scale, provoked reaction, ended by the Pinochet coup before it could fail informatively (Ch 7i).

**F11.** (Synthesis) Link the Friedman doctrine to the sink concept and to S5.
**A:** It gives managers a personal sink (a separate work-self) and a single maximising identity (an S5 that rules most information out), by replacing real shareholders with a black-box representative (Ch 8, 6).

**F12.** Describe the LBO mechanics and who bears the risk.
**A:** 10 per cent equity, 90 per cent debt, the acquired company borrows to repay; limited liability caps the buyer's loss, risk sits with workers, suppliers, customers, managers (Ch 8).

**F13.** What is "reversing the polarity" and how does it relate to the Great Moderation and populism?
**A:** The working class insures the corporate sector via unemployment (Great Moderation); once corporate capacity was gone and government capacity decerebrated, the one-bit scream followed (Ch 9).

**F14.** (Scenario) Your company will outsource a monitoring function. Which tests apply?
**A:** Boundary-crossing audit: trace a real signal across each boundary; channel-state test: if the contract carries only "OK / stopped working", you bought decerebration (Ch 9).

**F15.** What does "demand the impossible and you get the unethical" imply for control fraud?
**A:** Unrealistic targets plus underinvested compliance can produce self-organising control fraud with no boss-to-crook link (Ch 1, 9).

**F16.** (Synthesis) Why can't regulating excessive leverage by rule work, and what works?
**A:** Variety of financial situations exceeds any rule (Ch 4, 10). Locate where feedback is removed (limited liability) and make the controller guarantee the debts (Ch 10).

**F17.** What is the role of the red handle and what must accompany it?
**A:** A direct level-crossing channel for catastrophe signals; also translation capacity at the receiver; its frequency is a diagnostic about lower levels (Ch 5, 10).

**F18.** (T/F) Davies concludes a human should always be available to blame.
**A:** False; give up that demand and repair feedback (Ch 10, Ch 3).

**F19.** (Synthesis) Compare Chapter 3's explainability argument with the Chapter 3 intermission's.
**A:** Ch 3: explainability is political (who sees inside; research funded to explain refusals, not favourable decisions). Intermission: explanation is demystifying, so explainability and genuine intelligence pull against each other.

**F20.** List the "three revolutions" and say how Chile fits.
**A:** Managerial (forgotten), cybernetic (never arrived), neoliberal (succeeded). The debate was settled in Chile by helicopters and guns at La Moneda in 1973, not by diagrams (Ch 0, 7i).
