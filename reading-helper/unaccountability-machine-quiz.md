# The Unaccountability Machine Quiz Book
*The Unaccountability Machine: Why Big Systems Make Terrible Decisions — and How The World Lost Its Mind* by Dan Davies

**How to use:** Cover the answers, answer from memory (out loud or in writing), then check. Anything you miss is a section to reread. Each chapter has short-answer (SA), true/false (T/F), and multiple-choice (MC) questions. A cumulative final exam is at the end. Chapters follow the skill's numbering: Introduction, Chapters 1-10, and the three Intermissions (3i, 5i, 7i) in book order.

---

## Introduction

**Q1 (SA).** What is the "decisions, not events" stance, and what two explanations does it set aside?
**A:** Treat outcomes as the output of decision-making systems. It sets aside conspiracy (hidden motive) and cock-up (individual blunder). Systems have no hidden motivations (a consistent output is their purpose) and they don't make "mistakes" (they live with consequences or die of them). Individuals are named only as needed to describe the system ("if it hadn't been them, it would have been someone else").

**Q2 (SA).** What is a "decision-making system," and who are "the decided-upon"?
**A:** A decision-making system is any arrangement (algorithm, handbook, rule book, committee procedure) that produces decisions from standardised information rather than an individual reacting to circumstances. The decided-upon are the many people affected by decisions they had no part in and cannot address a person about.

**Q3 (MC).** Which is NOT one of the three revolutions in the Introduction's lens?
a) managerial  b) cybernetic  c) neoliberal  d) digital
**A:** d. Managerial happened and was forgotten; cybernetic looked promising but never arrived; neoliberal (early 1970s) succeeded.

**Q4 (SA).** What contrast does the Dugdale/Truman example draw?
**A:** In 1954 Sir Thomas Dugdale resigned over the Crichel Down affair even though it was unclear he was personally involved; as minister in charge he bore responsibility. Truman kept "The Buck Stops Here" on his desk in 1953. Sixty-six years later Trump said "the buck stops with everybody" about a shutdown. The intervening industrialisation of decision-making removed the link that made the earlier answer available.

**Q5 (T/F).** The Introduction argues that declining accountability is mainly a moral decline in rulers' character. **A:** False. It treats this as largely a consequence of fewer decisions being made by identifiable humans at all.

**Q6 (T/F).** Regulating the algorithms behind AI loan and insurance decisions is, on this view, a complete answer to unaccountable decisions. **A:** False. Automated decisions are a visible special case; the committees writing the rules are themselves bound by opaque rule books designed to prevent attribution to any individual.

## Chapter 1: 'Something's Up'

**Q1 (SA).** Define an accountability sink and give the diagnostic test.
**A:** An arrangement in which there is nobody to blame: a powerless person who conveys the decision plus a policy with no route of appeal. Test: is there any information from the affected person that could change the outcome? If not, it is a sink.

**Q2 (SA).** State the fundamental law of accountability.
**A:** The extent to which you can change a decision is precisely the extent to which you can be accountable for it, and vice versa. Ask "who can override this?"

**Q3 (SA).** What happened in the Schiphol ground squirrel story, and what does it show?
**A:** In April 1999, 440 ground squirrels from Beijing arrived at Schiphol on a KLM flight without import papers and were put through an industrial shredder built for worthless male chicks (the same machine had taken 200 more squirrels, dozens of turtles and a flock of parakeets). A sensible-looking ministry policy covered most cases; the pet-squirrel craze was the unanticipated input; no channel fed results back to those who could change the policy; staff had acted "formally correctly." It shows how a sink produces gruesome outcomes with nobody to blame.

**Q4 (MC).** Why is the common law described as a *good* accountability sink?
a) You can argue with the judge  b) It is a sink, but it evolves through slow reputational feedback (precedent weighted by judges' seniority and promotion)  c) It removes all rules  d) It is unwritten
**A:** b. A sink is bad when there is no such feedback channel at all.

**Q5 (T/F).** Relying on frontline disobedience as the only safeguard against a bad policy is realistic. **A:** False. It is neither psychologically plausible nor managerially realistic to expect someone to follow orders 99 per cent of the time and then act independently on the hundredth.

**Q6 (SA).** What are "control fraud" and "self-organising control fraud"?
**A:** Control fraud (Bill Black): a criminal takes over a bank and installs incentives that produce fraudulent loans and declared profits, with legitimate-looking cash transfers. Self-organising control fraud (Davies' coinage): the system is so criminogenic that banks organise themselves into fraud with no link between bosses and low-level crooks; executives need only set unrealistic targets and underinvest in compliance.

**Q7 (T/F).** Davies argues that restoring middle-manager discretion is a simple fix for sinks. **A:** False. "Naive de-sinking" reintroduces caprice, unfairness and racism; many policies exist precisely to prevent those. Legitimate reasons for sinks include legal liability shields, insurance underwriting and avoiding corrosive interpersonal conflict.

## Chapter 2: Stafford Beer

**Q1 (SA).** What is Beer's black box axiom?
**A:** "It is not necessary to enter the black box to understand the nature of the function it performs." Draw a boundary, assume nothing about internals, observe how it reacts to its environment, and iterate: do more of what works and less of what doesn't.

**Q2 (SA).** Distinguish clear box, muddy box and black box.
**A:** Clear box: something you control directly, like a factory machine. Muddy box: more analysis would help but at disproportionate effort. Black box: you decide you lack the understanding to treat it otherwise; being a black box is a decision by the observer, not an objective property.

**Q3 (SA).** What does POSIWID mean, and what does it not mean?
**A:** "The purpose of a system is what it does." It is not cynicism and says nothing about the intentions of the people working there. Example: the purpose of the Schiphol pet hotel was to shred squirrels, because the system was set up to ensure compliance with import regulation at lowest cost.

**Q4 (SA).** Explain the resource bargain.
**A:** A unit operates autonomously only to the extent that this doesn't jeopardise the broader system (budget, space, management time, goodwill). Bargains are kept until the situation changes, then renegotiated. Model: a platoon told to take a bridge, with only two expected messages, "bridge captured" or "external factor intervened, renegotiate."

**Q5 (MC).** Homeostasis differs from economists' equilibrium because it:
a) is always optimal  b) carries no implication of optimality, only that forces return the system there  c) ignores feedback  d) applies only to machines
**A:** b.

**Q6 (T/F).** Beer's 1947 army unit of soldiers labelled "psychopathological" showed that an outside theory of their minds was needed first. **A:** False. He chose variables, plotted trends, refused to assume, and followed feedback. The result was a "crash course" method that raised the educational age of troops, which his superiors told him was called operational research.

**Q7 (SA).** Why is central planning criticised here "on two counts"?
**A:** It's planning (a national economy is too complicated to write down) and it's central (getting the information and centralising it is the more wicked problem). The Soviet example: adding up different grades of wire, switches and fastenings.

## Chapter 3: Aliens Among Us

**Q1 (SA).** Why does Davies prefer "decision-making system" to "artificial intelligence"?
**A:** It drops the question of whether an organisation is "really" intelligent and asks only whether it makes decisions and whether they can be traced to identifiable humans. If not, attribute the output to the system ("Boeing did this").

**Q2 (SA).** How does cybernetics ground the principle of diminishing accountability?
**A:** The amount of individual accountability a system can support is inversely related to how far it must be treated as a black box. As organisations grow more complicated, accountability falls structurally. Ashby's example: four mutually connected parts have twenty circuits, and knowing all twenty still doesn't describe the whole.

**Q3 (SA).** How does the Fox News voting-machine case illustrate "not conspiracy, not cock-up"?
**A:** Internal emails show no scheme: individuals each reacted to immediate pain points (audience figures, share price, angry viewers) and assumed someone else would stop anything catastrophic. Yet it wasn't an accident: a long series of local decisions to stir up rage accumulated into a system-wide commitment to serve the angriest, least-informed viewers. That was a real decision that nobody made.

**Q4 (MC).** Boeing's technical pilot Mark Forkner is cited as an example of:
a) good management  b) scapegoating the one person whose paperwork is legible while corporate intent goes unproven  c) a whistleblower  d) the paperclip maximiser
**A:** b. It is "proof the accountability sink worked."

**Q5 (T/F).** Explainability research is mostly applied to favourable decisions. **A:** False. It is almost never applied to favourable decisions; it is funded to explain refusals. Ask who benefits from the box staying closed.

**Q6 (SA).** What is the "responsibility gap," and why does Davies say AI ethics is already business ethics?
**A:** The gap is the problem of assigning liability when an automated system causes harm. Corporations have operated above the black-box threshold for centuries, so AI's accountability problems already exist in non-computational form, under far weaker rights of explanation.

**Q7 (T/F).** Deep but partial expertise raises confidence in proportion to reliability. **A:** False. It raises confidence disproportionately to reliability (financiers' "it's different this time"; the State Department underperforming a parrot trained to say "don't start a war").

## Intermission: Computing Ponds and Rabbit Holes

**Q1 (SA).** What did Beer mean by "fabric"?
**A:** A substrate with the properties of a computer that doesn't depend on anyone specifying the algorithm ahead of time.

**Q2 (SA).** Why did Beer's computing pond fail?
**A:** The iron filings fed to the Daphnia (to make them respond to a magnetic field) left the water with a growing suspension of tiny permanent magnets, which collapsed any organisation that began to form. The medium's own dynamics destroyed the signal.

**Q3 (SA).** State the explainability paradox.
**A:** Explanation is demystifying (it shows how the conjurer put the rabbit in the hat), so "explainable" and "genuinely intelligent" pull in opposite directions.

**Q4 (SA).** What is the "pencil-and-paper test"?
**A:** Ask whether the result could in principle have been produced with pencil and paper given unlimited time. If yes, there is no magic, just iterated simple operations.

**Q5 (T/F).** Fabric-style methods are valuable mainly because they are fast and efficient. **A:** False. They are usually neither fastest nor most efficient; they earn their keep only if they can do things conventional methods cannot do at all.

## Chapter 4: How to Psychoanalyse a Non-human Intelligence

**Q1 (SA).** State Ashby's law of requisite variety.
**A:** Anything that aims to regulate a system needs at least as much variety as that system. A system can be stable only if every source of variability from the environment is matched by equal or greater variety in the regulator. Beer called it the cybernetic equivalent of Newton's laws of motion.

**Q2 (SA).** What is variety, and why isn't it intrinsic?
**A:** The number of states a system can be in. It is a property of how you choose to describe the system, so you must choose your black boxes and say which states you count.

**Q3 (MC).** The practical way to apply the law, per Davies, is to:
a) count bits exactly  b) compare whether regulator variety is greater or less than input variety  c) hire more staff  d) ignore it
**A:** b. "We all know what someone looks like when they're overloaded."

**Q4 (SA).** Name the moves of variety engineering and give an example of each.
**A:** Attenuation (continuous temperature becomes "too hot/too cold" for a thermostat); amplification (build regulatory subsystems using another source of variety, e.g. a thermostat per cage across a hundred cages); regulation by veto (Ashby's homeostat: sensors veto unacceptable states until all are satisfied); splitting the problem (regulate temperature apart from food and water, but only if variables are truly separable; smashing a Rubik's Cube into 26 sub-cubes solves nothing).

**Q5 (SA).** Why is an accountability sink recast here as a "solution as much as a problem"?
**A:** Building cause-and-effect subsystems so managers don't decide case by case is variety amplification; unappealability is its side effect.

**Q6 (T/F).** You can literally amplify variety. **A:** False. That would breach the second law; you do the stereo-amplifier trick by building regulatory subsystems inside the wider regulator.

**Q7 (SA).** What are ataxia and purpose tremor?
**A:** Wiener's two failure modes in the automated gunsight: feedback too weak (the gun trails its target) and too strong (it oscillates and overcorrects). Named after cerebellar injury symptoms; the moment control engineering became cybernetics.

## Chapter 5: Cybernetics Without Diagrams

**Q1 (SA).** Name the five systems of the Viable System Model and the function of each.
**A:** 1 Operations (makes change in the world); 2 Regulation (prevents clashes: timetables, quartermaster); 3 Integration/optimisation (coordinates the System 1s, strikes and polices resource bargains, handles residual variety); 4 Intelligence (the "there and then", watching the environment not yet in contact with operations); 5 Philosophy/identity (balances 3 against 4 by fixing what kind of organisation this is).

**Q2 (SA).** How can you tell System 2 from System 3?
**A:** System 2 is the management everyone agrees is necessary; System 3 is the management people complain about. System 3 managers often pretend to be System 2.

**Q3 (SA).** What are the two failure modes of management by exception?
**A:** Micromanagement (System 3 fights System 1's variety single-handed, gets bloated, neglects coordination) and excessive delegation (no bargains or planning; conflicts settled by politics and hoarding). System 3 should be sized to the residual variety.

**Q4 (T/F).** Forecasting is a form of System 4. **A:** False. Forecasts are the here-and-now in another form; a real System 4 looks at what isn't yet relevant.

**Q5 (MC).** An algedonic signal is best described as:
a) a quarterly report  b) a channel letting any level signal directly to the top that something threatens catastrophic consequences  c) a type of resource bargain  d) a System 2 timetable
**A:** b. Modelled on a train's emergency brake; resource the channel and the translation capacity at the receiving end. Frequent red-handle signals indicate missing or dysfunctional systems further down.

**Q6 (SA).** Distinguish information from data.
**A:** Variety counts as information only if it has a causal role in decision-making, arriving in time and in a form the receiver can act on. Otherwise it is data sitting on disk.

**Q7 (SA).** How does the chapter read the 2008 crisis through the VSM?
**A:** Central banks (a System 1 of government with independence as its resource bargain) named the calm ("Great Moderation," "NICE times," "Goldilocks economy") while a debt bubble built. Their "forecasting" was here-and-now extended, so System 4 was weak; 2006-07 warnings stayed data; only Lehman's bankruptcy acted as an algedonic squeal. The root was System 5: an identity that defined certain information as none of their business.

## Intermission: Decerebrate Cats

**Q1 (SA).** What does a decerebrate cat illustrate?
**A:** An animal with its higher brain severed can still walk, right itself, eat and groom, but cannot generate its own responses to change. An organisation that has hollowed out Systems 4 and 5 likewise still runs operations but cannot act purposively.

**Q2 (SA).** What warning sign marks a decerebrate organisation?
**A:** Attention runs only to the immediate surroundings (this quarter's revenue, current staffing level) and planning capacity has disappeared.

**Q3 (T/F).** Smooth day-to-day operations are good evidence of adaptive health. **A:** False. The decerebrate cat grooms itself; operational competence says nothing about adaptive capacity.

**Q4 (SA).** What are the only two outcomes when a shock hits a decerebrate organisation?
**A:** It finds resources from somewhere to re-establish intelligence and identity, or it collapses. The crisis is deferred, never avoided.

**Q5 (SA).** What was Beer's stock example, and what is Davies' practical corollary?
**A:** A university whose operations run smoothly while the capacity to decide what it's for has gone. Corollary: if you can see a company actively decerebrating itself, update your resume and call recruiters.

## Chapter 6: Economics and How It Got That Way

**Q1 (SA).** What are the steps of the Ricardian Vice?
**A:** (a) Model a feature of the economy, stripping away nearly all complexity; (b) make strong, often questionable simplifying assumptions; (c) derive the conclusion; (d) act as if it has been proved about the real world.

**Q2 (SA).** Walk through Ricardo's England/Portugal example.
**A:** Cloth costs 100 labour-hours in England and 90 in Portugal; wine costs 120 in England and 80 in Portugal. Portugal is absolutely better at both, but relative prices differ (a bolt of cloth is worth nine-eighths of a barrel of wine in Portugal, five-sixths in England), so Portugal gains by making only wine and trading. The arithmetic is airtight; the vice is the leap from it to claims about real international trade.

**Q3 (MC).** Which is NOT one of the four conditions to check before trusting an optimisation model?
a) commodity good  b) observable marginal cost curve  c) independent spot markets  d) high consumer brand loyalty
**A:** d. The fourth is negligible strategic interaction.

**Q4 (SA).** In what sense is economics a "variety-engineering technology" and "identity-creating function"?
**A:** It attenuates society to price and quantity so it can always give a definite answer, and it does System 5's job for policymakers by telling rulers what they don't have to think about (e.g. cost-benefit analysis lets whole approaches be rejected without consideration).

**Q5 (T/F).** Observing a market outcome licenses the inference that it is optimal. **A:** False. None of the four sources (Smith, Hayek, equilibrium proofs, market efficiency) licenses that, and the inference is "a huge accountability sink."

**Q6 (SA).** Why can't safety information go through cost-benefit analysis?
**A:** "This aircraft has a faulty stall sensor which might cause it to crash" is fundamentally not a statement about expected costs, however comfortable you are pricing a life; it needs a separate channel.

**Q7 (SA).** What do "homo economicus" and the "representative individual" do?
**A:** Homo economicus cares only about price and quantity and is perfectly informed and able to read other agents' minds. The representative individual/firm assumes identical preferences or aggregates all producers into one, dodging strategic interaction.

## Chapter 7: If You're So Rich, Why Aren't You Smart?

**Q1 (SA).** Distinguish management accounting from financial reporting.
**A:** Management accounting measures process efficiency and allocates costs to specific decisions. Financial reporting summarises the whole enterprise for financiers on standard periods and conventions, and so attenuates variety.

**Q2 (SA).** State the attributability test.
**A:** The extent to which a cost can be attributed to an output is exactly the extent to which it could be avoided if you weren't producing it. Also, allocating costs a manager cannot influence is pure harm.

**Q3 (SA).** What is business hallucination?
**A:** A firm acting on a picture of itself generated by reporting conventions rather than by its actual production process.

**Q4 (SA).** Summarise the skincare factory example.
**A:** Two lines from essentially the same cream: bulk barrels for construction workers, and small beauty tubs at ten times the price. Marketing is treated as a period cost, so the luxury line looks more profitable while the bulk line absorbs a disproportionate share of fixed packaging cost allocated by direct labour. Consultants compare in-house manufacturing (carrying beauty overheads) with a cheap external supplier; manufacturing is sold; the marketing and research costs don't vanish; and two years later consultants are hired to explain the slump.

**Q5 (MC).** The fast-food "chicken efficiency" story shows:
a) the danger of measuring the wrong efficiency  b) the value of cost allocation  c) the benefits of consultants  d) a successful bargain
**A:** a. Managers stopped frying an hour before closing, lost sales, and hit 100 per cent.

**Q6 (T/F).** Consultants can supply the managerial class with its identity function. **A:** False. Telling clients what they want to hear is better for repeat business, so consultancy can never supply it; their real product should be a repaired feedback loop. They also work as accountability sinks (professional scapegoats).

**Q7 (SA).** How do "Ricardians win arguments"?
**A:** By collecting the numbers: whoever designs data collection decides what is measured and ignored. The only reliable counters are data collected for a different purpose or an internal inconsistency in their model. (A single death can be encoded as liver failure, mercury poisoning or murder.)

## Intermission: Meanwhile, in Chile

**Q1 (SA).** Name Cybersyn's components and their role.
**A:** Cybernet (several hundred repurposed telex machines carrying production data); Cyberstride (statistical software judging whether data fell in the normal range and classifying departures as blip, trend change or new normal); the Operations Room (about a dozen managers with wall displays negotiating resource bargains); Checo (an economic simulator, planned but never delivered).

**Q2 (SA).** Map Cybersyn onto the VSM.
**A:** Operations Room = System 3; Cybernet = partial System 2; Cyberstride = variety engineering matching the room to data inflow; Checo = intended System 4.

**Q3 (T/F).** Cybersyn was an attempt at computerised central planning of the economy. **A:** False. It was a management-by-exceptions system built on the VSM, and management cybernetics already shows a centrally planned economy cannot meet requisite variety.

**Q4 (SA).** Which part proved most practically useful?
**A:** Cybernet: during a trucking strike it let the government track deliveries and secure essential supplies.

**Q5 (SA).** What does "out of scale" mean here?
**A:** The control system's channel capacity plainly cannot match the variety it regulates; a few hundred telex machines against an industrial economy fails the laugh test.

**Q6 (SA).** Name two further problems with Cybersyn.
**A:** Installing it provoked reaction from operations (building a regulator changes the regulated system), and interventors in nationalised factories passing on central orders undercut the devolved-autonomy logic. Chile also nationalised faster than it could manage, without an overall industrial plan.

## Chapter 8: Enter Friedman

**Q1 (SA).** What are the three moves of the Friedman doctrine?
**A:** (1) Look through the corporation to individuals; (2) replace actual shareholders with an abstract "representative shareholder" black box that cares only about profits; (3) invite the executive to split his moral self into judgements "as a person in his own right" and decisions "in his capacity as a businessman."

**Q2 (SA).** Why does Davies call the doctrine a "personal accountability sink"?
**A:** It lets managers attribute bad consequences and lost independence to a separate work-self bound by one simple principle.

**Q3 (SA).** Why is debt a "variety attenuator" for lenders?
**A:** The lender need only answer two questions (is the rate attractive compensation for time and risk; will I be paid back?), and so can make far more investments than they could supervise. With heavy debt, the payment signal swamps all other information.

**Q4 (SA).** Describe the leveraged buyout mechanism.
**A:** Put up roughly 10 per cent of your own money, borrow 90 per cent, then make the acquired company borrow to repay your creditors. Debt forces focus on cash; limited liability caps the buyer's loss at the initial 10 per cent; risk shifts to workers, suppliers, customers and managers.

**Q5 (T/F).** Courts have found a legal "fiduciary duty to maximise shareholder value." **A:** False. Courts have never found a duty to run a company one way rather than another; the phrase is legal jargon used outside its legal context, moral exhortation in sonorous tones.

**Q6 (MC).** "Financialisation," per Davies, is best understood as:
a) a parasite on the real economy  b) the growing importance of financial aspects of the whole system, since finance is how resource bargains are struck  c) a conspiracy  d) a tax policy
**A:** b.

**Q7 (SA).** What did Jensen (with Meckling) contribute?
**A:** A rigorous mathematical footing for the doctrine: "managerial behaviour" (slacking, pursuing social goals with shareholders' money), "agency costs" (shareholders' difficulty checking managers), and "ownership structure" as the fix (debt and stock options to make managers act as if a personal owner were watching).

**Q8 (SA).** What is "pre-emptive self-buyout"?
**A:** Companies behaved as if already under a leveraged buyout to avoid becoming targets, spreading the pathology without the transaction.

## Chapter 9: The Morbid Symptoms

**Q1 (SA).** What is "reversing the polarity"?
**A:** Following Jerome Levy, the investing class exists to serve the working class by insuring them against loss. The neoliberal era ran this backwards, with unemployment itself as the variety attenuator, so the working class insured the corporate sector against the business cycle. It produced the Great Moderation.

**Q2 (SA).** Why does cutting middle management and outsourcing destroy information?
**A:** Middle managers were the processing (translation/transduction) capacity at communication boundaries. Outsourcing replaces internal boundaries with contracts that carry essentially two states ("going as anticipated" or "it's stopped working"). Because the channel mostly says OK, spare bandwidth for "conditions are changing" always looks cuttable.

**Q3 (SA).** What does "the one-bit channel and the scream" mean for populism?
**A:** Where the population's channels carry very few bits, the only message possible is "Help! the Current State of Affairs is Intolerable to me." Respond to the fact of the scream, not its content; the movement's existence is the signal.

**Q4 (MC).** In the GE example, what was done to avoid spoiling future smooth growth after a one-off $2bn gain?
a) paid special dividends  b) closed factories and made over 1,000 people redundant  c) raised prices  d) acquired a rival
**A:** b.

**Q5 (T/F).** Marmot's Whitehall studies found that civil service grade predicted mortality independent of income, smoking and family history. **A:** True. This is the "status syndrome"; being out of control is physiologically harmful.

**Q6 (SA).** Why do short-term targets privilege costs over revenues?
**A:** Management controls costs within a quarter; revenue requires finding a customer, winning the order, making the product and getting paid, which is impossible in a quarter.

**Q7 (SA).** What was the "blind spot in the control parameters" before the financial crisis?
**A:** Only government deficits were monitored; private-sector debt sat outside the control system and was seen as positive by the post-Friedman corporate control system.

**Q8 (SA).** What is "el pueblo"?
**A:** Allende's name for the System 5 box: the last-resort absorber of information, which hardly ever does anything and has no postal address; even in a dictatorship there is a collective veto if things become intolerable.

## Chapter 10: What Is to Be Done?

**Q1 (SA).** What is Davies' single private-equity reform, and why?
**A:** Any entity taking control of an operating company should guarantee its debts. It closes the accountability sink (limited liability) that lets buyers take on debt and extract cash while bad news cannot travel upstream. Don't try to regulate "excessive" borrowing; requisite variety says that is doomed.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the three-step argument against economists in charge?
**A:** (1) Any system set up to maximise a single objective can go bonkers. (2) Every maximiser needs a higher-level system watching over it. (3) So the economists can't be in charge, not as they currently are.

**Q3 (MC).** Which describes "viability over maximisation"?
a) Firms should maximise long-run profit  b) Corporations have no motives; what looks like a profit motive is an imbalance between here-and-now and there-and-then, and viable systems seek stability  c) Firms should maximise employment  d) Firms should be nationalised
**A:** b.

**Q4 (SA).** What do Galbraith and Simon contribute?
**A:** Galbraith's technostructure (a managerial class across business, government, military and media working to maintain stability, whose executives mainly handle uncertainty) and Simon's behavioural theory of the firm (profits are a constraint, not a goal to be maximised). They described what was in front of them, and suggest the fix is changing the terms of the battle between capital and the managerial class.

**Q5 (T/F).** Davies says we should insist that an identifiable human is always available to blame for automated decisions. **A:** False. Important decisions will be made by actual robots; repair the feedback link instead ("I blame the system" becomes literal).

**Q6 (SA).** What is the proposed role of AI in communication?
**A:** A variety amplifier for two-way communication: summarising large text up to a smaller decision-making system and expanding short instructions down, rather than just making existing structures run faster.

**Q7 (SA).** What does "Only cybernetic problems are soluble" mean?
**A:** If a problem is organisational, its complexity comes from the people in the organisation, so they have enough variety to match it; the problem can't be bigger than the people whose misdirected efforts created it.

# Final Exam (10 questions)

**Q1 (SA).** What is an accountability sink, and what single question tests for one?
**A:** A combination of a powerless human interface and an unappealable policy, so there is nobody to blame. Test: is there any information the affected person could supply that would change the outcome?

**Q2 (SA).** State Ashby's law and the quick way to apply it.
**A:** Only variety can absorb variety; a regulator needs at least as much variety as what it regulates. Quick check: compare whether regulator variety is greater or less than input variety, rather than counting bits.

**Q3 (MC).** Which pairing is correct?
a) System 4 - regulation  b) System 3 - intelligence  c) System 5 - philosophy/identity, balancing 3 against 4  d) System 2 - identity
**A:** c.

**Q4 (T/F).** POSIWID is a claim about individuals' bad intentions. **A:** False. It says nothing about the intentions of people in the system; it's about reading purpose from output and a description of how System 5 preserves viability.

**Q5 (SA).** Give the Ricardian Vice and the four-condition check in one answer.
**A:** Prove something in a stripped-down model and act as if proved about the world. Before trusting an optimisation model, check: commodity good, observable marginal cost curve, independent spot markets, negligible strategic interaction.

**Q6 (SA).** How do decerebration and outsourcing connect across Chapters 5i and 9?
**A:** Systems 4 and 5 produce no directly measurable output, so they are easy to cut; thinning middle management removes translation capacity at boundaries, and outsourced contracts carry only "OK" or "stopped." The result is an organisation that functions until the environment changes structurally.

**Q7 (MC).** The attributability test says a cost is attributable:
a) in proportion to direct labour  b) exactly to the extent it would be avoided by not producing the output  c) if the accountant says so  d) if it is a period cost
**A:** b.

**Q8 (SA).** Trace how Friedman's doctrine, debt and the LBO combine, and what the effect is on the working class (Ch 8-9).
**A:** The doctrine supplies a single maximising goal and a personal accountability sink; debt (and the LBO's 10/90 structure) swamps all signals but cash and shifts risk onto workers, suppliers, customers and managers; with corporate shock-absorbing capacity cut, the polarity reverses and the working class absorbs volatility, with the population's only channel a one-bit scream.

**Q9 (SA).** What does Davies prescribe for maximising systems and for the "decided-upon"?
**A:** Pair every maximiser with a higher-level supervisor, keep the top level of any autonomous system a non-maximiser, and give the decided-upon a red handle to signal intolerability. Set survival conditions rather than maximisation targets.

**Q10 (SA).** Why is the Cybersyn story relevant to the VSM and to requisite variety?
**A:** Cybersyn was a management-by-exceptions design (Operations Room = System 3, Cybernet = partial System 2, Cyberstride = variety engineering, Checo = intended System 4) that was badly out of scale to the Chilean economy; it also provoked reaction, and it was ended by the 1973 coup before it could fail informatively.
