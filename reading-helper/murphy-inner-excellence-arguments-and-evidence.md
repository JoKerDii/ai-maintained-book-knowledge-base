# PART 2: Chapter by chapter

## Chapter 0: Preface, Presuppositions and Introduction (The Foundations)

**Central argument.** The pursuit of extraordinary performance and the pursuit of the best possible life are the same path; both run through the heart. The biggest obstacle on that path is self-centeredness. The order matters: heart first, performance second. Murphy supplies a lens (ten presuppositions) and a performance model (BFF) that the rest of the book builds on.

**Full stories.**

*Bunpachiro the samurai.* Murphy's great-great-grandfather, Bunpachiro, was a samurai whose identity was built on a role. When the role was lost, he fell into alcoholism. The notes use him as a mental model: build identity on something no circumstance can remove, not on a role, uniform or title that can be taken away.

*Ryan Dodd.* After a skull fracture, Ryan Dodd trained the same volume as before it. What differed, and what produced a world record, was how he saw the world. The lesson in the notes: the change that matters is the lens, not the training load.

*The lens.* Everyone wears glasses given by family, culture and experience; the extraordinary share a similar, uncommon lens. (A framework rather than a story, but it frames the ten presuppositions.)

**Evidence.** None in the form of studies. The chapter relies on Murphy's coaching experience and the two cases above. (Caveat: they are anecdotes.)

**Key terms.**
- *Quality of Life* has three elements: inner world (thoughts, feelings, beliefs, desires), frame of reference (mindset), relationships.
- *Quality of Performance = BFF:* **Belief** (who you are / what is possible), **Focus** (fully engaged heart-mind-body in the moment), **Freedom** (play like a kid, curious, unafraid of others' opinions). Later mapped: love gives freedom, wisdom gives belief, courage gives focus.
- *Inner Excellence mindset:* "I compete to raise the level of excellence in my life, to learn and grow, in order to raise it in others."
- *The Ten Presuppositions:* (1) every circumstance and person is here to teach and help you; you were created for glory (infinite inherent worth); (2) your life reflects your beliefs, the control panel / subconscious thermostat; the primary skill is how to believe; (3) self-centeredness is the root cause of fear (then self-consciousness, overanalysis, self-rejection); (4) everyone shares the same deep needs, to be fully known and fully loved; (5) everyone does the best they can with what is in their heart, and hurtful behavior reflects lack of love/joy/peace seen through fear or pain; (6) the map is not the territory (Magritte's pipe); (7) you are not your mind; your greatest freedom is where you place your thoughts; (8) the problem is not the problem; the problem is how you are thinking about it; (9) there is no failure, only feedback; (10) the person with the most control of their inner world has the most power, and mastering the ego is the great challenge in every competition. Drop: "I am my thoughts," "my value = my results," "the best were born that way."
- *World's mindset vs. Inner Excellence mindset:* winning is everything / inner strength and growth last longer; you are your results / results are an inconsistent measure (you can play well and lose); opponent = enemy / opponent = partner in the dance; failure not an option / failure is the key to growth; fierce competitors get mad / the best develop emotional control.
- *Fullness of life (zoe):* the real object of every ambition, what you think the trophy or house will make you feel.
- *Symbols vs. substance:* chasing money, status, numbers or approval is chasing the symbol of the dream.
- *Finite vs. infinite game:* most play a zero-sum finite game; life is an infinite one of connection and growth.

**So what.** Memorize the presuppositions and use them as the default lens when something goes wrong. Ask "which game have I been playing, finite comparison or infinite growth?" Do not pursue success in order to be happy (backwards); develop the inner world and results follow. Do not place identity in what you cannot control. Presupposition 8 echoes Nguyen's *Don't Believe Everything You Think* from a Three Principles angle.

## Chapter 1: Maslow and the Maserati (The Pursuit of More)

**Central argument.** Real freedom is costly: it means walking the narrow path of facing fears and connecting with your true self, not the wide road of comfort and symbols of success. Western culture's "affluenza virus" swaps the real dream (feeling fully alive) for its symbols.

**Full stories.**

*Costa Rica.* Murphy observed in Costa Rica that a dentist host, after a tow, invites the tow-truck driver in for dinner, and becomes visibly excited when the driver points out his hometown on a map. Lawyers, cab drivers and shop owners socially level with each other. These were people who "needed little and appreciated much" and seemed far happier than Murphy's own culture. The notes recommend it as a contrast case for a PALMS-free social world.

*Murphy's own Cubs experience.* Murphy built identity on the uniform; batting average became his self-worth, and his life was a roller coaster. It is the personal case for the anti-pattern "identity in the uniform/title."

*Joe Ehrmann (ladder on the wrong building).* Ehrmann had outward NFL success and inner emptiness: the ladder was against the wrong building. He redefined masculinity as relationships plus a cause bigger than self.

*David Foster Wallace (everybody worships).* The only choice is what you worship; worship money, body, power or intellect and "it eats you alive."

*Dean Smith and Krzyzewski.* Both treated winning as a by-product of process; defining success by what you cannot control undermines the process.

*Michelangelo's David.* The true self is revealed by removing what is not you, not by adding.

**Evidence.** Anecdotal and quoted. The notes cite no studies in this chapter. Maslow's self-actualizer characteristics (relabeled by Murphy as "selfless-actualizers") serve as the audit list.

**Key terms.**
- *Two Paths:* the wide road (praise, admiration, less risk, instant gratification) versus the narrow road (difficult, rocky, leading to deep contentment, joy, confidence and an undivided heart).
- *Selfless-actualizers, nine characteristics:* total absorption; personal growth (the means is the end); self-awareness; Gemeinschaft (community); fresh gratitude; authenticity / resistance to enculturation; comfort in solitude; purpose beyond self; lack of ego defenses. Use as a self-audit.
- *Affluenza virus, PALMS:* Possessions, Achievements, Looks, Money, Status. Not wanting them, but trusting identity in them. The engine is "more than others."
- *Three worship questions:* What do I dream about? What do I worry about? What do I get upset about? Your stability equals the stability of the answer.
- *Ordered loves (Augustine):* we are shaped by what we love; discontent comes from loves out of order; love most what is most powerful.
- *Three definitions of "best"* (previewed; developed in Ch 4): *the* best, *your* best, your *best shot*.
- *Entitlement:* symptom of infection, deep disturbance when reputation or status is threatened.
- *Winning as imposter:* you can win without being good; a personal best in a loss may be the better race.
- *Thoreau's price:* the price of anything is the amount of life you exchange for it.
- *Lollipop vs. candy store:* medals and CEO titles are lollipops; fullness of life is the store.

**So what.** Run the three questions on yourself this week. List what PALMS you lean on for identity. Watch for "I get hurt when my status is touched" as an infection sign. Treat winning as part solution, part problem. Anti-patterns: obsession with winning (makes you fear losing and steals joy), identity in the uniform, chasing feelings to feel better about yourself (slave to impulses).

## Chapter 2: Where the Wild Things Are (Pride, Fear and the Center of the Universe)

**Central argument.** Fear of failure is the chief obstacle to freedom and performance, and its root cause is self-centeredness: the natural, hard-wired preoccupation with self and attachment to the past that breeds self-consciousness, feeds the ego and ends in self-rejection. Fear is the symptom; self-centeredness is the disease.

**Full stories.**

*The free throw.* The notes locate the moment it starts at a free throw: the instant "I've gotta make this" replaces the routine. Heart is on one side, ego on the other. The routine gives way to self-concern about the result.

*The two colleagues.* Two colleagues sit through the same bad meeting. The self-centered one concludes "I'm a poor speaker, they're being nice, they'll gossip." The self-aware one notices the well-prepared colleagues did better, accepts the compliment, and prepares more. Same event, different lens. The lesson: the interpretation, not the event, drives the outcome.

*Infant default.* Self-centeredness is a default setting since infancy: cries are rewarded with food. It is not a moral failing.

*Me, Myself and I (exercise).* At a social event with strangers, go the whole evening without saying anything that puts you in a favorable light, without talking about yourself except to answer direct questions, and without using "me," "myself" or "I." The purpose is to expose how hard it is to be non-self-occupied and how acceptance-driven the ego is. Pair it with the 3x5-card day: mark every time your mind is in the past or future.

**Evidence.** Largely conceptual. The notes cite C.S. Lewis (pride), Keller (competitiveness at the heart of pride), Dean Smith (leaders must be able to handle losing) and Dweck (fixed mindset). No datasets in the notes. Caveat: no experimental results are given for the exercises.

**Key terms.**
- *The Path to Fear (Fig. 2.1):* self-centeredness, attachment to past failures, self-consciousness, ego amplified, comparison (via PALMS), pride and fear; doubts escalate from external ("I won't achieve it") to personal ("I'm not able") to identity ("I'm a failure").
- *Attachment Cascade:* identity in uncontrollable goals, attachment to circumstances (to monitor progress), attachment to thoughts, frustration when what "has to" happen does not.
- *Self-centeredness vs. self-awareness:* nearsightedness vs. observing thoughts unattached and seeing the bigger picture; setbacks as feedback.
- *Pride (Lewis):* "unsmiling concentration on the self"; pleasure only from having more than the next person.
- *Fear:* self-focused, future-oriented, projects past failures forward, separates and isolates; love connects and integrates.
- *Ego:* always comparing, always threatened, never satisfied; relies on external validation; cannot handle losing.
- *Fixed mindset (Dweck):* the ego's home; a failure can define you permanently; trying hard and failing is its worst fear.

**So what.** Whenever anxiety appears, trace it back along the Path to Fear. Ask "was it *me* or something *mine* that was threatened?" Treat thoughts as a spoiled child running the house: the mind needs training, daily renewal and rest like the body. Anti-patterns: comparing to feel better, placing security in what you cannot control, letting doubts go unchecked (nobody can make you feel inferior unless your own mind thinks, accepts and affirms it). Every morning you choose: step away from self-centeredness or slide toward insecurity by default.

## Chapter 3: The Greatest Opponents You'll Ever Face (The Critic, Monkey Mind and Trickster)

**Central argument.** Three named adversaries in your own mind sabotage focus and confidence: the Critic (judges and reacts), the Monkey Mind (clutters and overanalyzes) and the Trickster (lies and accuses). Each gains power from attachment to results. The first defense is to separate circumstances from thoughts.

**Full stories.**

*The lost sale.* You lose a big sale. Disappointment is natural. But because you were attached, you slide to devastated. The Critic says "this is really bad." The Trickster recalls every other failure and projects more into the future. Now you do not feel that you failed; you feel that you *are* a failure (D-slide: disappointed, devastated, defeated, defective). Intervention: write the circumstances (the sale went to a competitor; here are the facts) in one column and the thoughts in another. Nobody can argue with column one; only then consider the solution.

*The pandemic.* Circumstance: schools closed, events cancelled, people ill. Thoughts: "everything's falling apart, it's hopeless." Truth: life is different; we will all die; the real question is whether we will fully live; this might be the best season to rethink your path. The antidote presuppositions: "All things are here to teach me and help me" and "The problem is not the problem, it's how I'm thinking about it."

*Charlie Maher's baseball point.* "What if...?" thoughts fuse you with language and create a reality that is not there. The real reality is just a hitter, a catcher and runners.

**Evidence.** Expert-framework based. Dr. David Coppel's D-slide; Cal Botterill's observation that overanalysis is the number one obstacle for performers and caring too much is number two; Nouwen's claim that self-rejection is the greatest trap in life (not success, popularity or power); Charlie Maher's "fusing with language." No numbers or studies beyond the ranking above.

**Key terms.**
- *The Critic:* delivers a negative verdict on a thing, circumstance or person (often yourself) and reacts emotionally. Attached to circumstances because the ego reads every event as progress toward or away from the goal. *Judge* = negative verdict; *discern* = use wisdom to choose the best course. Judgment and curiosity cannot coexist; creativity dies with curiosity.
- *The Monkey Mind:* the endless stream of mostly self-concerned, unproductive thoughts. Thrives in a heart with no single central purpose; crowds out the space needed to dream.
- *The Trickster:* deception and accusation; sounds logical because it cites real failures. Core message: your worth depends entirely on your results. Lines: "you're a fraud," "there's not enough to go around," "you'll get rejected," "who are you to be great?" Tricks you into settling for lesser goals (the lollipop). The Great Trick is self-deception.
- *D-slide:* Disappointed, Devastated, Defeated, Defective.
- *Circumstances vs. thoughts vs. truth:* circumstances are facts free of opinion; thoughts are opinions with emotion; thoughts are not facts.
- *Fusing with language.*
- *Self-rejection (Nouwen).* *Living in reaction.* *Adversity as training.*
- Mental model: courtroom (Critic), noisy market (Monkey Mind), con artist with your files (Trickster).

**So what.** Name the voice. Split facts from opinions before solving. Set an alarm asking "Where's your mind right now?" and reset with two long breaths, exhaling longer than inhaling. Anti-patterns: judging every circumstance as on or off track, letting Trickster lies linger (they attract more negative thoughts and become beliefs), arguing for your limitations (the Trickster's win condition), perfectionism after success (high expectations, then belittling yourself on failure, blind to the lesson). Parallel: Nguyen on "thoughts vs. thinking."

## Chapter 4: The Daring and the Twilight (Three Pillars of Extraordinary Performance)

**Central argument.** Fear is overcome by the three most powerful resources, love, wisdom and courage, which become three pillars: lead with your heart, expand your vision, be fully present. Together they produce resonance, and pursuing them directly is self-mastery. This is the book's core framework.

**Full stories.**

*Lewis Pugh's North Pole swim (2007).* Pugh planned a swim at the North Pole. Days before, two test swims failed: cells in his hands burst, and he managed 600 m at most in warmer water. Scientists told him it was lethal; his doctor's hands shook. Then he did it. The notes stress that his fear did not change between the failures and the success. What changed was: (a) connection to a purpose beyond himself (global warming, and the coral reefs he had seen); (b) his coach planted one of 29 teammates' national flags every 100 m, telling him to think only about the Norwegians until the Norwegian flag, then only the Swedes, and never to think about the kilometer. Fear turned to aggression the moment he stepped on the ice. Lesson: purpose plus chunking plus team is the recipe for any daunting task.

*Georges St-Pierre on fear.* Fear never disappears; it gets worse. What changes is acceptance ("it's a suffering process I go through before a fight").

*Dawn Staley.* The gold medal is the goal; playing to win against the best is the dream.

*Unconditional love in traffic.* Let someone in with no attachment to a thank-you wave; that is stepping into your power.

*Why people risk death.* The notes give three reasons: purpose beyond self; to feel alive at full concentration; lack of self-concern.

**Evidence.** Case and expert testimony. The only number-heavy case is Pugh (600 m, 29 flags, every 100 m, 2007). The notes give a figure for humility: "~90% of anything good was given to you" (as part of the wisdom/humility pillar). Caveat: not a study.

**Key terms.**
- *Three Pillars:* **Love** (lead with your heart; freedom; passion): clarify how you want to feel and who you are meant to become; love unconditionally, the only fearless kind. **Wisdom** (expand your vision; belief; purpose): see beyond yourself, find a purpose beyond your goals; humility (an accurate view of self), then gratitude, then confidence. **Courage** (be fully present; focus; poise): fully engaged with a clear mind and unburdened heart; no needs or self-concern.
- *Zoe:* absolute fullness of life; the full expression of love, wisdom, courage; selfless-actualization.
- *Self-Mastery = mastering the ego:* **Unembarrassable**, **Unoffendable** (self-control, not self-protection), **Unirritatable**. Ego reality-checks: worried what people think; nervous about embarrassment; more concerned with being right than learning; obsessed with winning over experiencing; angry when humiliated. Mastery signs: more space in your rhythm; less caught up in mind; less attached to results; more concerned with empowering others; listen more than speak; strict daily routines.
- *Dreams vs. goals:* goals are external outcomes (win, hit .300, become CEO); dreams are feelings.
- *Resonance:* frequencies aligning; effortless excitement; results are the by-product.
- *Fearless Four:* (1) give my best, 100% of what I have today; (2) be present; (3) be grateful; (4) focus on my routines and only what I can control.
- *Three types of best (Frosty Westering):* *the* best (by-product, uncontrollable), *your* best (hard to know), your *best shot* (always available). *Double win:* bringing out the best in teammates and yourself.
- *Life Diamond (Fig. 4.2):* daily choice, self-centered path down toward fear vs. purpose-filled path up toward zoe.
- *Compete* (Latin *competere*): to seek together, strive in common.
- *Zero-sum lens:* clinging to life; letting go reveals "the whole pie... and cheesecake."
- *The bigger your goals, the smaller your peer group.*

**So what.** Review the Fearless Four every day: giving 100% of today's 40% is success. Replace "have to win" with "get to share." Be meticulous about routines but never attached to them: an interrupted routine "is how it's supposed to be today." Avoiding fear limits life; greatness comes from facing it and feeling whatever comes up. Two components of fearlessness: purpose beyond self plus unconditional love. Anti-pattern: outcome-oriented focus.

## Chapter 5: Code of the Samurai (How a Group of Warriors Mastered Their Ego)

**Central argument.** The samurai were fearless because they prepared to die each day: love of honor and service over self. Self-mastery is surrendering self-concern for a greater purpose, embracing suffering and dropping the attachments that hold you. Exchange the little lollipop (winning) for the whole candy store (fullness of life).

**Full stories.**

*Murphy's Scenario A vs. B.* In Scenario A, Murphy marched in the Sydney 2000 Opening Ceremony minutes after a talk from Nelson Mandela; the feeling was elation on top of anxiety about an uncertain life. In Scenario B, he sat broke and in debt in a friend's tiny Los Angeles apartment while the friend played a song of gratitude on guitar; the feeling was a deep sense of being fully known and fully loved. Extraordinary circumstances produced happiness over anxiety; nothing circumstances produced joy. Use it to test which one you are optimizing for.

*Paul Kalanithi.* "Wouldn't it be great if it [death] were more painful?" The pain of loss measures the size of the gift; do not refuse gifts for fear of losing them.

*Apolo Ohno.* At 3:30 a.m. in a parking lot with a miner's lamp: "I'm willing to do what nobody else is willing to do"; "the conversation doesn't end when you fail; that's when it starts."

*UBC golf team.* Their mantra: "The score is for the fans; we play for the love of the game." They lost when attached and won two national titles when they let go.

*Wim Hof.* Focus *on* the cold rather than ignore it.

*The Four Horses (Suzuki).* The best horse runs before the whip's shadow; the worst only when pain reaches the marrow; yet the "clever" often plateau while those who suffered learn deepest. Victory is the most dangerous position; failure is dangerous too.

*12-step groups.* The illness in every group is self-centeredness; the cure is dying to the old nature, self-awareness, surrendering control, vision beyond self (Serenity Prayer).

**Evidence.** Historical and testimonial. No studies are cited in these notes. The "Mastery vs. Ego" table is Murphy's synthesis (reconstructed in the notes).

**Key terms.**
- *Samurai Paradox:* learn to fight to the death by focusing on love (honor, service) more than self; "die anew every morning"; if you cling to your life you lose it.
- *Love vs. fear as two forces:* self-expression vs. self-protection. Staying on love: fuel your heart daily with the words and stories of those who lived in love; return via presuppositions and reset tools.
- *Mastery vs. ego (examples):* selfless / self-centered; strong sense of self / self-conscious; grateful / entitled; unembarrassable / easily embarrassed; compassionate / judgmental; always learning / always comparing; process / outcome; best shot / win-or-lose; willing to look foolish / afraid of humiliation; fully present / past-and-future; able to suffer / comfort-seeking; feeling of control / controlled by results; embrace opponent / fear opponent; humble / arrogant; seeks growth / seeks validation; sees beauty first / sees flaws first; walks by faith / walks by sight; centered / scattered; few needs / needy; purpose beyond goals / no real purpose; transcends circumstances / attached to them; stable / unpredictable.
- *Beauty Before Bed (5 steps):* review the day; recall three small, specific beautiful moments; reprogram mistakes then let go; preview tomorrow's routines; visualize tomorrow's feelings. Never carry worries to bed.
- *Happiness vs. joy:* happiness is temporary and circumstance-bound; joy is deep, independent, possible in suffering. Chasing happiness is "The Great Mistake."
- *Suffering as means:* suffering, perseverance, character, hope. Barriers: believing you should not suffer, and fear.
- *Happiness addiction:* all addiction is avoidance of emotional pain; hidden ones (pride, recognition, comfort) are hardest because they are acceptable.
- *Eight attachments:* how others see you; money and possessions; what you want; comfort; your past; how things are; expectations; your self.
- *Lollipop exchange.*
- Also: *renew the mind*; *humility is thinking of yourself less*; *rest rhythm* (one full day in seven with nothing on the to-do list); *comfort makes discomfort harder* (spend as much time with the homeless as with the high-status); "live as if you will die tomorrow; prepare as if you will live forever."

**So what.** Do Beauty Before Bed nightly and take a weekly day off. Aim for joy, not happiness. Name your addiction, especially the socially acceptable ones. List your eight attachments. Anti-patterns: fear-driven self-protection, resisting hardship (victimhood, "why me?"), chasing better circumstances for better feelings, slacking after victory, internalizing after defeat. Murphy's notes point to Clear's *Atomic Habits* and Eyal's *Indistractable* on comfort and discomfort-avoidance.

## Chapter 6: Change Your State, Change Your Life (How to Control Your Emotions)

**Central argument.** Everything pivots on how you feel. Your state (feelings plus physiology) comes almost entirely from what you think, directed by what your heart desires most and what you pay attention to. Learn to direct attention, desires, focus/relaxation and breathing, and you control your state.

**Full stories.**

*Driving to an important event.* Nerves rising, heart racing, you pull over and get centered: spot above the horizon, two long exhales. The mind settles and heart rate normalizes. You are still nervous but no longer scattered. Then run "I expect nothing... I can handle anything." More peace and presence; drive on.

*Erica K., the ballet dancer.* She stands at center stage hours before the performance, breathes, and imagines hugging the entire audience, with energy flowing from above through her to everyone.

*Gareth Bale.* Sleeps 9-10 hours plus a 1-2 hour nap and wakes exactly four hours before kick-off: homeostasis of routines.

*Freddie Roach.* "I don't want him angry. We have a plan; I want him to follow the plan."

*The kid on the bike.* You move toward what you focus on (the rock he stares at).

*The $10M ocean house (The Choice).* Choose between a $10M ocean house with normal stresses, or an apartment paycheck-to-paycheck with guaranteed deep contentment for life. The point: go directly for what the house is supposed to give you.

*The bottom-right-corner exercise.* Look only at one corner of a picture and resist the mind's jump to the whole; that is what sharp focus on one swing, bar or pitch requires.

**Evidence.** The priming research: hot coffee leads to warmer judgments and more generosity. No numbers are given in the notes. Fehmi's Open Focus is cited as method. The "juicy lemon drop" example: vividly imagined equals processed as real. Caveat: sources are cited by name only.

**Key terms.**
- *Results Chain (Diagram 6.1):* circumstance, filtered through memories and beliefs, thoughts, feelings, actions, results (which become new circumstances).
- *Four pattern processes:* assumptions, generalizations, deletions, distortions.
- *"What gets your attention gets you" (E. Stanley Jones)* and *priming.*
- *Direct your desires:* desire is a marvelous motivator and terrible master; discipline is delaying gratification; have-to (ego) vs. get-to (mastery).
- *Three focus/relax skills:* soft and sharp focus; moderate and complete relaxation (delta waves in sleep; anxiety is beta too fast); moving between; practice with peripheral vision and *Open Focus*.
- *Reboot / Basic Reset:* stop; spot above the horizon; stare and stop all thoughts; inhale through the nose (~4); exhale longer (~6) relaxing jaw and face, letting go of desires and concerns; twice (once is fine).
- *Box breathing:* in 4, hold 4, out 4 (jaw loose), hold 4.
- *Centering phrases:* "I am... completely stopping"; "I am... fully present"; "Focus... and enjoy"; "Relax... and smile." Word-shedding: "Be here / right now" to "Be here / now" to "Be / here" to "Be / —".
- *"I expect... nothing"* (samurai: expect nothing, prepare for anything).
- *Anchoring* (smell strongest) and *Float-Up.*
- *State; perception map* (some rugged with dangers, some smooth with opportunities).

**So what.** Practice soft/sharp focus and moderate/complete relaxation daily, not just on game day. Use the Reset whenever you are not present. Expect abundance, attach to nothing. Anchor the states you want and float up to release others. An anchored old feeling (a song, a venue) is "just a memory, not today's truth." Anti-patterns: gas pedal always down; giving in to every desire; carrying expectations of repeating past success (the five-foot putt becomes harder than the ten-foot one). Parallel: Bailey's *Hyperfocus* (scatterfocus and soft focus).

## Chapter 7: The World Is Flat (How to Develop Beliefs in Line with Your Dreams)

**Central argument.** Beliefs, subconscious ideas about who you are and what is possible, are the X factor behind every performance. They act as a thermostat that pulls results back to your comfort level. They form from repeated thoughts and especially feelings, and can be deliberately rewritten.

**Full stories.**

*The Super Bowl kick (the X factor).* The kick is decided less by form, wind or the snap than by the beliefs, thoughts and feelings of the preceding months: "are you the type of person who kicks game-winners?"

*Callista Balko.* Balko struck out nine times out of nine against Cat Osterman over a season. In preseason she wrote affirmations ("I am an incredible two-strike hitter," "I love pressure, the more intense the better I play," "I control my destiny"), scripted the post-championship reporter interview in full detail, and visualized the exact scenario daily for months: Osterman pitching, winning run on second, the World Series on the line, a game-winning hit. In the actual game they intentionally walked a batter to face her. She felt confidence, then on first base thought only that she had rehearsed this and it had just come true. The notes' reconstruction: affirmations plus scripted scene plus daily feeling plus hard practice.

*Andy Lopez's locker room.* He played College World Series footage on a continuous loop in the Pepperdine locker room all season; the small school won it all.

*Muggsy Bogues.* Shot at five at 5'3", he played 14 NBA seasons; imagination fed daily.

*The hurdle in the living room.* An Olympian cleared a 42-inch hurdle 25 times a day to plant the image.

*Delancey Street.* Its method with ex-felons: stand tall, look up when you do not feel confident ("act as if").

*Belief-forming stories.* An experience with strong feeling (rejection when you sang; the "spider" that was a ball of yarn) leads the brain to file the feeling and form a belief, often for life, often in an instant.

*Murphy's Achilles cast.* He wrote a healing affirmation on it ("My body is a fit, powerful machine" is one of the examples in the book's spirit).

**Evidence.** Mostly case examples. Duckworth's grit is cited (fed by vividly picturing life after the dream). Maltz's *Psycho-Cybernetics* is cited as a parallel (servo-mechanism and self-image thermostat). No quantified studies in the notes.

**Key terms.**
- *X Factor; Homeostasis* (the subconscious keeps performance inside what you believe is right for you, dragging down over-performance and lifting under-performance).
- *How beliefs form;* *beliefs are habits of behavior and expectation;* "we see the world as we are."
- *Belief audit:* the three main beliefs about achieving the goal; are they useful now; what would I have to believe?
- *Speak the Truth About the Past:* "In the past I struggled with X and I'm getting better every day," not "I'm struggling with X."
- *One-Second Tingle:* daily visualization until you feel excitement for at least one second.
- *Negative-thought overrides:* dismiss immediately (surfer letting waves pass), replace with the opposite affirmation, shout "STOP!" with a giant red stop sign, snap a rubber band on your wrist.
- *Affirmations:* positive, short, present tense, as if true today; not expected to be believed at first. Model the best ("what does the best in the world at this believe and say to themselves?"); silent phone pop-ups. Examples: "Something incredible is happening"; "Extraordinary opportunities pursue me daily."
- *Act as if.* *Congruence check (the final piece).*

**So what.** Run the belief audit on every significant goal; put every unwanted pattern in past tense when you speak; feel the dream daily for one second; check congruence (smokers' social bond; a wealth outcome a strict upbringing distrusts). Anti-patterns: present-tense struggle talk (prolongs slumps, ends careers), giving up a skill after one painful experience without testing the belief, ignoring congruence (repeated injury or illness before big events can be a subconscious veto). The metaphor: "the world is flat" if you believe it, so you never sail far enough to learn otherwise.

## Chapter 8: A Clear and Present Beauty (The Five Most Powerful Ways to Be Fully Present)

**Central argument.** Being fully present, a mind with no past or future, no needs and no thoughts of outcome, is one of the two decisive factors in peak performance (with belief). Five practices produce it: get out of your head; focus on routines and only what you control; be grateful; use a mantra; ruthlessly eliminate hurry.

**Full stories.**

*The PGA Tour client, 2019.* The client began meeting his caddie before every round to review the Fearless Four for the day, then took one silent minute to visualize pursuing those four goals. Several weeks later he won an event worth over $1M. The notes' reconstruction: a pre-work ritual in which you name today's four process goals with a partner, then take a minute of visualization.

*Harrington's mantra.* "I'm going to win" in the Open playoff.

*Murphy's trail-riding trio.* They sang out loud, visualized ESPN covering them, and repeated "I am the best athlete in the world."

*Wim Hof retreat.* "My body is a sauna... my backpack is a heater" on a two-hour shirtless hike at -8 C. Lesson: you are far more powerful than you think; your conscious mind does not know what is possible.

*The monks and the mandala.* Days of sand painting, then it is destroyed; the process and the attentiveness to each moment is what matters.

*Haiti.* Digging someone out of rubble, you are not thinking about the promotion you missed; single focus makes you selfless, therefore fearless, therefore present.

*Marconi.* Radio waves were always there; presence, beauty and confidence are always there, waiting to be tuned to.

*Tressel and the SEAL instructor.* Tressel: daily solitude plus one gratitude per player. Navy SEAL instructor: "humility can defeat any opponent; pride busts down the front door and gets you killed; humility sees the back door."

*Reid Hoffman and Lamott.* Hoffman's embarrassing first version and Lamott's messy first drafts, as proof that perfection is the enemy of presence.

**Evidence.** Hurry sickness (Friedman and Rosenman) with symptoms; **95% of executives have it** (Jolly). Willard's prescription to Ortberg: "You must ruthlessly eliminate hurry from your life. There is nothing else." Nouwen: busyness is a compulsive search for approval. Bruce Lee: the great mistake is anticipating the outcome. Caveat: figures are cited without methodology.

**Key terms.**
- *Desire vs. passion.* Desire is future-focused and judges whether the outcome will come; passion is in the present.
- *Practice 1, get out of your head:* anxiety is too many thoughts from too many concerns; a cluttered mind is a heart without one unifying devotion (Bonhoeffer); simplify to one thing (Phelps: "what could I remove from my life?"); sound tool: loudest sound, quietest, both, add one body part.
- *Practice 2, routines and only what you control:* meticulous but never attached ("that's how it's supposed to be today"); *simple mind* (Ronn Svetich): get the sign, see the pitch, throw it; ask "did I keep a simple mind at least 80% of the time?"; *GPS Tool:* 3 min Gratitude (small, specific, last 24-48 h), 3 min Presence (eyes open, let go of desires), 3 min Showtime (visualize the big moment under pressure), 1 min Funnel (outside, barefoot, arms in a Y, receive abundance). Wanting control and wanting perfection are the same fear.
- *Practice 3, gratitude:* you cannot be anxious and grateful at once, or judgmental and grateful; gratitude and entitlement are opposite poles; practice on small specifics (a blade of grass); gratitude expands vision, creativity, pattern recognition (the chess master seeing openings); treat every annoyance as sent to help you.
- *Practice 4, mantra.*
- *Practice 5, eliminate hurry:* hurry is a disordered heart, not schedule; the cause of stress, not its cure; kills joy and compassion (compassion interrupts plans). Rhythm: gaps about every 90 minutes, one full day in seven, one year in seven.
- *Fully present:* heightened awareness, time warps, movements effortless, sometimes spectator-of-yourself. *Stress is a choice.* *Sharpen the saw / time-outs.*

**So what.** Start every morning with GPS (10 minutes). Choose one devotion and one basic element per task. Build gaps into the day. Anti-patterns: multitasking as productivity (the mind processes one thought at a time), perfectionism that delays shipping/performing, trying to control everything (scatters energy), hurrying to reduce stress. Parallels in the notes: Burkeman's *Four Thousand Weeks* and Newport's *Deep Work*. "There is never a lack of beauty, only a lack of vision."

## Chapter 9: Unstoppable (How to Overcome Mental Blocks, Fears and Phobias)

**Central argument.** The yips, mental blocks and phobias are not malfunctions: they are the subconscious working perfectly to protect you from a feeling it has filed as danger. Because the block is an anchored association, it can be collapsed and replaced, often in one session, by removing the emotion, adding a small change and anchoring a positive state to the original context.

**Full stories.**

*Steve Sax.* The notes use "the Sax sequence": an error, then feeling bad, then looking at stats and projecting the future, then more errors, then obsession (*idee fixe*), then a career-threatening block. He threw perfectly blindfolded in an empty stadium, so effort and skill were not the problem. His smallest change was calling his dad immediately; his dad said "one day you'll wake up and it's gone; it happened to me too." That normalization was the first crack in the block.

*Rick Ankiel.* Wild pitches in the playoffs led to nightmares, vodka before games and years in the minors. He quit, then returned as an outfielder throwing 300-foot lasers. Same arm; a different anchored context. His smallest change: calling time after the first wild pitch and recalling a moment of perfect accuracy.

*The top-10 golfer.* He hits into the water on a par 3 on day one. Asked to picture the hole tomorrow, he rates his confidence "very low; I see it going in the water." Fifteen minutes on the phone running the dissociate, smallest change, positive-feeling-anchor sequence; he plays that hole effortlessly the rest of the week.

*The Masters client.* The night before the Masters, he names the holes he lacks confidence on; 20 minutes of the same sequence; he birdies or pars those holes all tournament and finishes top-10.

*The abuse survivor.* Abused as a child, a client rated four or five memories 8-10; after the work they were 0-2; he was ranked No. 1 within six months.

*Triggers you have forgotten.* Discomfort around a stranger's jacket that resembles a threat from adolescence.

**Evidence.** Client results (testimonial), not trials. The pain rating (1-10 before, target 0-2 after) is the measuring device. Neuroplasticity: every repeated thought-feeling carves a pathway; you can rewire deliberately or accidentally with fear. Third-person rule: the farther the viewpoint from the action, the weaker the emotion. Caveat from the notes: serious trauma may need more, including understanding the perpetrator's own fear; the technique is NLP-derived. Kross's *Shift* (distanced self-talk) is noted as a lighter-weight alternative.

**Key terms.**
- *The subconscious's three jobs:* (1) sift sensory input for physical or emotional danger (same folder); (2) run life in the background; (3) line up circumstances and results with your beliefs (the 70s golfer on pace for the low 60s is pulled back).
- *How a block forms.* Painful event, context locked in, added to the warn/protect list, embedded as deep as the emotion, replayed whenever the context recurs.
- *Why easy tasks block:* social pressure not to miss (extra points; two-foot putts).
- *Four removal principles:* (1) take the emotion out; (2) find the smallest change; (3) insert a positive high-energy feeling plus the small change into the block's context; (4) anchor that feeling to the original painful context.
- *Hollywood Helper:* theater, black-and-white still, projection booth, run black-and-white at 2x/4x/8x, then step in and run backwards first-person at 2x/4x/8x/16x.
- *Insert and anchor positive feelings:* recall a calm, confident time with all senses; locate in the body; find two more; stack three; re-enter first-person, in color, with the change; play 5-7 times.
- *Power APP (Anchor Peak Performance):* memory on a floor spot; step in, step out, shake it off; countdown breathing (inhale 10 to 7, pause, exhale 6 to 1, 1-2 min); step in with new energy; full circle; stay 3-4 min; step out.
- *Guard questions:* Are you willing to be uncomfortable? To look foolish and laugh? To fail? To face the feeling of failure or panic? Do you have a purpose beyond success in your profession?
- *Fusing images and feelings:* the subconscious works mainly in images and feelings.

**So what.** Treat a block as a protective anchor with a wrong threat list: update the list, do not fight the guard. Rate the memory first; repeat until 2 or below; no details need be shared. Anti-patterns: working harder at the blocked skill; analyzing and worrying about the block (convinces the subconscious the danger is real); assuming you are weird. The best protection is a purpose beyond your profession and willingness to look foolish. You move toward what you think about.

## Chapter 10: The Hero and the Goat (How to Have Poise Under Pressure)

**Central argument.** Poise under pressure is learnable. Pressure arises when stakes are high and the outcome uncertain, pulling the mind into the future. Poise comes from resonance, effortless excitement from being fully engaged as your true self, produced by four keys: share your heart not your ego, pursue mastery not the score, love your opponent, visualize presence not perfection.

**Full stories.**

*Dr. Tribble's resident.* In a failing cardiac operation, the resident performs an unorthodox procedure to get air to the patient. Asked "have you ever done that before?" he answers: "No, but I've thought about it a lot." He had dreamed contingencies and rehearsed poise in them. Reconstruction: list worst-case interruptions (no projector, notes lost, hostile question) and visualize handling each with a clear mind until nothing real can exceed the rehearsal.

*Stewart Cink, 2009 Open.* He said "nah, don't got it": no confidence, but no fear of the feeling of failing; gratitude to be there. Resonance without confidence; he won.

*Kyla Inaba and the adversity ladder.* Anything you cannot control is how it is supposed to be; "bad" weather is good for Inner Excellence athletes. Staying neutral puts you one notch above the field; embracing it as sent for you puts you two notches up, before the event starts. She won.

*The ski race told to Mickelson.* Two best friends race to the bottom, photo finish, high-five. Would you storm off when the photo shows you lost by inches? Then you were not skiing for the race.

*Ryan Dodd's dock mantra.* "I expect nothing... I can handle anything."

*Bonnie Blair.* Valued a 4th-place personal best over a gold.

*Client's four shifts before a world title.* Gratitude for opponents; respect as people; wanting the best for them; recognizing the greatest rival is necessary to reach your potential.

*Derin McMains, GSP, the Great Debaters.* McMains: flow comes from loving the people you are in the dance with; their best brings out yours. GSP prays for his opponent. The Great Debaters mantra: the opponent "does not exist... merely a dissenting voice to the truth I speak."

*Sully.* Absolute focus, "thought of nothing else," calm forced through training.

*Murphy's ivy-covered room.* Visualized with MLK, Wooden and Mandela, anchored to one song.

*Phil Jackson.* The moment the trophy is handed over, "we are no longer a success."

*Ohno's bubble.* Trying to beat your opponent is a distraction from staying in the bubble.

*Posture.* Scouts can spot the prospect stepping off the bus.

**Evidence.** Quoted athletes and coaches; GSP's split of roughly 80% visualizing what he will do and 20% overcoming adversity. No studies. Caveat: all are testimonials.

**Key terms.**
- *Six elements of performance:* circumstances, beliefs, thoughts, state, performance, results; the three that matter most (beliefs, thoughts, state) are unseen.
- *Presence* (clear mind, unburdened heart), *Resonance* (excitement and presence from full engagement in your natural state), *Goat* (the one blamed for the loss).
- *Resonance's characteristics:* clear mind; unburdened heart; non-judgmental awareness. *Obstacles:* overanalysis, self-consciousness, constant judging.
- *Key 1, share your heart, not your ego* (Sinek: perfect execution cannot produce mental toughness; giving something beautiful can, because you can always do that).
- *Key 2, pursue mastery, not the score:* "from ability to availability"; to be the G.O.A.T. you must be willing to be the goat.
- *Key 3, love your opponent:* the opponent is your partner in the dance; keep want-to greater than got-to.
- *Key 4, visualize presence, not perfection:* the master has failed more than the beginner has tried; emotionally prepare for more pressure than reality can deliver. Techniques: Preview (daily, 5 minutes before every sleep, including naps); Confidence memories (stack three); Presence under pressure (exaggerate to the ridiculous: Masters, back nine, Sunday, gale and rain, cameras in your face); Relax; Reframe (replay mistakes as you wanted them); Scene of Success (the 20 seconds and then 20 minutes *after*, not the performance itself); Role models/mentors (the best in the world asking *you* for advice).
- *Newburg's five questions:* How do I want to feel? What does it take to get that feeling? What keeps me from it? How can I get it back? What am I willing to work for?
- *Expect abundance, prepare to suffer.* *Pressure is the reward.*

**So what.** Winning is the goal, not the dream; feelings are yours, outcomes are not. Rehearse worst cases. Aim for resonance rather than confidence. Anti-patterns: wanting your opponent to fail (negative energy and bad strategy; a strong opponent is your best chance to play your best), visualizing perfection (sets up judgment), meeting others' expectations (future-bound, uncontrollable). Everything happens twice: first in the mind, then in life. Parallel: Nguyen Ch 8 (mushin).

## Chapter 11: Maslow, Michael Jordan and the Navy SEALs (Three Hallmarks of Extraordinary Leaders, plus Conclusion)

**Central argument.** Beyond technical expertise, the greatest coaches and leaders do three things: they redefine success, connect individuals with a vision beyond themselves, and seek self-mastery and help others do the same. Leadership is developing people to be their true selves so they can help others do likewise.

**Full stories.**

*The rapport dialogue.* Leader: "What's most important to you about your job?" Employee: "Being challenged." "What does being challenged give you?" "I get to be creative; I feel needed and useful." "What's important about being needed?" "Security." The answers stop advancing: the root value is found. Frame the team's vision in terms of the security it provides, and the person's discretionary effort follows.

*The angry employee.* Walk with the angry employee, match volume, then guide to sit (pace, pace, pace, lead).

*Westering's player.* "They're here to beat us; we're here to be us."

*Wooden.* He neither scouted opponents nor recruited.

*SEAL in Hell Week.* "I focused on my team; quitting wasn't an option."

*Brother Lawrence.* A dishwasher whose presence is studied 400 years later.

*Tressel.* Flew to learn from the reigning champion he would later beat.

*Michael Jordan.* Loved practice: "pure basketball," challenged in what he was good at, no crowd, no score. Practice and games became the same opportunity for resonance.

*The text to a PGA star.* Stop trying to make birdies; try to be who you are; it takes zero effort. "Success takes care of itself when you take care of yourself."

*Davey Johnson.* Yelling at one player is yelling at everyone.

*Conclusion.* The seesaw between past and future with the present as balance point. Sacred moments felt unsettling because they revealed potential and the suffering required. The reader now has a mindset, skill set and tool set. Seek first to develop the inner world and relationships, and the rest is added.

**Evidence.** Collins' myth #10 (visionary companies beat themselves, not the competition); Blue Ocean (stop trying to beat the competition); the rapport split: **posture/physiology 55%, tone 38%, words 7%**. The Warriors' four values: joy, compassion, mindfulness, competition. No further data. Parallel frameworks: Grove's *High Output Management* and Scott's *Radical Candor*.

**Key terms.**
- *Hallmark 1, redefine success:* Wooden (peace of mind from the effort to become the best you are capable of); Tressel adds "for the group"; Westering (winning is a by-product of learning to live decently). Separate purpose from goals.
- *Hallmark 2, vision beyond themselves:* purpose (who you are) vs. vision (a goal serving the greater good). *Rapport, pace, pace, pace, lead.* *Values elicitation.*
- *Pink elephant:* the brain cannot picture "don't"; "hold on to the ball," not "don't fumble."
- *Hallmark 3, self-mastery:* the wise leader serves (receptive, yielding, following; Tao of Leadership). *Renewal list:* focus on what is True; fill your mind daily with love/wisdom/courage from books, podcasts and sermons and memorize key ideas; train the heart's appetites; spend time with the much wiser and those in much greater need; review the day before bed then let go; monitor the ego; one day in seven; honest feedback.
- *A person first* (Vermeil); *growth is the end;* *servant-oriented focus* (Collins: contribution first, profits as by-product); *culture at scale.*
- Resonance is built on relationships: values-actions, mind-heart, self-others, self-environment.

**So what.** Define success as effort and growth for the group. Give people purpose and vision. Pace three times before you lead. Coach in pictures of what you want. Model renewal: leaders must know when they are not present. Anti-patterns: measuring by have/done/compare, trying to beat the competition as the primary focus, "don't" instructions in crucial moments, leading without renewal.

## (b) The big ideas of the book

1. **Heart first, performance second.** The pursuit of extraordinary performance and the best possible life are the same path. Appears in Ch 0, restated in Ch 4 and the Conclusion.
2. **Self-centeredness is the root of fear.** It is a limiting default, not a moral failing; attachment to past failures and comparison feed it. Ch 0, 2.
3. **Identity must rest on something no circumstance can remove.** PALMS, uniforms and titles are unstable foundations (Bunpachiro, Murphy's Cubs years). Ch 0, 1.
4. **Thoughts are opinions, not facts; separate them first.** The Critic, Monkey Mind and Trickster thrive on fusion. Ch 3, with presupposition 8 in Ch 0.
5. **Love, wisdom and courage overcome fear.** Each maps to freedom, belief and focus. Ch 4.
6. **Goals are outcomes; dreams are feelings.** Live the dream daily; winning is the goal, not the dream. Ch 4, 10.
7. **Self-mastery means dropping attachments and serving something beyond yourself.** The samurai and Pugh both show it. Ch 4, 5.
8. **Joy, not happiness, is the target.** Joy survives suffering; chasing happiness is The Great Mistake. Ch 5.
9. **You control state by controlling attention and breathing.** What gets your attention gets you. Ch 6.
10. **Beliefs are a thermostat.** Results will not exceed beliefs for long; rewrite beliefs with feeling, past-tense speech and congruence checks. Ch 7, 9.
11. **Presence is trained.** Five practices, especially gratitude and eliminating hurry. Ch 8.
12. **A mental block is protection, not malfunction.** Update the anchored association rather than fight it. Ch 9.
13. **Poise is learned: share your heart, pursue mastery, love your opponent, visualize presence.** Pressure is the reward. Ch 10.
14. **Leaders redefine success, connect people to a vision beyond themselves, and model self-mastery.** Ch 11.

## (d) Anti-patterns to avoid

- Success in order to be happy (Ch 0); identity in what you cannot control (Ch 0, 2).
- Obsession with winning; identity in the uniform; chasing feelings to feel better (Ch 1).
- Comparing to feel better; unchecked doubts (Ch 2).
- Judging every circumstance; Trickster lies lingering; arguing for limitations; perfectionism after success (Ch 3).
- Avoiding fear; outcome-oriented focus; attachment to routines (Ch 4).
- Fear-driven self-protection; resisting hardship; reactionary living; slacking after victory; internalizing after defeat (Ch 5).
- Gas pedal always down; giving in to every desire; carrying expectations of repeating past success (Ch 6).
- Present-tense struggle talk; giving up a skill after one painful experience; ignoring congruence (Ch 7).
- Multitasking as productivity; perfectionism; controlling everything; hurrying to reduce stress (Ch 8).
- Working harder at the blocked skill; analyzing the block; assuming you are weird (Ch 9).
- Wanting your opponent to fail; visualizing perfection; meeting others' expectations (Ch 10).
- Measuring by have/done/compare; beating the competition as the main focus; "don't" instructions; leading without renewal (Ch 11).

## (e) If you remember only ten things

1. Heart first, performance second.
2. Self-centeredness is the root of fear; fear is the symptom.
3. Separate circumstances (facts) from thoughts (opinions) before solving anything.
4. Name the Critic, Monkey Mind and Trickster; they run on attachment to results.
5. Love, wisdom, courage = lead with your heart, expand your vision, be fully present.
6. Goals are outcomes, dreams are feelings; run the Fearless Four daily (100% of whatever you have today).
7. Reset: spot above the horizon, in ~4, out ~6, jaw loose, release wants; "I expect nothing; I can handle anything."
8. Beliefs are the thermostat: speak the truth about the past, feel the dream daily, check congruence.
9. Presence: one devotion, gratitude, a mantra, and ruthlessly eliminate hurry.
10. Poise: share your heart, pursue mastery, love your opponent, visualize presence (80% execution / 20% adversity), and as a leader pace three times before you lead.

## (f) Open questions and limits the notes flag

- **Scope:** the notes cover the book content only; they do not evaluate it.
- **Spiritual framing:** Murphy's framing is explicitly spiritual (Christian references, "created for glory"); readers who do not share it may need to translate.
- **Evidence base:** anecdotal (athlete case studies); the block-removal techniques are NLP-derived and presented from coaching experience rather than controlled trials. Serious trauma may need more than the technique (Ch 9).
- **Statistics are thin:** the only numbers are illustrative (95% of executives with hurry sickness, 55/38/7 rapport split, 80/20 visualization, pain ratings), and the notes give no methodology.
- **Related views:** for the same "let go of thinking" thesis in a Three Principles frame see Nguyen; for evidence-based emotion regulation see *Shift*; for habits and environment see Clear and Eyal.
- **Unresolved by the notes:** how well the practices generalize outside sport and high-pressure performance beyond the leadership chapter; what dosage of practice is needed beyond the stated defaults.
