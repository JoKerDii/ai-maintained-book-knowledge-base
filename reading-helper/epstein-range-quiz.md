# Range Quiz Book
*Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World* by David Epstein

**How to use:** Cover the answers, answer from memory (out loud or in writing), then check. Anything you miss is a section to reread. Each chapter has short-answer (SA), true/false (T/F), and multiple-choice (MC) questions. A cumulative final exam is at the end. Chapter numbering follows the `epstein-range` skill (Introduction, Ch 1-12, Conclusion). This quiz is built from that skill's notes, not the book's own text.

## Introduction: Roger vs. Tiger

**Q1 (SA).** What are the Tiger path and the Roger path?
**A:** The Tiger path is early, narrow, technical, deliberately practiced specialization from the earliest possible age. The Roger path is broad sampling of many activities, unstructured play, and delayed concentration. Federer sampled many sports and specialized late and was still number one in his mid-thirties. Tiger Woods was handed a putter before he could talk.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the "sampling period" and why is it not a delay?
**A:** The phase where learners try a variety of activities in unstructured or lightly structured settings, gain a range of proficiencies, and learn their own abilities and proclivities before narrowing. It is integral to development because choosing before sampling means optimizing something you may be poorly matched to.

**Q3 (MC).** In the study definition, "deliberate practice" requires all of the following EXCEPT:
a) explicit instructions on the best method  b) individual supervision by an instructor  c) immediate informative feedback  d) a childhood start before age five
**A:** d. The four conditions are explicit instructions, individual supervision, immediate feedback, and repeatedly performing the same or similar tasks. If a domain can't supply them, the ten-thousand-hours logic doesn't straightforwardly apply.

**Q4 (T/F).** Copying what current elites do now is a good guide to how they should start. **A:** False. Elites at their peak do practice more deliberately, but that says nothing about how they started. Copying peak practices at the start skips the sampling period.

**Q5 (SA).** Give two pieces of evidence that the Roger path is the more common one.
**A:** (Any two) German World Cup winners who did not play more organized soccer than amateurs until age 22 or later; a two-year study in which 11-year-olds who played more sports and more nonorganized soccer improved more by age 13; Ester Ledecká winning Winter Olympic gold in two sports; Lomachenko crediting four years of Ukrainian dance for his footwork.

**Q6 (MC).** Compared with a 30-year-old, a 50-year-old tech founder is about ___ as likely to start a blockbuster company.
a) half  b) equally  c) nearly twice  d) ten times
**A:** c. The fastest-growing start-ups had founders averaging 45.

**Q7 (SA).** What is the "system of parallel trenches"?
**A:** Everyone digs deeper into their own specialty and never stands up to look into the next trench, where the solution actually sits. It produces silos in which each group's reasonable local action can add up to a catastrophic whole.

## Ch 1: The Cult of the Head Start

**Q1 (SA).** Define kind and wicked learning environments (Robin Hogarth).
**A:** Kind: patterns repeat, and feedback is extremely accurate and usually rapid, so you improve just by doing. Wicked: rules are unclear or incomplete, patterns may not repeat or be obvious, and feedback is delayed, inaccurate, or both.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the Kahneman-Klein accord?
**A:** Klein showed experts such as firefighters and grandmasters recognize patterns instinctively; Kahneman showed experience can breed confidence without skill. Coauthoring in 2009, they agreed both were right, depending on the domain. Narrow experience builds skill in chess, poker, and firefighting, but not in predicting financial or political trends or how employees or patients will perform.

**Q3 (SA).** What was the typhoid diagnostician example meant to show?
**A:** In the most devilishly wicked environments experience reinforces the wrong lessons: his hands-on tongue exam kept "correctly" predicting fever because he was spreading it.

**Q4 (MC).** In the chunking demonstration with Susan Polgar, what happened when the truck was turned around to show randomly placed pieces?
a) she reproduced them just as well  b) she could barely reproduce anything  c) she refused  d) she reproduced them faster
**A:** b. Grandmaster memory is pattern-dependent, not photographic (Chase and Simon, 1973).

**Q5 (SA).** Why do freestyle chess ("centaurs") results matter for the head-start argument?
**A:** Pattern honing is the part of skill most easily automated. Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997; in freestyle chess a duo of amateurs with three ordinary computers beat both the best chess supercomputer and grandmaster-plus-computer teams, and a 2014 tournament was won by a team captained by an engineer with no official chess rating (Anson Williams). Humans add value in strategy and integration.

**Q6 (T/F).** Experience always makes experts better when rules change slightly. **A:** False. Cognitive entrenchment: experienced bridge players adapted worse than nonexperts to altered play order, and experienced accountants did worse than novices under a new tax law.

**Q7 (SA).** What are the "circuit breaker" and "one foot outside your world"?
**A:** The circuit breaker (Hogarth) is drawing on outside experiences and analogies to interrupt the inclination to reuse a previous solution that may no longer work. Keeping one foot outside your world, plus drastically varying challenges within your domain, is Erik Dane's guard against cognitive entrenchment.

## Ch 2: How the Wicked World Was Made

**Q1 (SA).** What are "scientific spectacles"?
**A:** Flynn's term for Luria's categorical thinking: instead of relying on direct experience, we make sense of reality through classification schemes and layers of abstract concepts. The key ability is moving freely between categories.

**Q2 (SA).** What did Luria find in remote Uzbek and Kyrgyz villages in 1931?
**A:** The most remote villagers refused to group skeins of wool by color ("none of them are the same"), grouped by concrete use (tools needed the log), and would answer a syllogism about cotton, which they had grown, but not an identical one about white bears in the Far North. Collective farmers and lightly schooled students classified easily. The abstract, category-shifting mind is a product of modern work.

**Q3 (MC).** The Flynn effect is about ___ IQ points per decade, with the largest gains on:
a) 3 / Raven's Progressive Matrices  b) 10 / vocabulary only  c) 3 / arithmetic  d) 1 / memory
**A:** a. Documented in more than thirty countries, and largest on Raven's matrices (eduction) and the most abstract vocabulary.

**Q4 (SA).** What is Fermi-izing and how do you do it?
**A:** Breaking a problem into sub-quantities you can bound, estimating each roughly, and multiplying to reach the right order of magnitude. No individual estimate must be accurate, because independent errors partially cancel. Use it on any number you can't check.

**Q5 (T/F).** Flynn found that a student's major, or GPA, strongly predicts broad conceptual reasoning. **A:** False. The correlation between broad conceptual thinking and GPA was about zero. He called universities' product "narrow critical competence."

**Q6 (SA).** Why does the "conceptual Swiss Army knife" matter?
**A:** A small kit of broadly transferable reasoning tools (e.g., Fermi-izing, Jeannette Wing's computational thinking: abstraction and decomposition) helps with unfamiliar problems. Teach people to think before teaching what to think about. Constrained, repetitive work gets automated; value goes to those who carry conceptual knowledge between domains.

## Ch 3: When Less of the Same Is More

**Q1 (SA).** What does "breadth of training predicts breadth of transfer" mean?
**A:** The more contexts in which something is learned, the more abstract the learner's models and the less they rely on any one example, so they apply knowledge better to unseen situations.

**Q2 (SA).** Who were the figlie del coro and what is notable about their training?
**A:** The all-female musicians of Venice's ospedali (e.g., the Pietà, which raised abandoned girls) who dominated European music for a century. They had only about an hour a day of music study early on, with formal lessons three days a week, but learned every instrument the institution owned. Anna Maria della Pietà was a premier violinist who was also known for the theorbo, cembalo, cello, lute, viola d'amore, and mandolin.

**Q3 (MC).** In Sloboda's boarding-school study, the exceptional music students had:
a) started younger and taken more lessons  b) practiced more  c) taken fewer lessons and practiced less, but spread effort across about three instruments  d) one instrument only
**A:** c. They came from less musically active families and did not start younger. A follow-up two decades later found nearly all students admitted to a competitive conservatory had played at least three instruments.

**Q4 (T/F).** The main reason children quit instruments was lack of talent. **A:** False. In a study of 1,200 young musicians, quitters reported a mismatch between the instrument they wanted and the one they actually played. Amy Chua's daughter Lulu told her mother, "You picked it, not me."

**Q5 (SA).** What is "low prior restraint" parenting?
**A:** From Adam Grant: households with extremely creative children had an average of one rule versus six for typical children, and parents voiced their opinions after the child did something they disliked rather than banning it beforehand. Creativity is hard to nurture but easy to thwart.

**Q6 (SA).** What does the jazz (improvisation) comparison add?
**A:** Learn like babies and improv masters: acquire the sound first, the grammar later. Suzuki's method mimics language acquisition. Limb's MRI work showed brain areas for self-censoring turn down during improvisation. Cecchini noted jazz-school students who never solve problems themselves "all sound the same."

## Ch 4: Learning, Fast and Slow

**Q1 (SA).** What are desirable difficulties (Bjork)? Name the four supported ones.
**A:** Obstacles that make learning slower and more frustrating in the short term but better in the long term. The four: generation effect, testing/self-testing, spacing, and making-connections questions with interleaving.

**Q2 (SA).** Explain the generation effect and the hypercorrection effect.
**A:** Struggling to produce an answer yourself, even a wrong one, improves later learning. Hypercorrection: the more confident you were in a wrong answer, the better the correction sticks.

**Q3 (MC).** Blocked practice typically yields:
a) poor immediate performance, great transfer  b) excellent immediate performance, poor transfer  c) no effect  d) great retention
**A:** b. Interleaving does the reverse: it feels worse but forces you to identify which strategy applies.

**Q4 (SA).** What did the Oberon and Macduff macaque experiment show?
**A:** Monkeys with hints (automatic, on request, or half the time) did well in practice but collapsed when hints were removed on day four. Macduff scored zero on lists practiced with automatic hints. The no-hint lists were terrible at first but on test day Oberon got nearly three-quarters right and Macduff about half. Training with hints produced no lasting learning.

**Q5 (T/F).** Air Force Academy Calculus I instructors who boosted their own students' exam scores most also helped them most in later courses. **A:** False. They harmed performance in later courses, while the deep-learning instructors received worse ratings.

**Q6 (SA).** What are making-connections vs. using-procedures questions, and what happens to the first in classrooms?
**A:** Using-procedures questions apply a just-learned procedure; making-connections questions ask why it works and where it generalizes. In US classrooms studied, about 20% of questions started as making-connections problems and 0% survived the teacher-student interaction, because hints converted them into procedure problems.

**Q7 (SA).** What is fadeout and what explains it?
**A:** A temporary academic advantage that diminishes and vanishes as others catch up. Across 67 early-childhood programs, closed skills (quickly learned via procedure) were taught in place of open skills that scaffold later learning.

## Ch 5: Thinking Outside Experience

**Q1 (SA).** What is deep analogical thinking, and what is the failure mode of using one analogy?
**A:** Gentner's term for recognizing conceptual similarities across domains that look different on the surface. A single close analogy is worse than none: in the movie-forecasting study, using only the single most analogous film collapsed predictive power. Use many, from different base domains.

**Q2 (SA).** Describe the four steps of the outside view.
**A:** (1) Describe your project and estimate the outcome; (2) list other projects with broad conceptual similarity, ignoring surface features; (3) estimate each of those; (4) revise your own estimate against that reference class.

**Q3 (MC).** Roughly what share of major infrastructure projects go over budget, and by how much on average?
a) 10% / 5%  b) 50% / 10%  c) about 90% / 28%  d) 100% / 100%
**A:** c. Edinburgh's tram was costed from the inside at £320 million and was headed toward £1 billion.

**Q4 (SA).** What is the Ambiguous Sorting Task?
**A:** 25 cards of real-world phenomena that can be sorted by domain (economics, biology) or by deep structure (positive-feedback loop, negative-feedback loop, causal chain). It diagnoses and trains structural thinking.

**Q5 (SA).** How did Kepler use analogies, and what did he refuse to do about eight minutes of arc?
**A:** After five years he discarded his accurate Mars model because two of Brahe's observations were off by eight minutes of arc, refusing to patch the hypothesis. With no concept of gravity, he reasoned by analogy (smells and heat, light, magnets, whirlpool, boatmen steering across a current), interrogating each until it broke and generated the next question. He reached bodies pulling one another and predicted the moon's role in tides.

**Q6 (T/F).** More internal detail considered about your project gives more reliable judgments. **A:** False. The more details considered, the more extreme the judgment becomes. Detail is a warning sign.

**Q7 (SA).** What did Dunbar's study of molecular biology labs find?
**A:** The most productive labs offered a new analogy about every four minutes, from a wide variety of base domains. The E. coli-only lab, with uniformly specialized backgrounds, produced no new findings and spent weeks on a problem a mixed-background lab solved in one meeting.

## Ch 6: The Trouble with Too Much Grit

**Q1 (SA).** What is match quality, and why optimize it rather than persistence?
**A:** The degree of fit between the work someone does and who they are (abilities and proclivities). Match quality gains compound faster than skill losses, so switching to a better match beats persisting in a poor one.

**Q2 (SA).** What is restriction of range, and how does it apply to grit research?
**A:** When subjects are preselected, the selected variable stops predicting within the group, like studying height's importance only among NBA players. At West Point, the Grit Scale beat the Whole Candidate Score at predicting Beast Barracks dropouts, but only 71 of 1,218 plebes dropped out, from a population already filtered by that score.

**Q3 (MC).** The multi-armed bandit model says that early in a career you should:
a) pull the same lever forever  b) buy information at a high rate with high-risk, high-reward, fast-feedback paths  c) avoid all risk  d) wait until you know yourself
**A:** b. With least experience the cost of a wrong pull is lowest, so "young and foolish" is not foolish.

**Q4 (T/F).** The Army's $500 million retention bonus solved officer attrition. **A:** False. It was taken by officers already staying and refused by those already leaving. The Officer Career Satisfaction Program, trading extra service years for choice of branch or post, got 4,000 cadets to extend in four years.

**Q5 (SA).** What are the two components of Duckworth's Grit Scale, and which penalizes re-specifying goals?
**A:** Work ethic/resilience and consistency of interests. Consistency of interests penalizes healthy changes of goals.

**Q6 (SA).** What is Seth Godin's advice about quitting, and what is "informed switching"?
**A:** Enumerate your quit conditions in advance, deciding what counts as fit information versus mere difficulty. Informed switching is leaving because new information revealed a poor fit, distinct from quitting under difficulty.

**Q7 (SA).** What did Van Gogh's story illustrate?
**A:** A multi-armed bandit at maximum intensity: art dealer, teacher, bookseller, would-be pastor, itinerant preacher, each pursued with punishing work ethic and abandoned. His biographer scored him at the 40th percentile on the Grit Scale. The path zigzagged him to a place no one had been.

## Ch 7: Flirting with Your Possible Selves

**Q1 (SA).** Contrast test-and-learn with plan-and-implement.
**A:** Ibarra's test-and-learn: sample activities, contexts, jobs, then reflect and adjust your story, and repeat. Plan-and-implement: make a long-term plan and execute without deviation. Use test-and-learn when the goal itself is uncertain.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the end of history illusion?
**A:** Dan Gilbert's finding that we recognize how much we changed in the past but believe we will barely change in the future ("works in progress claiming to be finished"). So long-term commitments are bets on a stranger.

**Q3 (MC).** In the Dark Horse Project, how many of the first 50 participants apologized for a path that was actually the norm?
a) 5  b) 12  c) 25  d) 45
**A:** d.

**Q4 (SA).** What question does a dark horse ask, and what question should you ask instead of "Who do I want to become?"
**A:** "Here's who I am at the moment, here are my motivations, here are the opportunities; which is the best match right now?" Instead of "who do I want to become," ask "Which among my various possible selves should I start to explore now?"

**Q5 (T/F).** The context principle says to diagnose a lack of drive as a character flaw first. **A:** False. Diagnose a mismatch as a context problem before treating it as a character problem. Traits express differently across situations; ask *when* someone is gritty.

**Q6 (SA).** What did Frances Hesselbein's path show?
**A:** A vocation reached through short-term planning: four professional positions, none applied for, and she tried to turn down three. Girl Scout Troop 17 for six weeks became eight years; she took her first professional job at 54 for six months and stayed four years; at 60 she attended the national CEO interview only to decline. Thirteen years as CEO, minority membership tripled, a quarter million members were added, and Drucker called her the best CEO in the country.

**Q7 (SA).** What is Paul Graham's advice and his name for the alternative?
**A:** Don't work back from a twenty-year goal; look at the options available now and choose those that give the most promising range of options afterward. The alternative is "premature optimization."

## Ch 8: The Outsider Advantage

**Q1 (SA).** What is Lakhani's InnoCentive finding?
**A:** "The further the problem was from the solver's expertise, the more likely they were to solve it." Just over a third of challenges were completely solved, despite having stumped the specialists who posted them, and more diverse solver pools solved more.

**Q2 (SA).** Define local search and the Einstellung effect.
**A:** Local search: attacking problems with specialists from one domain using methods that worked before. Einstellung effect: employing only familiar methods even when better ones exist.

**Q3 (SA).** What is undiscovered public knowledge?
**A:** Don Swanson's idea of making discoveries by connecting information across subspecialty literatures that never cite each other. He found eleven neglected connections between magnesium deficiency and migraine; by 2012 the American Headache Society and American Academy of Neurology concluded magnesium should be considered a common migraine treatment.

**Q4 (MC).** Jill Viles's key insight about Priscilla Lopes-Schliep was that:
a) she had the same muscle disease  b) the shared feature was missing fat, indicating partial lipodystrophy  c) she was a carrier only  d) she needed a pacemaker
**A:** b. Garg's test confirmed both had the same rare Dunnigan-type partial lipodystrophy, with typos neighboring on the same gene.

**Q5 (T/F).** Rising specialization shrinks the outsider's opportunity. **A:** False. It increases it: more information created by specialists means more unmerged strands.

**Q6 (SA).** What was the Eli Lilly confidentiality reflex and Bingham's response?
**A:** Lilly scientists demanded every posted problem be pulled ("Why the hell do you think anyone other than us can solve that problem?"). Bingham's counter: try it on problems that give away no trade secret; if it fails, no harm done.

## Ch 9: Lateral Thinking with Withered Technology

**Q1 (SA).** What is lateral thinking with withered technology?
**A:** Gunpei Yokoi's doctrine: put cheap, simple, thoroughly understood technology to use in ways no one else considered. If he couldn't think more deeply about new technologies, he would think more broadly about old ones.

**Q2 (MC).** Which best describes the Game Boy's technology and result?
a) cutting-edge color; sold poorly  b) 1970s-era processor, four grayscale shades; 118.7 million sold  c) experimental 3D; success  d) none
**A:** b. It won on price, pocketability, durability, and battery life. Told a rival had a color screen, Yokoi said, "Then we're fine."

**Q3 (SA).** Why did the Virtual Boy fail?
**A:** Yokoi departed from his own tenets and used experimental technology; radio emissions forced a metal plate, weight forced a tabletop unit, and nobody wanted the posture.

**Q4 (SA).** Describe Ouderkirk's three 3M profiles and which won.
**A:** Specialists (deep, narrow), generalists (broad, no depth), and polymaths (a core area of depth, less than specialists, plus more breadth than generalists). Polymaths were most likely to succeed and win the Carlton Award.

**Q5 (SA).** Birds and frogs, T-shaped and I-shaped: what is the point?
**A:** Dyson: birds survey broad vistas, frogs solve particular problems deeply; we need both. Seth's version: T-shaped people consult I-shaped people to build the trunk of the T.

**Q6 (T/F).** Under high domain uncertainty, high-breadth team members mattered most. **A:** True (Melero and Palomeras). Use specialists when problems are well-defined, breadth as ambiguity rises.

**Q7 (SA).** What did the comics study find about experience and repetition?
**A:** High-repetition workload hurt performance, years of experience had no impact, and publisher resources didn't help. Breadth of genres drove creative output.

## Ch 10: Fooled by Expertise

**Q1 (SA).** Distinguish foxes from hedgehogs.
**A:** Hedgehogs "know one big thing" and toil inside one specialty, cherry-picking details that fit their theory, so more information makes them worse. Foxes know many little things, draw on eclectic traditions, and accept ambiguity. Fame and accuracy run in a "perverse inverse relationship."

**Q2 (SA).** What was the Ehrlich-Simon bet and the lesson?
**A:** Ehrlich bet five metals would rise in price as overpopulation depleted resources; in 1990 Simon collected $576.07 because all fell. Both declared vindication and dug in, though Ehrlich would have won in 62% of ten-year windows since 1900. Commodity prices were a bad proxy. Two hedgehogs grew more dogmatic and less accurate.

**Q3 (MC).** In Tetlock's twenty-year study of 82,361 forecasts, "impossible" events happened ___ of the time and "sure things" failed ___.
a) 1% / 1%  b) 15% / over a quarter  c) 50% / 50%  d) 5% / 10%
**A:** b.

**Q4 (SA).** What is active open-mindedness?
**A:** Jonathan Baron's disposition of treating your ideas as hypotheses to test, recruiting teammates to help falsify them, and proactively seeking contrary evidence. Two-thirds of participants in one study declined payment rather than read counterarguments.

**Q5 (SA).** Who were the superforecasters and what did they beat?
**A:** Ordinary volunteers with wide-ranging interests and no relevant credentials in the Good Judgment Project, who beat intelligence analysts with classified access. Superteams of foxes became about 50% more accurate individually and beat both crowd wisdom and prediction markets.

**Q6 (T/F).** Changing your position often ("flip-flopping") signals poor forecasting. **A:** False. It is a competence signal among adept forecasters, who update hard on surprise.

**Q7 (SA).** What does "take facts from experts, not conclusions" mean, and what did Scott Eastman notice?
**A:** Ellen Cousins's rule: specialists may have blinders on. Eastman found his areas of deep study (Russian) were among his weakest forecasting areas: "if you're too much of an insider, it's hard to get good perspective."

## Ch 11: Learning to Drop Your Familiar Tools

**Q1 (SA).** What does "dropping your familiar tools" (Weick) mean and what is overlearned behavior?
**A:** Dropping tools is a proxy for unlearning and adaptation. Overlearned behavior is a response repeated so often it is no longer perceived as a situation-specific tool; Quentin Rhoades did not realize he still carried his saw.

**Q2 (SA).** What is incongruence, and what did the 334-institution research find about congruence?
**A:** Incongruence (Patil, Tetlock, Mellers) means balancing the dominant culture with opposing forces: inject individualism and dissent into process-conformist groups; inject loyalty and cohesion into improvisers. Each injection turned non-learners into learning machines. Congruence had no measurable effect on success across 334 institutions.

**Q3 (MC).** What was the real problem with the Carter Racing / Challenger lesson, according to Epstein?
a) too little data  b) the 24-race data were mostly irrelevant; only the 7 exposure flights mattered, and Boisjoly's real evidence was qualitative  c) engineers lied  d) no one spoke
**A:** b. Seventeen flights had no O-ring exposure and carried no information; five of seven showed erosion and two showed blow-by. Boisjoly's evidence was a soot streak at 75 degrees versus jet-black soot at 53, and when asked to quantify he could only say it was "away from goodness." NASA's quantitative culture rendered that inadmissible.

**Q4 (SA).** What did Captain Tony Lesmes do at Bagram?
**A:** Faced with ambiguous intel in a mass-casualty rescue, he dropped the most sacred PJ tool (going with the team) to save helicopter space for patients, floated it as a hypothesis he wanted disproved, absorbed the anger, and got seven wounded soldiers out in one trip.

**Q5 (SA).** Explain Rex Geveden's rule and Monday Notes.
**A:** Chain of command is not the chain of communication: "I will not intercept your decisions... but I will give and receive information anywhere in the organization." Von Braun's Monday Notes were one page from every engineer, annotated and circulated; they died when William Lucas turned them into upward-only standardized forms.

**Q6 (T/F).** Under the sensemaking mindset, a leader gives decisive direction but flags the plan as revisable. **A:** True (Paul Gleason): "hunches held lightly." A decision becomes a possession you defend; sense-making stays dynamic.

## Ch 12: Deliberate Amateurs

**Q1 (SA).** What are Saturday morning and Friday night experiments?
**A:** A standing block of protected, unfunded, unjustified play (Smithies; Geim) alongside regular work, not a reward after it. Most will amount to nothing. Geim's frog levitation led eventually to graphene and a Nobel.

**Q2 (SA).** How did Oliver Smithies solve his insulin problem?
**A:** On Saturday, 23 January 1954 he remembered, from age twelve, starch congealing into a jelly when his mother starched his father's collars. He cooked starch grains into a gel, ran current through it, and the insulin molecules separated by size, yielding gel electrophoresis.

**Q3 (MC).** The principle of limited sloppiness (Delbrück) says:
a) be as sloppy as possible  b) be careful not to be too careful, or you limit exploration  c) never be sloppy  d) skip documentation
**A:** b. Sloppy enough that unexpected things can happen, not so sloppy you can't tell what happened.

**Q4 (SA).** What did Uzzi's study of 18 million papers find?
**A:** Hit papers built on mostly conventional combinations plus one atypical combination. Novel combinations (citing two journals never before cited together) are about one in ten papers, are slower to be noticed, and are far likelier to be top-1% cited fifteen years on.

**Q5 (T/F).** Stagnant teams of repeat collaborators produce the most creative work. **A:** False. 1920s Broadway had star names and a 90% flop rate. Creative ecosystems have porous boundaries.

**Q6 (SA).** What does the R3 Initiative propose?
**A:** Casadevall and Bosch: Rigor, Responsibility, Reproducibility. Despecialize graduate training with philosophy, history, logic, ethics, statistics, communication, and leadership, with courses like "How Do We Know What Is True?", keeping the same total time.

**Q7 (SA).** Who was Tu Youyou and why is she in the chapter?
**A:** The "professor of the three no's" (no academy membership, no foreign research experience, no postgraduate degree), who used a fourth-century Chinese wormwood recipe for artemisinin, an example of withered technology and outsider status.

## Conclusion: Expanding Your Range

**Q1 (SA).** What is Epstein's one sentence of advice?
**A:** Don't feel behind. Compare yourself to yourself yesterday; feeling behind supplies no information, only cost.

**Q2 (SA).** What does "breakthroughs are high variance" mean?
**A:** Simonton: the more work eminent creators produced, the more duds and the higher their odds of a supernova success. Simmons: in baseball the best swing scores four; in the wider world an occasional at-bat scores a thousand.

**Q3 (MC).** Howard Scott Warshaw designed both *Yar's Revenge* and *E.T.* in the same year. This illustrates:
a) luck is irrelevant  b) same method, opposite tails of an untruncated distribution  c) specialization fails  d) nothing
**A:** b. E.T. was called the biggest commercial failure in video game history.

**Q4 (T/F).** Epstein argues specialization itself is the enemy. **A:** False. The target is premature specialization sold as a shortcut.

**Q5 (SA).** What does "plan experiments, not plans" and "approach projects like Michelangelo approached marble" mean?
**A:** Run personal versions of Friday night/Saturday morning experiments (Ibarra), and be willing to learn, adjust, and even abandon a goal as you go.

**Q6 (SA).** Give two examples of late starts.
**A:** Richter began formal piano lessons at twenty-two; Nash got a basketball at thirteen and won two NBA MVPs.

# Cumulative Final Exam (10 Questions)

**F1 (SA).** State the book's master distinction and how to use it as a gate.
**A:** Kind vs. wicked learning environments. In kind domains, specialize and trust intuition. In wicked ones, distrust confident veterans and use breadth, outside views, and belief updating. Apply it before any other framework.

**F2 (MC).** Which finding best shows that early training volume does not predict elite outcomes?
a) Susan Polgar's chunking  b) Sloboda's exceptional music students had fewer lessons and less practice but three instruments  c) the Game Boy  d) Kepler
**A:** b. (The Roger path evidence in the Introduction points the same way.)

**F3 (SA).** How do desirable difficulties and the Oberon/Macduff study relate to judging teachers or training?
**A:** Practice performance and test performance invert: hints predicted practice success and test failure. Judge learning on delayed, unaided tests and downstream outcomes (as in the Air Force Academy calculus study), not on in-session fluency or ratings.

**F4 (T/F).** Using one close analogy is a good way to forecast a novel project. **A:** False. A single near analogy is worse than none. Build a reference class and use multiple, distant analogies.

**F5 (SA).** Why can't grit studies answer when to quit?
**A:** Restriction of range: samples like West Point are already selected, and grit measures like consistency of interests penalize healthy goal changes. Quitting can be informed switching toward better match quality.

**F6 (MC).** Which is NOT an element of the test-and-learn approach?
a) small cheap experiments  b) reflecting and rewriting your story  c) naming a twenty-year destination first  d) expecting to change

**F7 (SA).** Link InnoCentive, Swanson's magnesium finding, and Jill Viles.
**A:** All show outsider advantage: distant solvers and unlinked literatures solve what specialists can't (local search, Einstellung). Viles linked a muscular dystrophy paper and visual evidence of missing fat across fields no one specialty owned.

**F8 (SA).** Contrast Yokoi's Game Boy with the Virtual Boy and say what each teaches.
**A:** Game Boy: withered technology, user experience as competitive axis, ecosystem of developers who knew the hardware. Virtual Boy: abandoning his tenets for experimental tech failed. Lesson: compete on application, not frontier specs.

**F9 (SA).** Compare hedgehog forecasting in Ch 10 with NASA's behavior in Ch 11.
**A:** Both are the same failure at different scales: bending an unfamiliar situation to a familiar tool or theory. Ehrlich and Simon cling to one big idea; NASA's quantitative culture demanded numbers Boisjoly couldn't supply. Antidotes: foxes, active open-mindedness, incongruence, sensemaking, prospecting for doubts.

**F10 (SA).** How do Ch 12 and the Conclusion fit together as practical advice?
**A:** Protect unjustified exploration time (Friday night/Saturday morning experiments, dabble time), mix in one atypical combination, expect many duds because outcomes are high variance, and don't feel behind. Judge the portfolio and process, not a single attempt or someone else's timeline.
