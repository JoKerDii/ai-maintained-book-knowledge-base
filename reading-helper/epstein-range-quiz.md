# Range (David Epstein) - Quiz Book

> **Source note.** This quiz reflects the `epstein-range` skill's chapter notes (SKILL.md, glossary, patterns, cheatsheet and all 14 chapter files), **not the book's own wording**. Nothing here is quoted from the book itself; names, numbers and stories are only those recorded in the notes. If the notes are silent on something, it is not asked.
>
> **Chapter numbering.** The book has an Introduction, Chapters 1-12, and a Conclusion. The skill's chapter files are numbered one higher (file `ch02` = book Chapter 1, and so on). This quiz uses the **book's numbering**: Intro, Ch 1 ... Ch 12, Conclusion.

## How to use this quiz

1. Cover the answers (scroll or fold) and write or say your answer before looking. Guessing first is itself the "generation effect" the book describes (Ch 4): being wrong and then corrected helps more than just reading.
2. Do one section per sitting, then wait a day or more and re-do the questions you missed. Spacing and interleaving are the point: after your first pass, jump between sections in random order.
3. Question types: **[SA]** short answer, **[TF]** true/false, **[MC]** multiple choice, **[EX]** explain in your own words, **[SC]** scenario/application.
4. Score yourself honestly: 2 points for a correct answer with the reason, 1 for the right idea but fuzzy, 0 for a miss. Aim for 80%+ per section before the final exam.
5. The final 20-question cumulative exam at the end mixes chapters and asks for cross-chapter synthesis.

## Section 1 - Introduction: Roger vs. Tiger

**Q1 [SA].** What is the difference between the "Tiger path" and the "Roger path"?
**A.** The Tiger path is early, narrow, technical, deliberately practiced specialization from the earliest possible age; the Roger path is broad sampling of many activities, unstructured play, and delayed concentration. The notes tie them to Tiger Woods (handed a putter before he could talk, coached in media answers at three, told he was "the Chosen One") and Roger Federer (squash, skiing, wrestling, badminton, basketball, handball, soccer; "pully" rather than pushy parents; asked to be moved back to a younger training group to stay with friends; still ranked number one in his mid-thirties).

**Q2 [TF].** True or false: Tiger's story is the typical way elite performers develop; Roger's is the rare exception.
**A.** False. Epstein's claim is that the Roger path is the *more common* one; Tiger's story is a real but unrepresentative sample that became the template. Evidence in the notes: German World Cup winners who did not play more organized soccer than amateurs until age 22 or later, and a two-year tracking study in which 11-year-olds who played more sports and more nonorganized soccer improved more by 13.

**Q3 [MC].** Which of these is NOT one of the four conditions of "deliberate practice" as defined in the source study?
(a) explicit instruction on the best method (b) individual supervision by an instructor (c) immediate informative feedback and knowledge of results (d) repeatedly performing the same or similar tasks (e) a minimum of ten thousand hours.
**A.** (e). The ten-thousand-hours rule is a separate claim (that accumulated hours of highly specialized training are the sole factor in skill, regardless of domain). The notes use the four-condition definition as a diagnostic: if a domain cannot supply all four, the ten-thousand-hours logic does not straightforwardly apply.

**Q4 [EX].** In your own words, why is a "head start" not an asset in itself?
**A.** Early specializers jump out to an earnings lead, but later specializers catch up because they find work that fits their skills and personality (better "match"). The early lead is therefore a visible, temporary advantage that can mask a poor fit. The notes also say people abandon the Roger path prematurely because of its visible early deficit.

**Q5 [SA].** Give the two numbers from the Intro about the age of successful founders.
**A.** A fifty-year-old tech founder is nearly twice as likely as a thirty-year-old to start a blockbuster company, and the fastest-growing start-ups had founders averaging forty-five. Both numbers are used to show that late starts are quantitatively fine.

**Q6 [MC].** "Slow bakers" is the term British Olympic officials used for:
(a) coaches who train athletes gradually (b) athletes recruited to new sports as adults - late developers (c) children who start sports before age five (d) retired athletes who coach.
**A.** (b). It illustrates *late specialization*: entering focused technical training well after peers and catching up or surpassing them through better fit and a broader base.

**Q7 [EX].** What are "silos" and a "system of parallel trenches," and why do they matter?
**A.** Silos are specialization-induced organizational blindness: each group optimizes its own piece and the whole becomes catastrophic. The "parallel trenches" image is everyone digging deeper into their own specialty and never standing up to look into the next trench, where the solution actually sits. The notes stress that overspecialization produces collective tragedy even when every individual takes the most reasonable local course of action. Hyperspecialization example: oncologists specializing by single organ.

**Q8 [SC].** A parent is told that their 6-year-old must pick one sport now or "fall behind." Which concept applies, and what test should the parent run before agreeing?
**A.** The sampling period and the Tiger-vs-Roger choice. The parent should ask whether the domain has fixed rules, repeating patterns and fast accurate feedback (kind environment). If so the Tiger path is defensible; the cheatsheet adds that early specialization is justified only by a kind environment with a hard age ceiling. Otherwise let the child sample, because early breadth buys information about their own abilities and proclivities. Also discount advice from people whose interest is served by the head-start story (the notes mention youth leagues).

**Q9 [SC].** A former military veteran is embarrassed that their LinkedIn profile looks nonlinear. What does the Intro suggest?
**A.** In the notes, the Tillman Foundation veterans had accumulated inimitable life and leadership experience, yet read their nonlinear profiles as evidence of failure. The lesson: do not read a career swerve as a liability. Range is an asset, and "career streams" and breadth are normal for those who end up doing well.

**Q10 [EX].** What is wrong with studying what current elites do and copying it from the start? And what does Epstein suggest asking instead?
**A.** Elites at their peak do practice more deliberately, but that says nothing about how they *started*; adopting peak practices at the beginning skips the sampling period. Ask instead, "What does the entire developmental path look like?" The notes add a related point: inventors and artistic creators who sacrificed a modicum of depth for breadth as their careers progressed increased their creative impact.

## Section 2 - Chapter 1: The Cult of the Head Start

**Q1 [SA].** Define a kind and a wicked learning environment (Robin Hogarth).
**A.** Kind: patterns repeat over and over, feedback is extremely accurate and usually very rapid, so you improve simply by engaging and trying to do better. Wicked: the rules are often unclear or incomplete, repetitive patterns may or may not exist and may not be obvious, and feedback is often delayed, inaccurate, or both. Chess, golf, firefighting and anesthesiology are kind; most of modern work and life is wicked. (Note: the chapter note scores domains on three axes - stable rules, repeating patterns, fast accurate feedback - while the patterns file lists four criteria, splitting feedback into fast and accurate. Either way, a "no," especially on feedback, means wicked.)

**Q2 [EX].** What was the typhoid diagnostician story, and what does it show?
**A.** A doctor's hands-on tongue exam kept "correctly" predicting fever because he was spreading the disease himself. In the most devilishly wicked environments, experience reinforces exactly the wrong lessons, so more experience means more confident error.

**Q3 [MC].** The 2009 Kahneman-Klein collaboration concluded:
(a) Kahneman was right and Klein wrong (b) Klein was right and Kahneman wrong (c) both were right, depending on whether the domain is kind or wicked (d) intuition is never reliable.
**A.** (c). Klein's naturalistic decision making showed experts (firefighters, naval commanders, grandmasters) recognizing patterns in seconds; Kahneman's heuristics-and-biases work showed experience breeding confidence but not skill. Narrow experience makes better chess players, poker players and firefighters, but not better predictors of financial or political trends or of how employees or patients will perform.

**Q4 [EX].** Explain "chunking" using the National Geographic truck demonstration.
**A.** Chunking (Chase and Simon, 1973) is grouping information into fewer meaningful units based on familiar patterns. A truck bearing a 28-piece midgame diagram was driven past Susan Polgar, and she reproduced it perfectly after a glance; the truck turned around with randomly placed pieces and she could barely reproduce anything. Grandmaster memory is not photographic; it is pattern-dependent and vanishes when the arrangement could never occur in a game.

**Q5 [SA].** Who were the Polgar sisters' father and what was his claim? Give two details of the experiment.
**A.** Laszlo Polgar believed the right head start could manufacture genius in any discipline; he picked chess because it is objective and easy to measure (Klara's words). Susan started at four with "pawn wars," was homeschooled after a fight with the Hungarian Ministry of Education, and trained against a hand-cut card catalog of 200,000 game sequences. Judit became the youngest grandmaster ever at fifteen. Laszlo concluded his method applied to a thousand children could solve cancer and AIDS. Epstein's reply: the success is *domain-bound* to an extremely kind, repetitive domain.

**Q6 [SA].** What happened in chess after Deep Blue (1997), and what is a "centaur"?
**A.** Deep Blue beat Kasparov, who responded by inventing advanced (freestyle) chess, with each human paired with a computer. Centaurs are human/computer teams, which played the highest level of chess ever seen. A duo of amateurs with three ordinary computers destroyed both the best chess supercomputer and grandmaster-plus-computer teams; a 2014 freestyle tournament was won by a team captained by Anson Williams, a British engineer with no official chess rating whose relevant skill came from the real-time strategy game *Command & Conquer*. Kasparov's tactical advantage was nullified - he drew a player he had beaten 4-0 a month earlier.

**Q7 [TF].** True or false: in the Schwartz light-bulb puzzle, the students who were paid for repeating one working solution were the ones most likely to discover the general rule.
**A.** False. Every student new to the puzzle discovered the general rule behind all seventy solutions; only one of those who had been paid for repeating a single working solution did. Schwartz's subtitle: "How Not to Teach People to Discover Rules." Rewarding repetitive short-term success undermines discovery.

**Q8 [EX].** What are cognitive entrenchment and single-loop learning, and what evidence is given for entrenchment?
**A.** Cognitive entrenchment (Erik Dane) is trading flexibility for narrow skill so that experts adapt worse than novices when rules change slightly: experienced bridge players adapted worse than nonexperts to altered play order, and experienced accountants did worse than novices under a new tax law. Single-loop learning (Chris Argyris) favors the first familiar solution that comes to mind and turns defensive when it fails; Argyris's consultants excelled at well-defined, quickly assessed problems and turned brittle when their first solution failed.

**Q9 [SC].** A senior analyst finds that every time a familiar solution springs to mind in an unfamiliar, shifting market, she feels certain. Which tools from this chapter apply, and how?
**A.** The "circuit breaker" (Hogarth): import outside experiences and analogies to interrupt the pull of a previous solution that may no longer work, and Dane's recipe to avoid entrenchment - vary challenges within the domain drastically and keep "one foot outside your world." Treat the first familiar solution as a suspect, not an answer, since the market is wicked.

**Q10 [SC].** A young professional wants to stake her career on the most codifiable, pattern-based part of her field. What does the Polgar/Kasparov story warn?
**A.** The pattern-honing that kind domains reward is precisely what machines absorb: tactics are the part of expertise most easily outsourced, while strategy (the bigger-picture planning) is where humans add more as the task moves to an open world. Invest in strategy and integration. Keep multiple "career streams" open (Christopher Connolly): successful career transitioners "traveled on an eight-lane highway." Related facts from the notes: Nobel laureates are at least 22 times more likely than other scientists to be amateur actors, dancers, magicians or other performers; Moravec's paradox says machines and humans often have opposite strengths; "islands of genius" (Treffert) depend on repetitive structures, which is why they automate easily.

## Section 3 - Chapter 2: How the Wicked World Was Made

**Q1 [SA].** What is the Flynn effect, with its numbers?
**A.** The increase in correct IQ test answers with each new generation across the twentieth century - about three points every ten years, documented in more than thirty countries. The largest and least-expected gains were on Raven's Progressive Matrices (abstract pattern completion) and the most abstract vocabulary.

**Q2 [EX].** What did Alexander Luria's 1931 natural experiment show, and why was it a natural experiment?
**A.** Soviet collectivization was pushing remote Uzbek and Kyrgyz villages through centuries of change in a few years while the most remote villages remained untouched (a control group). Collective farmers and lightly schooled students grouped skeins of wool by color; the most remote villagers refused ("None of them are the same, you can't put them together") and named things like cotton in bloom, decayed teeth, sky, pistachio. It showed that abstract, category-shifting thought is a product of modern work and schooling, not innate equipment.

**Q3 [SA].** Name three specific responses from the remote villagers that illustrate concrete, experience-bound reasoning.
**A.** (1) Alieva saw a solid-line square and a dotted-line square as a map and a watch - how could they go together? (2) Rakmat said the log belongs with hammer, saw and hatchet because the tools are useless without it; and in a group of three adults and one child he insisted the boy must stay because the boy can run errands for the adults. (3) Villagers answered a syllogism about cotton (which they grew) but not the identical one about white bears in the Far North: "Your words can be answered only by someone who was there." Also: the Ebbinghaus illusion did not fool them.

**Q4 [MC].** "Scientific spectacles" refers to:
(a) lab eyewear (b) classifying reality via abstract concepts rather than direct experience (c) memorizing scientific facts (d) specialized vocabulary.
**A.** (b). Flynn's term for Luria's "categorical" thinking. The key skill by Luria's criterion is moving freely, shifting from one category to another. The failure mode: premodern people miss the forest for the trees; modern people miss the trees for the forest.

**Q5 [EX].** What is "eduction" and how does it connect to Raven's matrices?
**A.** Eduction is working out guiding principles when given facts or materials, with no instructions, even for material never seen before. Raven's Progressive Matrices is a "culturally reduced" test of exactly that, completing patterns of abstract designs.

**Q6 [SA].** Explain Fermi-izing: when to use it and how.
**A.** Break a problem into sub-quantities you can bound (population, households, fraction owning the thing, frequency of service, time per job, working days per year), estimate each roughly, and multiply. No single estimate must be accurate, because errors in independent rough estimates partially cancel. Use it on any numeric claim you cannot verify, e.g., news and advertising statistics. The lesson: a way of thinking beats detailed prior knowledge, and a little training in broad strategies like this transfers across domains.

**Q7 [TF].** True or false: Flynn found that broad conceptual thinking correlated strongly with GPA, so top majors confer general reasoning.
**A.** False. He found the correlation to be about zero. The notes add: on the causation-versus-correlation question, students performed worse than random, and three-quarters of American college graduates go into a career unrelated to their major (Bryan Caplan on narrow vocational training).

**Q8 [EX].** What is the "conceptual Swiss Army knife," and what is Jeannette Wing's contribution?
**A.** A portable kit of broadly applicable reasoning tools, contrasted with narrow critical competence in one field. Wing's computational thinking - "using abstraction and decomposition when attacking a large complex task" and "choosing an appropriate representation for a problem" - is one candidate she argues should be as fundamental as reading. The pedagogical rule: teach students to think before teaching what to think about. Toynbee: "no tool is omnicompetent."

**Q9 [SC].** A company's onboarding buries its broad management concept inside a pile of department-specific minutiae. What does this chapter say?
**A.** That is the anti-pattern Flynn indicts himself for: burying broad concepts in course-specific minutiae. Foreground the broad concept so it transfers across domains. Judge training by whether it transfers - can people apply the field's methods for evaluating truth to another area?

**Q10 [SC].** Epstein's own anecdote: he ran a standard statistical test on a big database. What went wrong and what lesson does it teach?
**A.** He did not understand the test's limitations, produced an almost certain false positive, and was rewarded with a master's degree - "a very wicked learning environment," because a flawed method was rewarded with credentials. The lesson: do not rush into specialized research without learning the reasoning behind it, and watch for wicked environments you build for yourself. Also, expect constrained, repetitive work to be automated and position yourself where conceptual knowledge must travel between domains.

## Section 4 - Chapter 3: When Less of the Same Is More

**Q1 [SA].** State the chapter's central principle in one sentence and say why it works.
**A.** Breadth of training predicts breadth of transfer. The more contexts in which something is learned, the more abstract models the learner builds and the less they rely on any single example, so they apply knowledge better to unseen situations - the essence of creativity.

**Q2 [EX].** Who were the figlie del coro, and why does their story matter?
**A.** The "daughters of the choir," all-female musicians of Venice's ospedali (charitable institutions; the Ospedale della Pietà raised abandoned girls and trained them in music), who dominated European music for a century. Infants were surrendered through a drawer in the wall (the scaffetta). They learned every instrument the institution owned, and early on had only about an hour of music study a day with formal lessons three days a week. It matters because their training contradicts every head-start prescription: extraordinary performance came from the *number of instruments*, not early heavy structure.

**Q3 [SA].** Name details about Anna Maria della Pietà and the instruments.
**A.** A German baron called her the premier violinist in Europe; Vivaldi spent four months' salary on a violin for her at sixteen and wrote 28 concertos preserved in her scarlet leather notebook. She was, by stages, "del violino," "del theorbo," "del cembalo," "del violoncello," "del luta," "della viola d'amore" and "del mandolin." Charles Burney was astounded that "these young persons frequently change instruments." Rousseau praised the music and was shocked to see the performers behind the crepe-covered grille, disfigured by smallpox and syphilis.

**Q4 [MC].** In Sloboda's British boarding-school study (admission purely by audition), the exceptional students compared to the average ones:
(a) started younger and practiced more (b) came from more musical families (c) had taken fewer lessons, practiced less, did not start younger, but spread effort more evenly across three instruments (d) played only one instrument.
**A.** (c). They came from less musically active families. A follow-up two decades later found nearly all students admitted to a competitive conservatory had played at least three instruments, and more than half played four or five. Also: every student with a large amount of structured lesson time early fell into the "average" category; not one was in the exceptional group.

**Q5 [EX].** What did Sloboda's most successful students do about practice, and what does "let the instrument drive the practitioner" mean?
**A.** They started practicing much more only *after* they identified an instrument they wanted to focus on, whether because they were better at it or just liked it more. The instrument drives the practitioner: choose the thing that pulls you, then ramp up volume. Less skilled students stayed on the first instrument they picked up, as if they could not give up a perceived head start.

**Q6 [SA].** Why did Chua's daughter Lulu quit, and what does a study of 1,200 young musicians add?
**A.** Lulu told her mother "You picked it, not me," and quit at thirteen (the Tiger Mother model: choose early, focus narrowly, never waver; only piano or violin). In the study of twelve hundred young musicians, those who quit reported a mismatch between the instruments they wanted to play and the ones they played.

**Q7 [EX].** What is "learning like improv masters / like babies"?
**A.** Imitate and improvise first, learn formal rules later. Cecchini: you acquired the sound first and the grammar later; nobody handed you a book about dangling participles before you spoke. Suzuki designed the Suzuki Method to mimic natural language acquisition (children don't practice exercises to talk; they learn to read after speaking is established). Paul Berliner's "learning by osmosis": professional improvisers explored the band room before choosing an instrument. Cecchini's creative vs. re-creative artist: it's easier for a jazz musician to learn classical literature than the reverse; his jazz-school students "all sound the same" because they never solved problems themselves.

**Q8 [TF].** True or false: during improvisation, brain regions for focused attention, inhibition and self-censoring turn up.
**A.** False. Limb's MRI work found they turn *down* - almost as if the brain turned off its ability to criticize itself. Improvisers do the opposite of stopping to fix errors.

**Q9 [SC].** A manager wants her team to be more creative but sets many rules in advance. Which idea applies?
**A.** Low prior restraint (via Adam Grant): households with extremely creative children averaged one rule versus six for typical children, and the parents voiced their opinions *after* kids did something they disliked rather than proscribing beforehand. Creativity is difficult to nurture but easy to thwart: cut rules, not effort.

**Q10 [SC].** A professional's one skill is disrupted (injury, market change). How does the music chapter argue versatility helps? Give the two examples.
**A.** Versatility is insurance. When Pelegrina's teeth fell out in her sixties she switched from oboe back to violin and played into her seventies; when Django lost two fingers he re-invented his fret hand. Also use the "third instrument" rule: the modest investment in a third tool or domain paid off handsomely for the exceptional children.

## Section 5 - Chapter 4: Learning, Fast and Slow

**Q1 [SA].** Define "desirable difficulties" (Bjork, 1994) and list the four supported ones in the notes.
**A.** Obstacles that make learning more challenging, slower and more frustrating in the short term but better in the long term. The four: (1) the generation effect, (2) testing/self-testing, (3) spacing, (4) making-connections questions and interleaving. Judge by delayed performance, never in-session performance.

**Q2 [EX].** Explain the generation effect and the hypercorrection effect.
**A.** Struggling to produce an answer yourself, even a wrong one, enhances later learning; pre-testing works even when wrong answers are guaranteed. Hypercorrection: the more confident you were in a wrong answer, the better the correction sticks. So confident wrong answers are prime learning material.

**Q3 [SA].** What was the macaque hint experiment (Oberon and Macduff)? Give the numbers.
**A.** Two rhesus monkeys learned ordered lists of images under four conditions: automatic hints, hints on request, hints on half of attempts, no hints. With hints they used them and did well in three practice days. On day four hints were removed: Oberon got about a third right, Macduff under a fifth - and Macduff scored zero on lists practiced with automatic hints. On no-hint lists, terrible on day one but improving, Oberon got nearly three-quarters and Macduff about half on test day. Conclusion: "training with hints did not produce any lasting learning."

**Q4 [MC].** Which is the best description of interleaving?
(a) repeating one problem type until perfect (b) mixing problem types so the learner must first identify which strategy applies (c) studying in several rooms (d) reviewing once a week.
**A.** (b). Blocked practice is (a): excellent immediate performance, poor transfer. Heuristic: "When your intuition says block, you should probably interleave." Example: shuffle the Cézanne/Picasso/Renoir flashcards together.

**Q5 [EX].** What is spacing, and how do you know your spacing is long enough?
**A.** Leaving time between practice sessions on the same material - "deliberate not-practicing between bouts of deliberate practice." If self-testing feels easy, wait longer so the next test is harder; revisit later than feels comfortable.

**Q6 [SA].** What are the two question types in classrooms, and what happened to making-connections problems in US classrooms studied?
**A.** Using-procedures questions apply a just-learned procedure; making-connections questions ask why it works and where it generalizes. About 20% of questions in the US classrooms started as making-connections problems and 0% survived the teacher-student interaction, because excess hints converted them into procedures. The Japanese practice bansho (arraying multiple student solutions on the board to track conceptual connections) is the contrast.

**Q7 [EX].** Explain the Air Force Academy Calculus I finding.
**A.** Instructors who most boosted students' own exam scores and evaluations harmed students' performance in later courses; the deep-learning instructors were punished with worse ratings. So judging teachers by current scores and satisfaction selects for methods that teach less. Build evaluation that looks at downstream outcomes.

**Q8 [TF].** True or false: the fadeout in 67 early-childhood programs happened because closed skills were taught instead of open ones.
**A.** True. Closed skills are acquired quickly by repeating procedures and everyone gets them eventually; open skills scaffold later knowledge (e.g., hunting for and connecting contextual clues), so the advantage compounds. Fadeout = a temporary advantage that diminishes and vanishes.

**Q9 [SC].** A training director sees trainees performing brilliantly in sessions with lots of coaching hints and is delighted. What concept warns against celebrating?
**A.** The feeling of learning is decoupled from deep learning; hints are "a loan against future performance" (buy today's answer by selling tomorrow's understanding), as the macaques showed. Test delayed, unaided performance. Use Kornell's "make it easy to make it hard": engineer difficulty in deliberately.

**Q10 [SC].** A teacher is tempted to run a "platoon multiple choice" game and give hints when students stall. What should she do instead, and why?
**A.** "Platoon multiple choice" lets learners guess until the instructor's tone confirms, so they master interrogating *you*, not the concept. Instead pose making-connections problems and withhold hints so they survive the interaction; use generation before explanation and interleave. Also: brain-training apps and supplements crowd out the three strategies with backing - spacing, testing, making-connections questions - all of which impair short-term performance. Expect lower ratings while serving learners best. Expert problem solving in wicked settings is "evaluate then choose" rather than "choose then evaluate" (as in chess), and interleaving trains the former.

## Section 6 - Chapter 5: Thinking Outside Experience

**Q1 [SA].** What is deep analogical thinking (Dedre Gentner), and what is its failure mode?
**A.** Recognizing conceptual similarities across multiple domains or scenarios that seem to have little in common on the surface. Failure mode: a single close (surface) analogy is worse than none - in the movie-forecasting study, using only the single most analogous film collapsed predictive power.

**Q2 [EX].** Describe Kepler's route to his insight and the "eight minutes of arc."
**A.** Assigned Mars's baffling retrograde orbit, Kepler spent five years building an accurate model, then discarded it because two of Brahe's observations were off by eight minutes of arc (an eighth the width of a pinkie at arm's length): "If I had believed we could ignore these eight minutes, I would have patched my hypothesis accordingly." With no concept of gravity, force or momentum, he reasoned by analogy: smells and heat (dissipate with distance), light (exists at source and target - proof action at a distance is conceptually possible; then ruled out because motion continued through eclipses), magnets with poles, a whirlpool sweeping boats, boatmen steering across the current - each interrogated until it broke and generated the next question. He arrived at bodies pulling one another in proportion to size, predicted the moon's effect on tides (Galileo mocked him), and invented astrophysics.

**Q3 [SA].** What did Kevin Dunbar find in molecular biology labs?
**A.** The most productive labs offered a new analogy every four minutes, drawn from a wide variety of base domains. An E. coli-only lab, with uniformly specialized backgrounds and few analogies, spent weeks on a problem a mixed-background lab solved in one meeting, and was the one lab that produced no new findings. "A group of similar minded individuals will not provide more information to make analogies than a single individual." Breakthrough capacity scales with diversity of backgrounds ("Keplers by committee").

**Q4 [MC].** The "outside view" (vs. inside view) means:
(a) asking outsiders for opinions (b) estimating from the details of your own project (c) building a reference class of structurally similar cases and taking the base rate before adjusting for specifics (d) being pessimistic.
**A.** (c). Steps: describe your project and estimate; list other projects with broad *conceptual* similarity ignoring surface features; estimate each; revise yours against that class. The reference class (the full set of analogous cases, not the best match) is "the pillar of the outside view."

**Q5 [SA].** Give the infrastructure numbers and the Edinburgh example.
**A.** About 90% of major infrastructure projects go over budget, by 28% on average. Edinburgh's tram was rigorously costed from the inside at £320 million and was headed toward £1 billion. Detailed bottom-up expert estimates are the inside view, and more internal detail pushes judgments toward the extreme.

**Q6 [EX].** What is the Ambiguous Sorting Task?
**A.** Gentner's 25 cards describing real-world phenomena, each classifiable by domain (economics, biology) or by deep structure (positive-feedback loop, negative-feedback loop, causal chain). Forcing sorting by causal structure (economic bubbles with melting ice caps; sweating with Federal Reserve rate policy) trains structural thinking. People trained across domains sort by deep structure.

**Q7 [TF].** True or false: the first analogy that comes to mind is a reliable guide in a wicked problem.
**A.** False. The "kind-world hypothesis" assumes the analogy that pops to mind is apt because the environment repeats - "perfectly fine if you stay in the same village or the same savannah all your life." Stanford students primed with WWII chose war, primed with Vietnam chose diplomacy, on the identical scenario. Treat the first analogy as a bias, not an answer.

**Q8 [SA].** What did the business-student strategy study find about analogies?
**A.** Students believed the best strategy was a single same-industry example, but those given Nike and McDonald's (distant, multiple) generated more strategic options than those given Apple and Dell. "That's usually exactly the wrong way to go about it." Also: "problem solving begins with the typing of the problem" - successful solvers determine deep structure before matching a strategy; most solvers of the radiation problem summoned more medical knowledge instead of reaching outside the domain.

**Q9 [SC].** A project lead is asked to forecast the ROI on a new product she is deeply invested in. What does she do?
**A.** Use the outside view: first describe the project and estimate; then ignore its distinctive surface and list conceptually similar projects (reference class), estimate those, and revise. Treat extra internal detail as a warning, not a confidence signal. Insiders will object that their case is different - that is the inside view talking.

**Q10 [SC].** Which institutional step does the chapter give for supporting wide analogizing at a firm, and what team-staffing rule follows?
**A.** BCG's intranet of exhibits sorted by discipline, concept and strategic theme. Staff for variety of base domains - analogical range is a property of the group, not only the individual. Also treat an unexpected finding as a doorway, not an error, but only after ruling out your own miscalculation (Kepler's anomaly). Netflix example: predict by "who is this like?" (customers with similar histories), not "what is this?"

## Section 7 - Chapter 6: The Trouble with Too Much Grit

**Q1 [SA].** Define match quality and say why Epstein makes it the optimization target.
**A.** The degree of fit between the work someone does and who they are - their abilities and proclivities. Every course, job or assignment yields two returns: domain-specific skills and information about fit; optimize fit information early. Gains from better match quality compound faster than skill losses from switching ("the benefits to increased match quality outweigh the greater loss in skills").

**Q2 [EX].** Explain the multi-armed bandit model of careers and the phrase "young and foolish."
**A.** (Robert A. Miller) A gambler faces slot machines with unknown payoffs and must allocate pulls between testing and exploiting the best. Start with paths of the highest informational value: high-risk, high-reward, fast unforgiving feedback (athlete, actor, start-up founder). With the least experience you should buy information at the highest rate because a wrong pull costs least - so "young and foolish" is not foolish.

**Q3 [SA].** What is the Grit Scale, its two components, and which is problematic?
**A.** Duckworth's twelve-statement self-assessment combining work ethic/resilience and *consistency of interests* (knowing exactly what one wants). The second penalizes healthy re-specification of goals; Epstein and West Point graduates both score low on it.

**Q4 [EX].** What is restriction of range and how does it apply to West Point?
**A.** When subjects are preselected on a variable, that variable stops predicting within the group and other traits look inflated - like studying height's importance using only NBA players. The Whole Candidate Score (test scores, class rank, fitness, leadership) was the admissions filter, so it was useless for predicting Beast dropouts; Duckworth's Grit Scale beat it, but only 71 of 1,218 plebes dropped out that year. Her team noted preselected samples "necessarily limited the external validity."

**Q5 [SA].** Give the West Point retention figures and the ordering of retention by route.
**A.** Since the mid-1990s about half of West Point graduates leave active service at the first legal opportunity (five years) and three-quarters before the twenty-year pension mark - the highest attrition of any officer-training route. Retention runs inverse to investment: OCS trainees stay longest, then unfunded ROTC, then two-year, then three-year scholarships, then West Point and full-scholarship ROTC last.

**Q6 [MC].** The Army's $500 million retention bonus:
(a) solved attrition (b) was taken by officers already staying and refused by those already leaving (c) was never paid (d) raised grit scores.
**A.** (b). The problem was matching, not money. The alternative, the Officer Career Satisfaction Program (three extra service years in exchange for choice of branch or geographic post), got 4,000 cadets to extend in four years. A talent-matching market mechanism had existed in the private sector since the 1980s but not in the Army. The Strategic Studies Institute monograph diagnosed match quality, not a grit collapse.

**Q7 [EX].** How is Van Gogh used, and what is the "altered gospel"?
**A.** Van Gogh was an art dealer, teacher, bookseller, would-be pastor and itinerant preacher - each pursued with punishing work ethic and each abandoned; his biographer's Grit Scale answers put him at the 40th percentile. A multi-armed bandit at maximum intensity that zigzagged him somewhere no one had been. "Altered gospel" (Naifeh and Smith) is his pattern of total devotion followed by total disavowal; in this book the term is also used for the popularized, truncated grit message that drops caveats. Also Churchill's "never give in, never, never, never, never" ends "except to convictions of honor and good sense."

**Q8 [SA].** What is Seth Godin's advice and what is "informed switching"?
**A.** Enumerate your quit conditions in advance, before undertaking an endeavor, deciding what counts as a fit signal versus mere difficulty. Informed switching is leaving because new information revealed a poor fit, distinct from quitting under difficulty. Quitting is the purchase of information, not the forfeiture of investment.

**Q9 [SC].** An employee who has invested three years in a program is unhappy; her main argument for staying is time already spent. What concept applies and what does she do?
**A.** The sunk cost fallacy (continuing because of what is spent and gone; conmen ask for several small investments first). She asks, "what have I learned about myself since I chose this?" and checks her pre-written quit conditions. Supporting evidence: Scottish students forced to study broadly for two years switched careers less and caught up in income quickly; switchers take a skills hit but have higher growth rates; teachers who switch schools become more effective (Kirabo Jackson).

**Q10 [SC].** A CEO sees departing junior officers/managers as lacking drive and proposes bonuses. What would Epstein say?
**A.** Do not read turnover as lost drive: departing junior officers had "a strong drive for personal development" that changed their goals. Build a matching mechanism (internal mobility) rather than paying people to stay in a poor match. Use grit when the goal is given and measurable (Beast Barracks, an 800-meter season) and sampling when choosing the goal is the problem. Context: 85% of workers globally are not engaged or actively disengaged, so quitting takes more guts than being carried along.

## Section 8 - Chapter 7: Flirting with Your Possible Selves

**Q1 [SA].** What is "test-and-learn, not plan-and-implement" (Herminia Ibarra)?
**A.** Maximize match quality by sampling activities, social groups, contexts, jobs and careers, then reflecting and adjusting your personal narrative, and repeating. Procedure: notice dissatisfaction or a newly visible world; ask "Which among my various possible selves should I start to explore now? How can I do that?"; run a fast, cheap, temporary experiment; reflect, rewrite your story, choose the next short-term plan.

**Q2 [EX].** What is the end of history illusion?
**A.** Dan Gilbert's finding that we recognize how much we changed in the past but believe we'll barely change in the future - "works in progress claiming to be finished." So long-term commitments are bets on a stranger; personality changes most between eighteen and the late twenties.

**Q3 [SA].** What is the Dark Horse Project and its strategy?
**A.** Rose and Ogas's study of fulfilled high achievers with winding paths; 45 of the first 50 apologized for a path that was actually the norm. Dark horses practice short-term planning: "Here's who I am at the moment... which of these is the best match *right now*?" and form long-term goals only after a period of discovery. Ogas calls the cultural bargain trading exploration for early rigid goals the "standardization covenant."

**Q4 [MC].** Paul Graham's alternative to working back from a twenty-year goal is to:
(a) premature optimization (b) work forward from the options available now, choosing those that give the most promising range afterward (c) quit every two years (d) specialize at once.
**A.** (b). "Premature optimization" is Graham's name for the work-back-from-a-goal approach.

**Q5 [EX].** What is the context principle (if-then signatures)?
**A.** Traits express differently across situations; ask not whether someone is gritty or introverted but *when*. Diagnose a mismatch as a context problem before a character problem. Lack of drive in a poor context is not lack of grit; put the person in a fitting context and the drive reappears.

**Q6 [SA].** What is Ibarra's critique of quizzes and strengths-finders?
**A.** They freeze a moving target by introspection and "give people license to pigeonhole themselves." You cannot introspect your way to the right career because the person you are planning for does not exist yet.

**Q7 [EX].** Summarize Frances Hesselbein's path with key numbers.
**A.** Four professional positions, all president or CEO, never applied for one, tried to turn down three. At 34 she agreed to lead Girl Scout Troop 17 for six weeks and stayed eight years; she chaired the local United Way as a volunteer (Bethlehem Steel and the steelworkers' union; Johnstown led the nation in per capita giving); at 54 she took her first professional job to fix the council's finances for six months and stayed four years; at 60 she went to the national CEO interview only to decline in person, relaxed because she didn't want it, and described circular management, four age-targeted handbooks and diversity as the primary problem. In thirteen years as CEO: minority membership tripled, a quarter million members added, cookie business past $300 million; Drucker named her best CEO in the country. "I was unaware that I was being prepared."

**Q8 [TF].** True or false: the best response to feeling behind peers who started earlier is to commit to a long-term goal quickly.
**A.** False. Optimize for the present match, expect to switch, and form long-term goals only after discovery. Fulfilled long-term goals are outputs of exploration, not inputs to it.

**Q9 [SC].** A 27-year-old is offered a temporary "filling in" role outside her field. Should she take it?
**A.** Yes, in the book's logic: say yes to temporary and "just filling in" roles - the cheapest match-quality tests. Hesselbein's accumulated capabilities (resourcefulness from photography, shared leadership from the troop, stakeholder alignment from United Way) resolved into a career only in retrospect. Reflect after each experiment ("I know who I am when I see what I do") and treat each experience as "a big basket."

**Q10 [SC].** A student is told "don't give up on your dreams" and to enter a credential program now. What warning applies?
**A.** Committing to a credential before knowing the fit is riskier (nothing wrong with a law or medical degree itself); treating an early plan as binding is premature optimization against a world whose jobs may not exist yet. Prefer the option that maximizes the range of promising options afterward, and treat yourself as "a scientist of yourself" running small testable questions.

## Section 9 - Chapter 8: The Outsider Advantage

**Q1 [SA].** What did Karim Lakhani find about InnoCentive problems?
**A.** "The further the problem was from the solver's expertise, the more likely they were to solve it." Just over a third of challenges were completely solved, despite all having stumped the specialists who posted them. The more diverse the array of solvers a challenge attracted, the more likely it was to be solved.

**Q2 [EX].** What is "outside-in thinking" (Alph Bingham) and how do you run it?
**A.** Finding solutions in experiences far outside focused training for the problem. Steps: post the problem publicly rather than routing it to the domain team; strip trade-secret specifics so it can be shared; frame it to appeal to attorneys, dentists and mechanics as well as scientists; accept solutions by a "different methodology." Caution: over-specifying in insider vocabulary re-constrains it to the home field.

**Q3 [SA].** Define local search, Einstellung effect, and "Russian nesting dolls."
**A.** Local search: attacking problems with specialists from one domain using methods that worked before. Einstellung effect: employing only familiar methods even when better ones exist. Russian nesting dolls: as specialists subdivide into sub-subspecialties, escaping one box lands you in a slightly larger one. Pedro Domingos: knowledge is a double-edged sword that lets you do some things but blinds you to others.

**Q4 [EX].** What is undiscovered public knowledge (Don Swanson)?
**A.** Making discoveries by connecting information across subspecialty literatures that never cite one another and share no collaborators. Swanson found "eleven neglected connections" between magnesium deficiency and migraine; by 2012 the American Headache Society and American Academy of Neurology concluded magnesium should be considered a common migraine treatment, on evidence as strong as ibuprofen's. He built Arrowsmith so others could do this.

**Q5 [SA].** Summarize Jill Viles's two diagnoses.
**A.** A part-time substitute teacher in Iowa with muscular dystrophy, she read a *Muscle and Nerve* paper on Emery-Dreifuss, recognized her father's "Popeye arm" and her own contractures; a Des Moines neurologist refused to look. She insisted on cardiac monitoring for her 45-year-old father, whose pulse dropped into the twenties - emergency pacemaker surgery saved him; an Italian group confirmed an LMNA mutation in 1999. Later, from photographs of Olympic hurdler Priscilla Lopes-Schliep, she recognized the pattern of missing fat; Abhimanyu Garg's test confirmed the same Dunnigan-type partial lipodystrophy (neighboring "typos" on the same gene), Priscilla having three times normal blood fat. Cold-contacting hyperspecialist Etienne Lefai (SREBP1) led him to say "Now, I have changed the path of my team."

**Q6 [MC].** Why did Eli Lilly's scientists demand posted problems be pulled?
(a) cost (b) confidentiality reflex: "Why the hell do you think anyone other than us can solve that problem?" (c) legal ruling (d) low solver count.
**A.** (b). Bingham's counter: try it on problems giving away no trade secret; if it fails, no harm.

**Q7 [TF].** True or false: accelerating specialization shrinks the outsider's opportunity.
**A.** False. It increases it - the more information specialists create, the more unconnected strands exist for a dilettante to merge. Hence the "growing surface area" model of knowledge.

**Q8 [SA].** What is the role of distant analogy in this chapter?
**A.** Abandon your specialty's default move and re-describe the problem as something from ordinary life (a slushy, chewing gum on a leg, tear gas), then search that domain for the mechanism. Davis: "you want to do as little chemistry as possible." NASA initially resisted Cragin's answer "because it was using a different methodology" - credentialing the solution rather than testing it.

**Q9 [SC].** Your best specialists have been stuck on a problem for years. What do you do, and why would insiders resist?
**A.** Change the solver population, not the technique: broadcast an abstracted problem outside the field and expect winners from the margins. Insiders will resist through the confidentiality reflex and the belief that "we have every training and resource advantage" - which the notes call a warning sign of local search. "If it were solvable inside the industry, it would already be solved."

**Q10 [SC].** A department makes scholars feel guilty for not being "more specialized." Respond using this chapter.
**A.** That is the anti-pattern of elevating narrowness as an ideal. Specialists are enormously valuable (hyperspecialist Lefai) once someone supplies the connecting question, but the interfaces between literatures are where undiscovered knowledge sits; protect access to non-specialist experience, the raw material of the outsider advantage.

## Section 10 - Chapter 9: Lateral Thinking with Withered Technology

**Q1 [SA].** State Gunpei Yokoi's philosophy and its famous summary line.
**A.** Put cheap, simple, thoroughly understood technology to use in ways no one else considered: "If he could not think more deeply about new technologies, he decided, he would think more broadly about old ones." Withered technology is tech old enough to be extremely well understood, easily available, and free of specialist requirements.

**Q2 [EX].** Walk through Yokoi's four-step method.
**A.** (1) Pick technology old enough to be well understood and available. (2) Strip functions to the minimum that still delivers the experience (the Lefty RX: single-channel, could only turn left, a tenth of the cost). (3) Compete on user experience - durability, portability, battery life, price. (4) Exploit that developers and users already know the old tech, so their creativity is unencumbered.

**Q3 [SA].** Give the Game Boy and Game & Watch facts.
**A.** Game & Watch reused LCD screens Sharp had commoditized in the calculator wars, with the display gap solved by embossing dots using tweaked hanafuda card printing machines; it sold 43.4 million. The 1989 Game Boy ran a 1970s-era processor, showed four grayscale shades on a screen tinted between mucus and old alfalfa, and smeared in fast motion; told a competitor had launched, Yokoi asked only if it had a color screen - "Then we're fine." It sold 118.7 million, the bestselling console of the twentieth century, on price, pocketability, near-indestructibility and days of AA battery life. Love Tester: a transistor and a store-bought galvanometer.

**Q4 [TF].** True or false: the Virtual Boy failed because Yokoi was too faithful to withered technology.
**A.** False; it was the opposite. His greatest failure came when he departed from his own tenets and used experimental technology, whose radio emissions forced a metal plate, whose weight forced a tabletop unit, and whose posture nobody wanted. Also his first toy, Drive Game, failed because its mechanism was so advanced it was fragile, expensive and defect-ridden.

**Q5 [MC].** Ouderkirk's 3M study found which type most likely to succeed and win the Carlton Award?
(a) specialists (b) generalists (c) polymaths: a core area of depth (less than specialists) plus more breadth than generalists (d) managers.
**A.** (c). Specialists had deep, narrow patent classes; generalists were broad with no depth and bored in one area. Grow the polymath profile by working across teams and technology classes and accepting some loss of depth. Specialists suit well-defined problems; as ambiguity rises, breadth matters more.

**Q6 [EX].** Explain Dyson's birds and frogs and the T-shaped/I-shaped/pi-shaped vocabulary.
**A.** Birds survey broad vistas and unify concepts; frogs live in the mud and solve particular problems deeply; "we need birds and frogs working together." T-shaped people (breadth plus one depth) go to I-shaped people (single narrow depth) with questions "to create the trunk for the T" (Jayshree Seth: mosaic building). Pi-shaped people dive in and out of multiple specialties and look like "round pegs to the square holes" in mechanistic HR screens.

**Q7 [SA].** What do serial innovators look like (Abbie Griffin) and what is a "network of enterprise"?
**A.** Modern Edisons: high tolerance for ambiguity, systems thinkers, peripheral technical knowledge, repurposers, wide readers with many hobbies; they said they would not be hired under their companies' current practices. Network of enterprise (Howard Gruber on Darwin): many simultaneous projects, "a bunch of bobbers hanging in the water that have little thoughts attached to them."

**Q8 [SA].** What did the comics study and the Melero-Palomeras study find?
**A.** In comics, high-repetition workload hurt performance, years of experience had no impact, and publisher resources didn't help; breadth of genres drove creative output. Melero and Palomeras: in high-uncertainty domains (which produce both useless patents and blockbusters), high-breadth team members mattered most. Note: one broad "super" individual is more innovative than a team with the same collective range.

**Q9 [SC].** A small firm cannot match rivals' raw technical specs. Which approach applies?
**A.** Lateral thinking with withered technology: do not compete in computing power (Yokoi: the shortcut for a lack of ideas, which screen makers and graphics experts inevitably win). Compete on the barrier users actually face - complexity and price. Christensen's "empowering innovation" example is the Wii: the barrier was complexity of play, not graphics quality. Adopt "producer, not engineer": hold vague knowledge of everything, source depth from specialists, and blurt out crazy ideas so junior people never learn to say "it's not really my place to say."

**Q10 [SC].** A hiring manager screens with a mechanistic rubric that produces highly reproducible results. What is the risk?
**A.** It screens out serial innovators and pi-shaped people. Also, consensus that something is impossible should be treated as a signal of originality (Ouderkirk: agreement means your chance of being first is "precisely zero"). Test expert verdicts with the "adjacent stuff" test: the optics author "knew his stuff. The problem is, he didn't know the adjacent stuff." Jayshree Seth's story: advice to stay in a field she disliked because she'd already started is the sunk cost fallacy in career specialization.

## Section 11 - Chapter 10: Fooled by Expertise

**Q1 [SA].** Define foxes and hedgehogs (Tetlock, after Berlin) and the key accuracy finding.
**A.** Hedgehogs "know one big thing" and toil devotedly inside one specialty; foxes "know many little things" and range across disciplines, accepting ambiguity and contradiction. Hedgehogs cherry-pick details that fit their all-encompassing theory, so more information makes them worse; they are outstanding at predicting the past, "dart-throwing chimps" at predicting the future, and make great TV, hence a "perverse inverse relationship" between fame and accuracy.

**Q2 [EX].** Tell the Ehrlich-Simon bet and its lessons.
**A.** Biologist Paul Ehrlich (*The Population Bomb*) bet economist Julian Simon that five metals would rise in price as overpopulation depleted resources; in 1990 Simon collected $576.07 because all prices fell. Both declared vindication and dug in, yet Ehrlich would have won the same bet in 62% of ten-year windows since 1900; commodity prices were a bad proxy and a recession moved them. Ehrlich was right about environmental degradation and wrong about famine; Simon right about ingenuity and wrong to credit markets for air and water gains that came from regulation. Two hedgehogs grew more dogmatic and less accurate.

**Q3 [SA].** Give the numbers from Tetlock's twenty-year study.
**A.** 82,361 forecasts. The average expert was a horrific forecaster; "impossible" events happened 15% of the time and "sure things" failed over a quarter of the time. Fox integrators who held both that Gorbachev was a genuine reformer and that the USSR had lost legitimacy outside Russia were the only ones to see the collapse coming. A "curiously inverse relationship" existed between how well forecasters thought they were doing and how well they did.

**Q4 [MC].** Active open-mindedness (Jonathan Baron) means:
(a) agreeing with everyone (b) treating your own ideas as hypotheses in need of testing (c) never taking positions (d) deferring to experts.
**A.** (b). Aim to get teammates to help you falsify your notions; cross disciplines hunting for contrary evidence. Default humans produce "myside" ideas and never search for why they might be wrong; two-thirds of one study's participants declined payment rather than read counterarguments. Kahan's "science curiosity" (appetite for new evidence) differs from science knowledge, which increases dogmatism.

**Q5 [SA].** Who are superforecasters, what is the Good Judgment Project, and what are superteams?
**A.** Ordinary volunteers with wide-ranging interests and no relevant credentials who beat intelligence analysts with classified access. GJP was Tetlock and Mellers's team in the IARPA tournament, built by open call and weighted toward the foxiest forecasters. Superteams: small teams of foxes whose members became about 50% more accurate individually; they beat crowd wisdom and prediction markets. Practice "extremely polite antagonism" and "foxes with dragonfly eyes."

**Q6 [EX].** Why is "good judges are good belief updaters" notable, and what does flip-flopping signal?
**A.** Good forecasters embrace the logic of a loss as they would a win and change positions constantly - a competence signal, unlike politicians. Anti-patterns: claiming total victories and near-miss defeats (unfalsifiable) and updating in the wrong direction (some hedgehogs became more convinced after wildly wrong predictions).

**Q7 [TF].** True or false: an hour of foxy training measurably improved accuracy by teaching forecasters to generate structurally similar events before looking at details.
**A.** True per the notes: the outside view via structural analogies; "no country has ever left the eurozone" still has analogues in failed negotiations and forced currency conversions. Uniqueness is a matter of degree; starting with the inside view is dangerous.

**Q8 [SA].** What did Scott Eastman and Ellen Cousins contribute?
**A.** Eastman found his own deep-study areas (Russian) were among his weakest forecasts: "if you're too much of an insider, it's hard to get good perspective." Cousins' rule: take facts from experts, not conclusions, as they may have blinders on. Also mistaking a proxy for the thing - picking a metric that will "prove" your worldview but has little to do with it.

**Q9 [SC].** A founder with deep domain expertise is making a prediction about a political shift. List three steps.
**A.** (1) Ask whether you are reasoning from one big idea or many little ones, and import a discipline you don't work in. (2) Build the reference class of structurally similar events before specifics. (3) Score yourself, especially on misses, creating feedback the wicked domain won't supply; and state what would change your mind - if you can't, you're a hedgehog on that topic.

**Q10 [SC].** A team leader hiring forecasters has a choice between credentialed subject experts and curious generalists. What does the chapter recommend?
**A.** Recruit for range and curiosity, not credentials; run the team on polite antagonism with individual predictions and collective scoring (superteams). Remember more information about your own specialty is fuel for cherry-picking, and treat confidence as inversely informative.

## Section 12 - Chapter 11: Learning to Drop Your Familiar Tools

**Q1 [SA].** What is "dropping your familiar tools" (Karl Weick) and what is overlearned behavior?
**A.** "Dropping one's tools is a proxy for unlearning, for adaptation, for flexibility." Under pressure in unfamiliar situations, expert groups "regress to what they know best." Overlearned behavior is a response repeated so often it is no longer recognized as a situation-specific tool: Quentin Rhoades did not realize he still carried his saw any more than he realized he had arms. There are no tools that cannot be dropped, reimagined, or repurposed.

**Q2 [EX].** Explain the Carter Racing exercise and its twist.
**A.** Harvard MBAs get a fictional team with seven engine failures in twenty-four races deciding whether to race at 40 degrees F. Most vote to race. Next day the professor reveals he offered the missing data four times and nobody asked; plotting all races shows every race below 65 degrees failed, and logistic regression gives 99.4% failure probability at 40 degrees. The numbers are Challenger's O-ring data, taught for thirty years as a lesson about incomplete data.

**Q3 [EX].** What is Epstein's twist about the Challenger data?
**A.** Seventeen flights had no O-ring exposure (the insulating putty held) and so carried zero information; of the seven exposure flights, five showed erosion (not temperature-related, not life-threatening) and only two showed blow-by. Thiokol's engineers had two relevant data points, not twenty-four. Allan McDonald: looking at only the relevant data actually supported NASA's position. Count the *relevant* data points, not the available ones.

**Q4 [SA].** What was Boisjoly's real evidence and why was it inadmissible?
**A.** Qualitative: a thin gray soot streak at 75 degrees versus jet-black soot fanned across the joint at 53 degrees. Asked to quantify his concern, he could say only that it was "away from goodness." NASA's excellent quantitative culture, which had returned twenty-four flights safely, rendered the argument inadmissible. Other engineers agreed but stayed silent: "if I don't have data to back me up, the boss's opinion is better than mine." Feynman: "When you don't have any data, you have to use reason."

**Q5 [MC].** The 334-institution finding on congruence:
(a) congruence strongly predicted success (b) congruence had no measurable effect on success (c) incongruence harmed success (d) it was never tested.
**A.** (b). Incongruence (Patil, Tetlock, Mellers; subtitle "Balancing the Risks of Mindless Conformity and Reckless Deviation") turned non-learners into learning machines when injected: process-conformist groups got individualism and dissent; improvisers got loyalty and cohesion. It produces "ambidextrous thought."

**Q6 [SA].** Define process vs. outcome accountability and the Monday Notes.
**A.** Process accountability is being judged on following the method; outcome accountability on results; each alone has a distinct failure mode (process alone breeds conformity and defensibility: NASA's process "culminated with more concern for being able to defend a decision than with using all available information to make the right one"). Monday Notes (von Braun): one page of salient issues from every engineer, annotated by hand and circulated to all; William Lucas turned them into upward-only standardized forms and quality collapsed.

**Q7 [EX].** What are sensemaking and "chain of command is not chain of communication"?
**A.** Paul Gleason: "If I make a decision, it is a possession... If I make sense, then this is more dynamic and I listen and I can change it." Give decisive direction with rationale plus an explicit note that the plan is ripe for revision (hunches held lightly). Rex Geveden: "I will not intercept your decisions that belong in your chain of command, but I will give and receive information anywhere in the organization, at any time." Also go prospecting for doubts informally; Gene Kranz: hear the same hunch twice and investigate without data.

**Q8 [SA].** What did Captain Tony Lesmes do at Bagram?
**A.** Faced with ambiguous intel on a mass-casualty rescue, he dropped the most sacred PJ tool - going with his team - to save helicopter space for patients, floated the idea as a hypothesis he wanted disproved, absorbed the anger, and got seven wounded soldiers out in one trip.

**Q9 [SC].** An engineer has a hunch about a launch risk but cannot quantify it. What should the organization have in place?
**A.** An admissibility path for qualitative evidence before it's needed; never require a doubt to be quantified before it counts. Silence must not be read as consent, avoid the "offline caucus" where dissent gets resolved into conformity, and hold informal doubt-prospecting. Oculostenotic reflex ("if you see a blockage, you'll reflexively fix a blockage") illustrates tool-defined specialists; "when all you have is a volcanologist, every extinction looks like a volcano."

**Q10 [SC].** A leader notices that her culture is admirably consistent and aligned. What does the chapter recommend?
**A.** Treat consistency as the warning sign, not the achievement. Identify the dominant culture and push the opposite way; use the "would I feel naked without this?" detector (Mulloy without numbers, Rhoades without his saw, Lesmes's PJs without togetherness). Swap "can do" conformity for "make do" improvisation outside usual bounds; Toynbee: there is no master-key; collect a toolshed. The Columbia board concluded NASA was not a learning organization given the near-identical causes of the two disasters.

## Section 13 - Chapter 12: Deliberate Amateurs

**Q1 [SA].** What are Saturday morning (Smithies) and Friday night (Geim) experiments?
**A.** A standing block of protected, unfunded, unrationalized play balancing the week's standard practice with wide-roaming exploration: "a pinch of this, a dash of that"; "On Saturday, you don't have to be completely rational." Expect most to amount to nothing; it lets the brain work on something different so unrelated memories become available. Geim's run produced graphene and a Nobel a decade after an Ig Nobel for levitating a frog.

**Q2 [EX].** Tell the gel electrophoresis story.
**A.** On Saturday, January 23, 1954 in a Toronto lab, Oliver Smithies was stuck: insulin stuck to moist paper used for separation and the children's hospital's starch-grain alternative required slicing and analyzing fifty sections. A memory from age twelve in Halifax - his mother starching his father's collars, the cooled starch congealing into a jelly - led him to raid supply closets with a skeleton key, cook starch into a gel, run current through it, and watch insulin molecules separate by size. His notebook: "Very promising!" It became gel electrophoresis. He never found the insulin precursor, switched from medicine to chemistry on a whim, took a sabbatical in his fifties to move two floors to learn DNA work, wrote his most important paper around sixty, and won the Nobel as a geneticist.

**Q3 [SA].** What does "deliberate amateur" mean and who are Geim and Casadevall?
**A.** Sarah Lewis: "amateur" comes from the Latin for one who adores an endeavor; "Breakthroughs often occur when you start down a road, but wander off for a ways and pretend as if you have just begun." Geim: "I do not dig deep - I graze shallow," switching subject about every five years; "not interested in doing re-search, only search." Casadevall's black-mold-eating-radiation project began with a news article about a Chernobyl robot; he proposes the R3 Initiative (Rigor, Responsibility, Reproducibility) to despecialize graduate training with courses like "How Do We Know What Is True?" and "Anatomy of Scientific Error" at the same total time.

**Q4 [MC].** Uzzi's finding across 18 million papers:
(a) hit papers were fully unconventional (b) hit papers were mostly conventional combinations with one atypical combination injected (c) interdisciplinary papers are never cited (d) age predicts impact.
**A.** (b). A paper citing two journals never before cited together occurs in one in ten papers; it's slower to be published and noticed but far likelier to be top-1% cited fifteen years on (expect about three years of being ignored). Also across 10,000 researchers there is no standard relationship between experience and contribution: the most impactful paper is as likely to be the last as the first.

**Q5 [SA].** What is the principle of limited sloppiness and what is "new skills to an old problem or a new problem to old skills"?
**A.** Delbruck: be careful not to be too careful, or you will unconsciously limit exploration (sloppy enough that unexpected things can happen, not so sloppy you can't tell what happened). Smithies: "Don't end up a clone of your thesis adviser. Take your skills to a place that's not doing the same sort of thing."

**Q6 [EX].** What are porous boundaries and stagnant teams?
**A.** Porous boundaries are the "universal" feature of creative ecosystems - individuals moving easily between teams across organizational and disciplinary lines. Stagnant teams are repeat-collaborator clusters: efficient, comfortable, creatively dead (1920s Broadway had the star names and a 90% flop rate). Bill Gore's "dabble time" and noticing firms do their best creative work in a crisis when boundaries collapse.

**Q7 [SA].** Name the withered-technology cases in this chapter and Tu Youyou's three no's.
**A.** Starch (Smithies), cyclostyling (Southern blot), and a fourth-century Chinese wormwood recipe (Tu Youyou's artemisinin). "Professor of the three no's": no academy membership, no foreign research experience, no postgraduate degree. Also "NBGBOKFO": "no bloody good, but OK for Oliver," the label on broken equipment left for Smithies.

**Q8 [TF].** True or false: scientists should be rushed to specialize because it improves their ability to detect bad work.
**A.** False. Rushing scientists to specialize before they learn to think leaves them unable to produce good work or detect bad or fraudulent work; retractions have outpaced publication. Ohsumi's warning that his discovery couldn't happen today under demands for immediate application; the Senate subcommittee that classed biology, geology, economics and archaeology as distractions; physicians who can't compute a 2% post-test probability are "unprimed," not innumerate.

**Q9 [SC].** A lab head must justify time on "pointless" side projects to leadership. What arguments does the chapter supply?
**A.** Curiosity-driven research is inventory: society had retrovirus knowledge "off the shelf" when HIV arrived, which is why 1981 to effective therapy took fifteen years. Protect lunch ("the best time to bounce ideas"), value the interface between specialties nobody is funded to defend, and note that grazing is unfundable by proposal and mostly unproductive, so judge the portfolio.

**Q10 [SC].** A specialist feels "spread thin." How does the chapter reframe it?
**A.** "Spread thin" and "going for breadth" are leading indicators, not deficits - Novoselov looked like he was wasting his life until he became the youngest physics Nobelist in forty years. Use expatriate arbitrage: move an idea from where it's common to where it's rare and valued. Read outside your field every day.

## Section 14 - Conclusion: Expanding Your Range

**Q1 [SA].** What is Epstein's one sentence of advice?
**A.** "Don't feel behind." Compare yourself with yourself yesterday, not with younger people who aren't you; feeling behind supplies no information, only cost.

**Q2 [EX].** Why is the Tiger narrative attractive and what is wrong with it?
**A.** It is tidy, low on uncertainty, high on efficiency, and told in retrospect as an orderly journey from A to B; examined in depth or over time, such stories get murkier. Retrospective innovation stories omit the detours.

**Q3 [SA].** What are Simonton's finding and Simmons's truncated vs. untruncated distributions?
**A.** The more work eminent creators produced, the more duds *and* the higher their odds of a supernova success. In baseball the best swing scores four (truncated); in the wider world an occasional at-bat scores a thousand (untruncated). Alph Bingham: breakthrough and fallacy look a lot alike initially.

**Q4 [EX].** Tell the Warshaw story.
**A.** Atari designer Howard Scott Warshaw used constrained technology resourcefully to build *Yar's Revenge*, the bestselling original 2600 title while Atari was the fastest-growing company in US history. The same year, same method, he designed the *E.T.* adaptation, called the biggest commercial failure in video game history and blamed for Atari's collapse. Same designer, method and year; opposite tails.

**Q5 [SA].** Name three other "same creator, opposite tails" examples and the late-start examples.
**A.** Edison's thousand-plus patents (mostly unimportant) around the light bulb and phonograph; Shakespeare's *Timon of Athens* between *King Lear* and *Macbeth*; Rachel Whiteread winning both the Turner Prize and the "Anti-Turner Prize" in one year. Late starts: Richter began formal piano lessons at 22; Nash got a basketball at 13 and won two NBA MVPs. Caesar wept at a statue of Alexander, peaked early and got murdered.

**Q6 [MC].** "Plan experiments, not plans" (via Ibarra) and "approach projects like Michelangelo approached marble" both mean:
(a) never plan (b) be willing to learn and adjust as you go, even abandon a goal and change direction (c) work harder (d) specialize earlier.
**A.** (b). Run your personal version of Friday night/Saturday morning experiments.

**Q7 [TF].** True or false: Epstein argues specialization is the enemy.
**A.** False: nothing is inherently wrong with it; we all specialize at some point. The target is *premature* specialization sold as a shortcut. Breadth is a complement: a diverse group of specialists cannot fully replace broad individuals.

**Q8 [EX].** What does "abandoned domains as retained capital" mean?
**A.** Even when you move on from an area entirely, that experience isn't wasted. Treat direction changes as information, not sunk cost.

**Q9 [SC].** A 35-year-old compares herself to a 24-year-old founder and considers abandoning a mid-course pivot. Advise her.
**A.** Don't benchmark against others' timelines or highlight reels; convert anxiety into scheduled experiments; budget for duds because dud rate and hit rate rise together; judge the path by its outcome distribution over time, not its most recent result. Tolerance for failure is a production quota, not a slogan. Match quality is found by experimentation.

**Q10 [SC].** A university offers an early-specialization "life hack" program for sport and music. What does the Conclusion say about those domains?
**A.** The "kind" domains that market early specialization (sport, music) don't actually support it (Ch 3 and 4 evidence in the notes' cross-references). Delayed specialization is the norm among eventually elite athletes; head start fervor demands you decide what to be before figuring out who you are.

## FINAL EXAM - 20 Cumulative Questions

Try the whole exam closed-book, mixed order. Score 2/1/0 per question (max 40). Below 28, revisit the sections cited in the answers.

**F1 [SA].** State the book's whole thesis in two sentences, using the kind/wicked distinction.
**A.** The 10,000-hours / early-specialization model is real but narrow: it holds in kind learning environments (repeating patterns, clear stable rules, fast accurate feedback) and fails in wicked ones, which describe most modern work and life. In wicked domains, breadth, late specialization and analogical thinking outperform accumulated domain hours. (Intro, Ch 1)

**F2 [MC].** Which pair is correctly matched? (a) Chunking - Luria (b) Fermi-izing - Gentner (c) Interleaving - Bjork's desirable difficulties (d) Outside view - Yokoi.
**A.** (c). Chunking is Chase and Simon; Fermi-izing is the decomposition method from Ch 2; the outside view is Kahneman and Tversky; Yokoi is withered technology. (Ch 1, 2, 4, 5, 9)

**F3 [EX].** Why does the same pattern-based expertise that makes a grandmaster great also make her replaceable?
**A.** Chess is kind and pattern-dependent (chunking); what is pattern-dependent is machine-replaceable. Deep Blue beat Kasparov; centaurs and amateurs with ordinary computers beat supercomputers and grandmaster teams; tactics are outsourced while strategy and integration are where humans add. (Ch 1)

**F4 [SC].** *Cross-chapter synthesis.* A manager must decide whether to promote the most senior "expert" to forecast next year's market or to build a team of curious generalists. Which concepts from at least three chapters apply, and what does she do?
**A.** The domain is wicked, so experience yields confidence not accuracy (Ch 1); hedgehogs get worse as they learn more, foxes with belief updating win, superteams use polite antagonism (Ch 10); the outside view and reference classes (Ch 5) and incongruence/doubt-prospecting (Ch 11). She builds a diverse team, scores forecasts including misses, and requires everyone to state what would change their mind.

**F5 [TF].** True or false: the music evidence (figlie del coro; Sloboda) shows that sampling is a detour that eventually pays back.
**A.** False in the book's framing: the sampling period is integral to elite development, not a detour to be excised. The exceptional musicians had taken fewer lessons and practiced less but spread effort over about three instruments. (Intro, Ch 3)

**F6 [SA].** What is the common mechanism behind "breadth of training predicts breadth of transfer," interleaving and far transfer?
**A.** Varied contexts and mixed problem types force the learner to identify the deep structure of a problem and build abstract models rather than memorizing a single instance; this reduces in-session performance but raises durable, transferable learning. (Ch 3, 4)

**F7 [EX].** Reconcile the facts that grit predicts West Point dropout and that grit research can mislead about quitting.
**A.** The Grit Scale beat the Whole Candidate Score at predicting Beast dropouts (71 of 1,218), but the population was already filtered (restriction of range) and the goal was given. Where choosing the goal is the problem, quitting is the mechanism that finds match quality; half of West Point graduates leave at five years because they discover their options. (Ch 6)

**F8 [SC].** *Synthesis.* A 24-year-old is unsure about career direction and feels behind. Combine ideas from the Intro, Ch 6, Ch 7 and the Conclusion into a plan.
**A.** Don't feel behind (Conclusion); late starts are fine (founders at 45-50; Intro). Treat the next step as a cheap, reversible test of a possible self (test-and-learn, Ch 7); write quit conditions in advance and favor high-information, fast-feedback paths (multi-armed bandit, Ch 6); expect to change more than you predict (end of history illusion). Reflect after each experiment and rewrite the story.

**F9 [MC].** Which anecdote best shows the danger of over-relying on a quantitative tool in a wicked situation? (a) Oberon and Macduff (b) Roger Boisjoly and NASA (c) Jill Viles (d) Anson Williams.
**A.** (b). Quantitative culture made his qualitative evidence inadmissible; and Epstein shows Thiokol had only two relevant data points. (Ch 11)

**F10 [SA].** Name three distinct "outsider" success cases from different chapters and what each outsider had that insiders lacked.
**A.** Anson Williams (freestyle chess; real-time strategy skill, no rating; Ch 1); Jill Viles (visual pattern recognition from lived experience across conditions; Ch 8); Tu Youyou (no academy, no foreign experience, no postgraduate degree; a fourth-century wormwood recipe; Ch 12). Others: Kepler's analogies (Ch 5), InnoCentive solvers (Ch 8).

**F11 [EX].** Explain how Fermi-izing (Ch 2), reference classes (Ch 5) and active open-mindedness (Ch 10) are all "conceptual Swiss Army knife" tools.
**A.** Each is a domain-general reasoning method that travels across fields: decomposing unknowns, anchoring on structurally similar cases' base rates, and treating beliefs as hypotheses to falsify. They are the "think before deciding what to think about" skills that transfer.

**F12 [SC].** A company leader is choosing between investing in cutting-edge technology to beat rivals and repurposing mature, cheap technology. Which chapters inform the choice and what are the cautions?
**A.** Ch 9 (withered technology: compete on user experience and ecosystem; the Virtual Boy as the failure when departing from tenets), Ch 12 (starch, cyclostyling, wormwood as withered tools). Cautions: the doctrine reads as unambitious; specialists mock the specs; and the reverse failure is abandoning the doctrine for novel tech you don't understand.

**F13 [TF].** True or false: in Ch 5 and Ch 10, a single very close analogy is a good starting point for forecasting.
**A.** False. A single near analogy is worse than none (movie-forecasting study); use multiple, distant analogies and a full reference class. (Ch 5, 10)

**F14 [SA].** Give three numerical facts you can recall about forecasting, hints, and infrastructure.
**A.** 82,361 forecasts; "impossible" happened 15% and "sure things" failed over a quarter (Ch 10). Macduff scored zero on lists practiced with automatic hints (Ch 4). About 90% of major infrastructure projects go over budget, by 28% on average (Ch 5).

**F15 [EX].** How do "incongruence" (Ch 11) and "porous boundaries" (Ch 12) address the same problem as the "parallel trenches" image (Intro)?
**A.** All three concern the dangers of homogeneous, siloed, aligned specialists. Incongruence injects opposing forces; porous boundaries enable movement across teams and fields; parallel trenches describe the failure when nobody looks into the next trench. The cure is cross-boundary contact and communication.

**F16 [SC].** *Synthesis.* A school wants to raise test scores this term and is considering heavy hints, blocked drilling and early specialization tracks. Using Ch 3, 4 and the Intro, evaluate.
**A.** Hints and blocking raise in-session performance but not durable learning (macaques; Air Force calculus); fadeout shows closed-skill head starts vanish (67 early-childhood programs); early heavy structure was associated with "average" outcomes in music. Use generation, spacing, testing and interleaving, keep making-connections questions alive, and judge by delayed tests.

**F17 [MC].** Match each phrase to its source: "Test-and-learn" / "Hunches held lightly" / "Adjacent stuff" / "Graze shallow."
**A.** Test-and-learn - Ibarra (Ch 7); hunches held lightly - Gleason's sensemaking (Ch 11); adjacent stuff - Ouderkirk on the optics author (Ch 9); graze shallow - Geim (Ch 12).

**F18 [EX].** The notes say breakthroughs are "high variance." Give two examples from different chapters and the managerial implication.
**A.** Warshaw's *Yar's Revenge* and *E.T.* the same year (Conclusion); Smithies and Geim's mostly fruitless play producing rare field-defining results (Ch 12); Edison's patents. Implication: judge the portfolio and the process, not any single attempt, and budget for duds.

**F19 [SA].** What does the cheatsheet say about when to specialize now versus keep sampling?
**A.** Specialize now when the environment is kind with stable rules, the skill ceiling is age-limited (elite sport, music virtuosity), you have tested the fit against alternatives, feedback is fast and unambiguous, and switching costs are rising steeply. Keep sampling when the environment is wicked or shifting, there is no hard age ceiling, fit is untested or inherited, feedback is delayed or noisy, and switching costs are still low.

**F20 [EX].** If a friend remembered only one idea from the book, what should it be, and how would you defend it using evidence from three chapters?
**A.** Sample to find match quality and don't feel behind (Conclusion) - or equivalently "diagnose kind versus wicked before choosing a strategy." Defense: Federer/German World Cup/Ledecka evidence (Intro), figlie del coro and Sloboda (Ch 3), West Point match quality and the $500M bonus (Ch 6), Hesselbein (Ch 7). Accept any well-defended answer tied to the notes.
