# The Genius Myth (Helen Lewis) - Quiz Book

> **Source note:** This quiz reflects the *skill's chapter notes* for "The Genius Myth: A Curious History of a Dangerous Idea" (SKILL.md, glossary, patterns, cheatsheet and the 20 chapter files), **not the book's own wording**. Every fact, number and story below comes only from those notes. Where the notes are silent, nothing has been invented.

## How to use this quiz

- Work one section at a time (chapters are grouped in 1s, 2s and 3s). Cover the answers, write or say your answer, then check.
- Question types are marked: **[SA]** short answer, **[TF]** true/false, **[MC]** multiple choice, **[EXPLAIN]** explain in your own words, **[SCENARIO]** apply a concept to a situation.
- Score yourself: 2 points for a correct answer with the reason, 1 for the right answer only, 0 otherwise. Re-do any section below 70% after re-reading the matching chapter in the companion file `lewis-genius-myth-arguments-and-evidence.md`.
- Finish with the 20-question cumulative final exam at the end.
- The book's one rule, for orientation: **grant acts of genius; deny the class.**

## Section 1 - Introduction and Chapter 1: "Avonian Willy"

**Q1. [SA]** State Lewis's core distinction in one sentence.
**A:** She grants *acts* of genius (a painting, a transistor, a novel can be extraordinary) but rejects the idea of a *class* of "geniuses", a superior sort of human. "Special People" is the book's alternative title. *Why it matters:* the class idea is what licenses latitude no one else gets.

**Q2. [EXPLAIN]** What does "had -> was" mean, and why is the shift dangerous?
**A:** In Greek and Roman thinking, *genius* was a visiting spirit or guardian (*daimonion*, *furor divinus*) - something you *had*. The Renaissance "great men" and then the 18th-century Romantics made it something you *were*. That switch creates "special people" and sets up disappointment (and excuses) when the person turns out to be deplorable.

**Q3. [TF]** True or false: Lewis argues Elon Musk is simply not a genius, while his critics on the right argue he is.
**A:** **False (oversimplified).** The "Musk Referendum" idea is that the argument over his genius is really an argument over what society values and tolerates; the right exaggerates, the left denigrates, and either verdict is a political victory. Lewis's own position is nuanced: genius at engineering, not at human emotion, suited to one place and time (early-21st-century American tech).

**Q4. [MC]** According to the Berners-Lee vs. Musk test, fame tracks:
(a) originality of the invention (b) importance to humanity (c) performance of the cultural role of genius (d) years of work
**A:** **(c).** The more original and important inventor (Berners-Lee) is less famous because he doesn't perform the role: provocative statements, extreme work habits, an unusual personal life, self-mythologizing.

**Q5. [SA]** Name the four stages of the Shakespeare pipeline in Chapter 1.
**A:** Preservation (the First Folio, assembled by two actor-friends seven years after his death), Popularization (Restoration impresarios like Davenant rewriting freely), Canonization (Garrick's 1769 Stratford Jubilee, "bardolatry"), and Monetization/nationalization (the national poet, imperial export, tourist-board asset).

**Q6. [MC]** What would have been lost without the First Folio?
(a) Nothing, the plays were printed (b) eighteen plays, including *Macbeth* and *The Tempest* (c) only the sonnets (d) his portrait
**A:** **(b).** Eighteen plays including *Macbeth* and *The Tempest* would have vanished.

**Q7. [TF]** True or false: Garrick's 1769 Jubilee was a festival of Shakespeare performances.
**A:** **False.** It had odes to "Avonian Willy" and relics from the mulberry tree - and *zero performances* of the actual plays. That is the birth of bardolatry, and the "merch test" (toothpicks outnumber performances = canonization has become commerce).

**Q8. [EXPLAIN]** Why does Lewis say "irreverence, not reverence, kept the work alive"?
**A:** Restoration impresarios rewrote the plays freely (flying witches, a happy-ending *Lear*, ballet in *The Tempest*) and in doing so kept them performed. Works survive by being reused ("raw clay vs. best china"), not enshrined; kid-glove treatment can kill a living tradition.

**Q9. [SCENARIO]** A museum director notices that the gift shop sells more items about a long-dead composer than the orchestra performs his pieces. Which concept applies and what should she conclude?
**A:** The **merch test / bardolatry** from Ch 1: when the toothpicks outnumber the performances, canonization has become commerce. She should ask who benefits (the "high priest incentive": anointers siphon acclaim and money, e.g. Macklin on Garrick's "golden calf") and whether reuse - actually performing the work - is happening.

**Q10. [SCENARIO]** A biographer has very little data about a historical figure, and fans are circulating theories that someone else wrote the work. Which rule explains this and what is the right response?
**A:** The **Biographical Vacuum Rule**: audiences want to idolize the man, and when data is scarce (Shakespeare: a 40-page life written a century later) the vacuum fills with conspiracy (anti-Stratfordians: Oxford, Bacon, "a woman") or relic-worship. The right response is to accept uncertainty rather than fill gaps with fantasy.

**Bonus (estate as brand) [SA]** Give two examples from Ch 1 of "genius" as ambient branding.
**A:** Einstein's "priests" hid his first child until 1986, and the university licensing his image has earned ~$250M; Picasso's name on a Citroën; "Genius Bar", Genius.com, and nootropic "Genius" pills.

## Section 2 - Chapters 2 and 3: Vasari, the Romantics, and Galton

**Q1. [SA]** What is "biography as fable" (Vasari's method)?
**A:** A whole life honed down to a few key details that illustrate a moral. Readers wanted gossip and personality; criticism was the pretext.

**Q2. [MC]** Which Vasarian archetype is Michelangelo ("sleeps in his clothes, boots fused to his skin, *il divino*")?
(a) scatter-brained polymath (b) art monk (c) arrogant visionary (d) vindicated bureaucrat
**A:** **(b) the art monk.** Leonardo is the scatter-brained polymath ("capricious and fickle", dies regretting unfinished work); Brunelleschi and the egg is the arrogant visionary whom bureaucrats must not question.

**Q3. [EXPLAIN]** Describe the "story-fitness selection" examples in Ch 2.
**A:** Figures with a ready-made parable get remembered - Artemisia Gentileschi's rape-and-revenge legend - while equally accomplished figures without one are forgotten, such as Sofonisba Anguissola, court painter to Philip II, who died at 93.

**Q4. [SA]** What did Brian Hayes find about the Gauss schoolboy story?
**A:** It doesn't appear until 1856, gains the arithmetic series in 1938, goes viral with E. T. Bell's gothic "brutal teacher" version, then evolves by natural selection (whips dropped as readers got squeamish). The invariant across versions: the prodigy, marked from childhood, whose gift looks like magic. Lesson: *source the anecdote*.

**Q5. [TF]** True or false: In the Romantic model, genius comes from society and education.
**A:** **False.** The Romantics said genius comes from *nature*, uncorrupted by society (Rousseau's *Emile*; Kant's "nature's elect"), and added libertine passions (Byron: "we of the craft are all crazy"), genius as an excuse (Byron's incest scandal absorbed into his legend), and tuberculosis as the Romantic disease.

**Q6. [SA]** What is the "discouragement effect" of the prodigy template?
**A:** If talent is inborn magic, the rest of us learn we are "the less distinguished classmates" - the effect that makes the myth socially deflating.

**Q7. [EXPLAIN]** What was Galton's *Hereditary Genius* (1869) method and the numbers he derived?
**A:** List eminent men (judges, statesmen, scientists, oarsmen), count their eminent relatives, conclude ability is inherited. From a textbook's list vs. the male population over fifty he estimated 1 in 4,000 "eminent" and 1 in a million "illustrious" (class X: public funeral, mourned by "the whole intelligent part of the nation"). Others were banded A-G above the mean and a-g below; 95% "mediocre".

**Q8. [SA]** What is the Nepotism Confound, and what is its modern form?
**A:** Galton couldn't distinguish inherited talent from inherited resources, connections and opportunity (Merivale on the Atkyns judges: "a snug little party of jobbers"). Modern form: nepo babies. Galton tried a nephews-vs-sons control and admitted the calculation was beyond him.

**Q9. [MC]** Which is NOT among de Candolle's/Simonton's omitted environmental factors?
(a) rickets (b) lead (c) sex bars on training (d) favourable birth month
**A:** **(d).** The notes list rickets, lead, sex bars on training, religious exclusion, serial pregnancy, color bars, plus independent wealth, favorable opinion and temperate climate (de Candolle's 18 factors).

**Q10. [EXPLAIN]** How does Gauss refute Galton's thesis?
**A:** Galton's statistics depend on Gauss, yet Gauss's family tree gave no hint of genius and his brother was a laborer. Gauss also praised Sophie Germain's "superior genius" for overcoming obstacles - the opposite of Galton's complacency.

**Q11. [EXPLAIN]** What is the Austen Problem?
**A:** Havelock Ellis sourced from a biographical dictionary, cut entries under three pages, and lost Jane Austen. A genius needs a *story* as well as achievements; uneventful excellence fails an eminence filter. Two researchers using the same method produced different lists, so it isn't science.

**Q12. [SCENARIO]** A company claims "great engineers run in families" because the CTO's children all work in engineering. What control applies and why?
**A:** The **Nepotism/Environment Control**: list transferred resources, connections, training access and exclusions before accepting heredity. Galton could not separate inherited talent from inherited opportunity; neither can the company.

**Q13. [SCENARIO]** A magazine ranks "the 100 most influential scientists" by length of Wikipedia-style entries. Which Lewis concept warns you, and what is the likely failure?
**A:** The **Austen Filter Check / eminence-as-proxy problem**: the method rewards narrative drama over quality and drops quiet excellence. Expect uneventful but excellent figures to be missing.

## Section 3 - Chapter 4: Terman's Termites

**Q1. [SA]** Who invented IQ-style testing and for what purpose?
**A:** Binet built graded tasks to find children who needed help and feared "brutal pessimism" about fixed intelligence. Stern's single "mental age" number was called a *betrayal* by Simon. It was later hijacked by Goddard and Terman to rank humanity.

**Q2. [SA]** What is the Flynn Effect, with numbers?
**A:** IQ scores rose about 3.3 points per decade across the 20th century (SKILL.md and cheatsheet say "~3"). Example: a 1900s child answers "dogs hunt rabbits", a modern child "both are mammals" - schooling in abstraction, not smarter brains. So never compare groups or eras on raw scores.

**Q3. [MC]** Goddard's classification placed "moron" at:
(a) 51-70 (b) 26-50 (c) 100-120 (d) 71-85
**A:** **(a).** Imbecile 26-50; idiot 25 or below.

**Q4. [EXPLAIN]** Why does the Kallikak study matter despite being debunked?
**A:** The "bad" branch's founder wasn't even Martin's child, yet Goddard's work brought Binet to America. The legal legacy includes the 1924 Immigration Act, *Buck v. Bell* (1927) and Nazi sterilization laws.

**Q5. [SA]** Describe Terman's Study of the Gifted.
**A:** Begun 1921: ~1,500 California children, IQ >=140 and then >=135, teacher-nominated, tracked for life. Results: healthier than average (debunking wasted-genius), 90% college-bound, 1,500 papers, 100+ patents - but no genius by Galton's standard. Terman admitted in 1947 that America since Jamestown had not produced a Shakespeare or Newton.

**Q6. [TF]** True or false: Terman's study identified the two future Nobel physicists among his sample.
**A:** **False.** William Shockley and Luis Alvarez were both tested as boys and both excluded for scoring too low - then both won physics Nobels.

**Q7. [EXPLAIN]** How was the "gifted" label itself an intervention?
**A:** Parents bragged, children knew they were "special" ("royal jelly"), and teacher nominations skewed to confident, eloquent boys; poor and minority schools were under-sampled. Labelling changed how everyone treated the child.

**Q8. [SA]** What was Cox Miles' "ruler method" and why is it silly?
**A:** Estimating dead geniuses' IQs from encyclopedia entry length: Goethe 210, Leibniz 205, Newton 190, Cervantes and Copernicus 105, Shakespeare absent. "One of the silliest experiments" in social science - the Austen Problem again.

**Q9. [SA]** What do Jess Oppenheimer and "Sara Ann" illustrate?
**A:** Prediction failure in each direction. Oppenheimer, creator of *I Love Lucy*, was logged as a conceited boy "ruined by his mother" with no detectable sense of humor. Sara Ann, a physics graduate, had eight children in ten years partly to secure her husband's draft deferment, never worked as a physicist, and blamed being made self-conscious of her Termite status with "too little to actually do" with it.

**Q10. [SCENARIO]** A school wants to test seven-year-olds and place the top 2% in a fast stream permanently. Which Chapter 4 lessons apply?
**A:** Picking winners early (Terman; the 11-plus): precocity is not adult originality; the label is an intervention with side effects; confidence and eloquence get mistaken for intelligence; childhood IQ predicts a comfortable life, not breakthrough. Treat a score as "a lottery ticket that also changes how everyone treats the child."

**Q11. [SCENARIO]** A researcher wants to say "country X is less intelligent than country Y" using IQ means from 1950 and 2020. Which concept blocks the claim?
**A:** The **Flynn Effect** - about 3 points per decade means cross-era (and cross-country) raw comparisons are treacherous.

**Q12. [EXPLAIN]** What did WWII show about the Termites?
**A:** Life outcomes are savagely shaped by uncontrollable events: an atomic bomb supervisor, an internment-evading name change, a radiation death, a Nuremberg psychiatrist's suicide by cyanide.

## Section 4 - Chapter 5: Cyril and Hans

**Q1. [SA]** What was the "too-perfect correlation" tell in Burt's data?
**A:** His twin IQ correlations (0.944 reared together, 0.771 apart) stayed identical to three decimals while his sample grew from 21 to 53 pairs - twenty such coincidences. Invariant statistics across changing samples signal fabrication.

**Q2. [EXPLAIN]** What were "phantom collaborators"?
**A:** "Miss Howard" and "Miss Conway" published only in Burt's own journal and only while he edited it; over half the journal's forty contributors appear to be Burt pseudonyms. Control of one journal enables a hall of mirrors.

**Q3. [MC]** The "genius exemption" is Eysenck's argument that:
(a) geniuses make more errors (b) geniuses may fudge figures to confound vulgar mediocrities, and if the result is wrong then he wasn't a genius (c) fraud should be forgiven after 30 years (d) only twins can be geniuses
**A:** **(b).** It is circular and disqualifying.

**Q4. [TF]** True or false: Hearnshaw's "justifiable prerogative of a great man" and Corr's "Sometimes you need a pariah" are essentially the same defence a generation apart.
**A:** **True.** This is "defense by reputation" recurring verbatim; judge the evidence, not the champion.

**Q5. [SA]** What is "status capture"?
**A:** A leader of the field who is also patron of the next generation: everyone owes him, nobody is rewarded for demolition, and the reputation stays intact until his death.

**Q6. [EXPLAIN]** Describe the "just asking questions" ecosystem around Eysenck.
**A:** Tobacco funding (GBP 800,000), cancer-prone-personality claims, "smoking does not kill", astrology, and sunspots and gout as genius correlates. King's College declared 26 co-authored papers unsafe; dozens retracted; no inquiry into his IQ work. Gout correlates with achievement because rich middle-aged men both eat richly and have had careers - correlation is not causation.

**Q7. [SA]** What is Harden's middle ground on the IQ wars?
**A:** Intelligence is partly heritable and IQ predicts educational and career outcomes; the error is treating the number as an inerrant ranking of human worth. Reaction against hereditarians is understandable given who fiddled the figures.

**Q8. [SA]** Trace Anthony Pelosi's 30-year pursuit.
**A:** He reads Eysenck's heart-disease/cancer papers, finds the trial "worked" while the tables show huge downstream death rates, and concludes the data is impossible, not just unethical. His 1992 BMJ article was lawyered into euphemism ("some of the most remarkable claims ever to appear"); complaints were ignored; a 2019 open letter listed 61 papers; King's investigated and found them "unsafe". Lesson: institutional correction lags stature by decades.

**Q9. [TF]** True or false: The notes say Burt's figures were definitely wrong in conclusion.
**A:** **False.** The "later irony" noted is that Burt's figures may have been broadly right, however obtained. Fabrication is judged by method, not conclusion.

**Q10. [SCENARIO]** A senior professor's lab publishes results where a key statistic never changes across datasets, and co-authors appear only in his house journal. What do you do?
**A:** Apply the **Too-Perfect Data Tell**: look for statistics that don't move with the sample, collaborators who publish only in the star's venue, and a viewpoint the numbers conveniently support. Expect that accusations are costly and correction lags - document the evidence like Pelosi.

**Q11. [SCENARIO]** After a scandal, defenders say "the critics are just ideologues." Which anti-pattern and technique?
**A:** Anti-pattern: dismissing critics as ideologues to avoid the data. Technique: **Reputation-Defense Script Detection** - spot the script ("prerogative of a great man", "you need a pariah") and evaluate the evidence, not the champion.

## Section 5 - Chapters 6 and 7: Shockley and Marilyn vos Savant

**Q1. [SA]** What is scenius, and what is Paul Graham's line?
**A:** Collective genius of a place and time. "To make Leonardo you need more than innate ability; you also need Florence in 1450." Milan, same size, 300 km away, produced no Leonardo.

**Q2. [SA]** Which "Florences" made Shockley?
**A:** Proto-Silicon-Valley California, Caltech during the quantum revolution, and Bell Labs (nine Nobels; monopoly-funded freedom).

**Q3. [EXPLAIN]** What is "insulation by identity"?
**A:** Pulled over unshaven with a loaded gun, Shockley got the gun back. A Black Shockley would likely have been jailed and never reached MIT. Talent is necessary; being white, male, American and at the right university at the right time let it compound.

**Q4. [EXPLAIN]** How did Shockley grab the transistor's credit?
**A:** Brattain and Bardeen built the point-contact transistor; Shockley, excluded from the patent, threatened revolt, was put at the centre of publicity, answered the press, wrote the textbook - and became "the father of the transistor". The team never recovered; Bardeen left and won a second Nobel on work Shockley blocked.

**Q5. [SA]** How is Shockley connected to Fairchild and Intel?
**A:** His untameable-genius management (lie-detector tests for staff, refusing to hear ideas) made the Traitorous Eight leave -> Fairchild -> Intel. Silicon Valley owes its birth partly to his bad personality.

**Q6. [MC]** The best rebuttal to a credentialed crank like Shockley is:
(a) he is evil (b) he is wrong (c) ignore him (d) strip his Nobel
**A:** **(b).** Moral condemnation hands him a martyr's costume as a "persecuted empiricist" and escalates him.

**Q7. [SA]** Why is Shockley "the persecuted becomes the persecutor"?
**A:** He was excluded from Terman's study for low scores (129 and 125; his mother scored 161), then used the same tests to argue for sterilization bonuses ($1,000 per IQ point below 100). He also made the spuriously precise claim of "1 IQ point per 1% Caucasian ancestry".

**Q8. [SA]** What happened with the Nobel sperm bank?
**A:** Robert Graham's Repository for Germinal Choice: Shockley was the only laureate to admit donating (at ~70, unaware sperm quality declines with age); no laureate donations produced a child, so the "Nobel" branding was a lie. Doron Blake (IQ 180, magazine covers, bullied) concluded: "I don't think you can breed for good people."

**Q9. [EXPLAIN]** What does the Syl Jones interview show?
**A:** Shockley screened reporters with an IQ-heritability lecture; he greeted the white photographer as "Mr Jones", was staggered that Jones was Black, improvised another test, then called him "the exception that proves the rule". In *Playboy* (1980), pressed on Burt's faked data, he called it "deplorable and sad" without questioning his own objectivity. The genius self-image blocks self-correction.

**Q10. [SA]** What does Chapter 7 call the self-fulfilling prophecy of the label, using Marilyn vos Savant?
**A:** Attention leads to more attention. Marilyn was 178th of 613 in her high-school class, married at 16, divorced twice, a puzzle hobbyist; after *Guinness* printed her score in 1985 she got "Ask Marilyn", books and omni-opinions. Nothing about her changed; her life did. Her score is murky: 228 vs. the school board's ceiling of 167, with date discrepancies.

**Q11. [EXPLAIN]** What is galaxy-brainedness and what did high-IQ polls show?
**A:** Willingness to hold opinions on everything (Jarvik: relativity, the Big Bang and black holes all "wrong"). High-IQ polls on foreign policy were useless; a "clever" self-image makes people hold wrong opinions *more* forcefully.

**Q12. [SA]** Name the pathology of high-IQ societies.
**A:** Mensa's founder wanted a new aristocracy of brains (fireplace tiles and yellow map pins to recognize each other); randomly assigned volunteer roles collapsed ("high intelligence is a poor defence against collective stupidity"); Mega, Triple Nine, Cincinnatus and Prometheus saw constant schisms, name lawsuits and a self-declared Dictator. Members' preferred label: Outsider.

**Q13. [TF]** True or false: When Lewis took the Mensa test she concluded it measures mostly speed.
**A:** **True.** Speed above all; arguable synonyms; dated assumptions (skirts, "savages", husbands' surnames). Not measured: showing up daily, the ego to insist you're right, marketing yourself as the spirit of the age.

**Q14. [SA]** What did Guinness do in 1990, and what does GWAS show?
**A:** Retired the category - no test can produce a definitive ranking. GWAS shows even eye colour is polygenic and gene-environment interactive; intelligence far more so.

**Q15. [SCENARIO]** A Nobel-winning physicist is invited to keynote a genetics conference. Which tests apply?
**A:** The **omni-expertise** test ("what conference is he at, and why?") and the Three-Problems Test: competence is domain-bound (physics competence didn't transfer to genetics). Shockley is the cautionary case; "What law of nature have *you* discovered?" was his retort.

**Q16. [SCENARIO]** A manager has a brilliant but abusive scientist and considers indulging him to keep the team. Lesson from Ch 6?
**A:** Managing geniuses by indulgence loses the team and the second Nobel (Bardeen). The Traitorous Eight left; the credit grab destroyed two teams.

## Section 6 - Chapter 8: So You Want to Be a Genius?

**Q1. [EXPLAIN]** What is the Potus problem?
**A:** Great discoveries create the conditions for their own under-appreciation. *The West Wing* made "POTUS" ordinary; Shakespeare's psychological complexity is now every drama's baseline; zero took centuries and now a child grasps it. To appreciate past geniuses you must reconstruct the world before them.

**Q2. [SA]** What is the multiple problem and its number?
**A:** Ogburn & Thomas (1922) documented 148 cases of simultaneous independent invention (telephone, natural selection, calculus, aeroplane, logarithms, Neptune, sunspots). We remember one name - the one who claimed credit harder, filed first, or self-mythologized (Newton smearing Leibniz; Wallace naming his book *Darwinism*).

**Q3. [MC]** The adjacent possible for the printing press consisted of:
(a) moveable type + paper + ink + wine press (b) paper only (c) steam power (d) a national library
**A:** **(a).** YouTube = cameras + broadband. Ahead-of-time ideas fail (Babbage). Edison should be remembered as a manufacturer, not an inventor.

**Q4. [SA]** What is the Matthew Effect and the forty-first seat?
**A:** Merton: to those who have, more is given. The forty-first seat refers to the illustrious who missed the Académie: Descartes, Pascal, Moliere, Rousseau, Diderot, Flaubert, Zola, Proust. Recognition converts into labs, grants, first-author credit and press. Science advances one funeral at a time.

**Q5. [EXPLAIN]** What is the flipped reading of Eysenck's genius "portrait"?
**A:** His portrait (male, lose a parent before 10, born in February, die at 30 or 90 but never 60) isn't what *makes* a genius but what gets picked out of a biography afterwards to justify the anointing.

**Q6. [TF]** True or false: Culture prefers foxes over hedgehogs.
**A:** **False.** Culture acclaims hedgehogs (one big idea) unless range can be storied (Leonardo); Tetlock found foxes forecast better. Hedgehogs fall into assuming depth in one field gives superior judgment everywhere.

**Q7. [SA]** Distinguish white elephant art from termite art (Farber).
**A:** White elephant art: self-conscious masterpieces "shrieking with precocity, fame, ambition" (awards campaigns about lost weight and natural light; *Ulysses*). Termite art eats its boundaries and forgets itself. White elephant art is the route to the label.

**Q8. [EXPLAIN]** What does the Fleming story show?
**A:** The mould story omits the assistant who connected it to an earlier nose-drip experiment. Fleming chose medicine because of his brother, St Mary's because of water polo, bacteriology to stay in the rifle club. "Every decision is like a murder" of possible selves (Dubos). Dumb luck sits under the label.

**Q9. [SA]** What are conceptual vs. experimental innovators (Galenson)?
**A:** Conceptual: big-idea makers, most likely to be called geniuses. Experimental: refine for decades.

**Q10. [SCENARIO]** Two labs announce the same vaccine approach within weeks; only the better-publicized founder is feted. Which concepts?
**A:** The **multiple problem** (simultaneous invention; we remember one name) and the **Matthew Effect** (recognition compounds). Ask who else got there and who claimed credit harder.

**Q11. [SCENARIO]** You are asked to assess a "visionary" founder. Walk through the Distortion Audit.
**A:** (1) Potus - what did the world lack before them? (2) Multiples - who else got there? (3) Adjacent possible - which components had just arrived? (4) Matthew - what compounding recognition, who took the forty-first seat? (5) Weirdness - which eccentricities got folded into the legend? (6) Luck - which trivial decisions routed them here? What survives is the act; what falls away was the myth.

**Q12. [EXPLAIN]** Why might starting just after a paradigm shift hurt your chances of a world-changing breakthrough?
**A:** You are working inside a settled model. Even shift-makers (Einstein, Picasso) "lost contact with the implications of their own revolution" and created the conditions of their own surpassing. Shockley's youth during quantum mechanics (Kuhn) was an advantage.

## Section 7 - Chapters 9 and 10: The Rebel; Monsters and Tortured Artists

**Q1. [EXPLAIN]** What is the Galileo syndrome?
**A:** Taking opposition as evidence of insight: "if everyone says I'm wrong, I must be right." Examples in the notes: ivermectin "truthers" (Heying, Weinstein), Mark Rylance's garlic and chemo scepticism, Freeman Dyson's climate contrarianism. "You're probably not Galileo."

**Q2. [TF]** True or false: Galileo's "*Eppur si muove*" is contemporary with his trial.
**A:** **False.** It first appears in 1757, a century after.

**Q3. [SA]** Name three facts that "unmyth" Galileo.
**A:** He sought papal permission; he died in his bed; he was a superb lens-maker (3x -> 8x -> 15x -> 20x; Kepler had to borrow his telescope); he scrambled to claim credit before Jesuit telescopes caught up - empiricist, self-publicist, politician.

**Q4. [SA]** What is the Semmelweis reflex and why is it dangerous to the myth?
**A:** Authorities' knee-jerk rejection of challenges (handwashing rejected; Semmelweis died in an asylum). It is real but rare - the exception that makes the myth. For every Semmelweis: phlogiston, mesmerism, phrenology, polywater.

**Q5. [EXPLAIN]** Why did Edison's phonograph and Columbus undercut "they all laughed"?
**A:** Edison's phonograph was an instant hit; Columbus didn't prove the earth round. Rejection templates are frequently false; source them.

**Q6. [MC]** The ivermectin timeline shows that:
(a) the rebels were vindicated (b) reasonable scepticism and the rebel persona diverge when evidence arrives (c) Merck suppressed the data (d) masks never mattered
**A:** **(b).** Early scepticism was reasonable (dexamethasone had worked, mask guidance had flipped), then Merck said no evidence and *Nature* reported the evidence base evaporated; contrarians whose identity is heterodoxy couldn't update and escalated to vaccine-Nuremberg rhetoric.

**Q7. [SA]** Who was Dyson's exemplar rebel and what does he show?
**A:** Thomas Gold - right about the ear, wrong about moon dust and abiogenic oil. Dyson's rebellion aligned with fossil-fuel interests at a decisive moment: "the rebel is not as powerless as he pretends."

**Q8. [EXPLAIN]** What is "benefit of clergy" and who coined its use for artists?
**A:** Orwell, on Dali: the medieval exemption of clerics from civil courts, transferred to artists. Neither denounce the cad nor defend the untouchable genius; ask why he exhibits the aberration. If Shakespeare's hobby were raping children, we would not tell him to carry on for another *King Lear*.

**Q9. [SA]** List four features of the Chris Goode mechanism.
**A:** Cultivated outsider/depressive persona; work repeatedly eroticizing adult-child relationships; an all-male, largely unpaid ensemble recruited at 18 to early 30s with nudity and "real sexual contact" in the advert; critics who feared being philistines praising work they found "boring... or theatrically unfruitful"; queerness and left politics as a shield. (Also "check-ins" as consent theatre.) He was arrested after child-abuse images were found by his husband, died by suicide on bail, then was erased from the internet.

**Q10. [EXPLAIN]** What is Lewis's rule on separating art from artist?
**A:** Separate where possible; impossible when the art is propaganda for the crime (Polanski's "celebrations of sexual sadism"; Goode's plays). Otherwise it is a personal, rarely coherent choice - own it.

**Q11. [SA]** What is "contradiction, not causality" (Simon Stephens)?
**A:** Great artists who are alcoholics don't make art *because* of it; "all his plays were suicide notes" is a specious Romantic myth. Goode was a criminal and a kind mentor, an abuser and a maker of good art, side by side. "We are *dividuals*."

**Q12. [SCENARIO]** A friend says "you can't criticize the director's casting practices, he's a queer outsider and critics will use it against him." Which concepts?
**A:** Political identity as a **shield** and fear of philistinism (Ch 10); the cult of genius as complicity. The unquestionable thing is whether identity and politics fuel the outsider-genius self-myth.

**Q13. [SCENARIO]** You are tempted to back an unpopular scientific claim because it's heretical and you feel vindicated. What to do?
**A:** **Hoofbeats -> horses**: assume the obvious while staying open; demand sourced "they laughed at him" stories; pre-commit to an update trigger before the evidence arrives ("if the trial says X, I change my mind").

## Section 8 - Chapters 11 and 12: Great Wives and Keepers of the Flame

**Q1. [SA]** What did Sofia Tolstaya do for Tolstoy?
**A:** Married at 18 to a 34-year-old; sixteen pregnancies; copied *War and Peace* seven times; edited; kept diaries he mined; supplied material (her teenage novella; her grief for a dead son became Anna's "eternal fear"); worked in bed after childbirth. He borrowed; she believed - "a genius, and a parasite."

**Q2. [EXPLAIN]** What is the reader thesis (A. N. Wilson)?
**A:** The marriage bound while Sofia was his *reader*; it broke when a rival reader - the disciple Chertkov, with 2,000 letters, secretary-copyists, control of the diary and a secret will - replaced her. Swap Sofia for Chertkov and you swap *Anna Karenina* for unreadable tracts.

**Q3. [SA]** What is the Wife Glitch (Schaffer)?
**A:** Someone who anticipates needs, orders the home, tends raw emotion, tracks infinite details, balances books and fills weaknesses with her strength - "and means it". Institutions can substitute (nunneries for Hildegard, a Cambridge college for Newton, a Tokyo hospital for Kusama) but none is so intimately intertwined.

**Q4. [SA]** Give two examples of #thanksfortyping and Vera Nabokov.
**A:** Acknowledgements thanking wives for typing whole manuscripts five times while nursing and teaching; an economist thanking his professor-wife for "managing the entire infrastructure of our lives" while he "sat in the study thinking about utility maximisation". Vera Nabokov: agent, translator, secretary, driver, substitute lecturer, page-turner, pistol in handbag after *Lolita*.

**Q5. [EXPLAIN]** What was Einstein's contract for Mileva Maric?
**A:** Laundry kept, three meals in his room, no personal relations, stop talking when told. She was a fellow physics student and early sparring partner; she was replaced by a cousin with no ambitions, and the "Einstein priests" wrote her out.

**Q6. [SA]** What is "Yoko-ing"?
**A:** Blaming the wife for the decline she was holding back; the wife-vs-fans pattern (who really understands him?). Tolstoyans cast Sofia as the enemy; she waited outside the station room as he died, admitted only after he lost consciousness; her memoir was suppressed for eight decades.

**Q7. [SA]** How did Lee Krasner manage Pollock's legacy and what do the prices show?
**A:** She sold one or two Pollocks a year, preferably to museums, and "almost single-handedly forced up prices": $2M for *Blue Poles* (1973), $140M for *No. 5* decades later. Her own work had been blacked out: morning painting in a bedroom (Little Image series) while he had the barn; after his 1956 crash she took the barn and painted seventeen-foot canvases. Market lag today: Pollock $200M (2015); Krasner's record $11M (2019).

**Q8. [EXPLAIN]** Describe the media anointing of Pollock.
**A:** *Life*'s 1949 "Is he the greatest living painter?" and the 1951 "Irascibles" portrait created abstract expressionism as a movement. The photo excluded Krasner and included one woman, Hedda Sterne, who regretted it ("known more for that darn photo than for eighty years of work"). *Life* said a grocer "bought" a Pollock; he actually took it against unpaid debts.

**Q9. [SA]** List Jo van Gogh-Bonger's moves.
**A:** Widowed at ~29 with a baby and 200+ paintings plus the brothers' letters in a boarding house in Bussum; fused art and life (the brothers' love story; peasant painter too sensitive for salons); won over the critic who first called it "nearly vulgar"; organized a 484-work show (1905); *bought back* *Starry Night* because "uncomfortable is good"; helped invent the starving-artist-in-a-garret image; withheld her diary until 2009. Three descendants sit on the museum board; van Gogh is now a national artist.

**Q10. [SA]** Name three further keepers/legacy cases from Hamilton's guardians and copyright.
**A:** Donne's son (possibly stole the papers to publish them); Marvell's "widow" (probably his landlady); Pope's feud with a pirate publisher; Boswell for Johnson; Burns sanitized (the revisionist biographer got a bullet in the post); Beckett's estate forbids women in male roles; le Carre's womanizing published eight years later. Sonia Orwell: fourteen weeks a wife, thirty years a widow.

**Q11. [SCENARIO]** You're reading a heroic biography of a prolific mid-century male novelist. Which first question and technique?
**A:** "Who typed it?" and **Who Cooked the Dinner?** - list who fed, typed, edited, managed, shielded and funded; check whether the estate or fans wrote them out; ask what is owed to those harmed in production.

**Q12. [SCENARIO]** A founder's estate/foundation controls all archives and an approved biographer. How do you assess the legacy?
**A:** Use the **Legacy-Management Checklist**: where the work lives, whether the life is packaged with the work, which hostile tastemaker was converted, the largest retrospective, which unsettling piece was made the icon, who sits on the board, and what is suppressed until when. "Ask who holds the papers."

## Section 9 - Chapters 13 and 14: A Cover Version and Alchemy

**Q1. [SA]** What is the shared premise of *Yesterday* and *Cover Version*, and their opposite conclusions?
**A:** One man alone remembers the Beatles. *Yesterday* (Curtis): great talent cannot be repressed. *Cover Version* (Barth, the original): talent isn't enough; songs don't land without personality, era, manager, producer and luck.

**Q2. [EXPLAIN]** How do the two writers' careers explain their films?
**A:** Curtis: Harrow, Oxford, *Not the Nine O'Clock News*, *Blackadder*, *Four Weddings*, Working Title - "never been knocked out". Barth: twenty-five unproduced scripts, one *Simpsons* episode and no callback, social awkwardness that made self-selling nauseating. Each wrote the theory of success his life taught him.

**Q3. [SA]** Who are the omitted "Fifth and Sixth Beatles"?
**A:** George Martin (producer) and Brian Epstein (committed manager). The film's manager is a cynical shark who could have made the star anyway - "an insult" to Epstein's integrity.

**Q4. [EXPLAIN]** What credit irony surrounds the film?
**A:** Curtis says he never read the script; both films have a fisherman John Lennon and a "nobody remembers Harry Potter" gag; Barth got a shared "Story by" credit and a share of *net* profit - the film "lost money" by Hollywood accounting. The film about taking credit became an instance of it.

**Q5. [SA]** Give Barth's "Star Wars wouldn't sell today" point.
**A:** The same idea pitched without the name and the moment fails; success = product, person, timing, sponsor. Also the protagonist realizes he has become Townes Van Zandt (name-checked by everyone, never had a hit, died at 52).

**Q6. [SA]** List five near-misses in the Beatles' origin (Craig Brown).
**A:** Paul's parents met because bombers flew on one night and not the next; John and Paul both lost mothers young; Ringo nearly died of peritonitis; Paul failed Spanish and stayed down a year to meet George; they dodged conscription; they found Epstein as they considered quitting; Sutcliffe stayed in Hamburg; Epstein fired the popular drummer and ignored a picket; Ringo nearly emigrated but the forms were too many.

**Q7. [TF]** True or false: The Decca rejection proves the Beatles were an undeniable revolution that Decca missed.
**A:** **False.** The audition setlist was mostly covers plus three forgettable originals; *Please Please Me* is raw, simple, sub-three-minutes for radio; Little Richard was "way ahead" five years earlier. George Martin heard *potential*, not a revolution. (A Potus problem makes it hard to unpick, as Springsteen and America experienced it as transformative.)

**Q8. [SA]** What is the Beatles' "adjacent possible"?
**A:** Television as mass medium, Little Richard as forebear, post-war stability letting working-class musicians earn, George Martin (classically trained, comedy producer for the Goons), the Boomer demographic bulge, charts as cultural force, *Ed Sullivan* - no equivalent today in a fragmented algorithmic culture.

**Q9. [EXPLAIN]** What are Simonton's age curves and the conceptual/experimental contrast?
**A:** Peak near 40 on average (Beard); steep rise, gentle decline (more productive at 70 than at 20); poets and mathematicians earliest, scholars latest; outliers (Darwin at 50, Copernicus at 70, Disraeli PM at 64). Picasso's early Cubism is worth more than his late work; Cezanne the reverse. We glorify the conceptual innovator because it explains why we aren't geniuses.

**Q10. [MC]** When should a genius die for the best myth (Simonton)?
(a) at 40 (b) young (Mozart) or very old (Goethe) - never sixty (c) at the peak only (d) it makes no difference
**A:** **(b).** Memorable deaths as fable: Jefferson and Adams on the same July 4th; Mozart's requiem; James Dean's Porsche; Elvis's bathroom.

**Q11. [SA]** How did the Beatles do "both halves" of the lifecycle?
**A:** They ended at their peak (George was 27) and refused to reform; Lennon was murdered in 1980 - sainted, airbrushed (Julian, Cynthia), no longer able to trash Paul's "muzak"; McCartney outlived the "Boring Beatle" label to become patriarch and canny catalogue manager (Glastonbury at 80 duetting with isolated Lennon vocals). The Rolling Stones just carried on.

**Q12. [EXPLAIN]** What is "perspiration reclaimed"?
**A:** Gladwell's Hamburg hours (the "10,000" is spuriously precise; the point stands); *Get Back* shows songs emerging from aimless jamming - McCartney's tolerance for the cloudy zone and intrinsic motivation to keep writing through "Frog Chorus" jibes.

**Q13. [SCENARIO]** A startup founder credits the company's success to "obvious quality". What tests apply?
**A:** **Counterfactual Product Test** ("would I buy this from a stranger?"; list persona, moment, sponsor, producer and luck as co-authors) and **Count the Near-Misses**.

**Q14. [SCENARIO]** Commentators say a living musician is "less deep" than his dead partner. Which correction?
**A:** **Endings Awareness**: correct for the mythology bonus of dying young and the "boring survivor" discount (Lennon/McCartney); compare records fairly.

## Section 10 - Chapters 15 and 16: The Deficit Model and Disruptors

**Q1. [SA]** Define the deficit model.
**A:** Genius extorts a price - for every credit a debit - with oddness as the currency. Examples: Beethoven deaf, Milton blind, Mozart a child who couldn't handle adulthood. It feels like justice ("ledger-book justice") and sells tickets.

**Q2. [SA]** Name three biopic tropes.
**A:** The flash of insight (Nash's bar-scene); the lone rebel against an uncaring regime; the great man as the sun with everyone orbiting; struggle as mandatory motor. "Biopics are Vasari with a budget."

**Q3. [EXPLAIN]** Why are biopics bad at scenius, and what is the exception?
**A:** A life is used in service of a theme (*Amadeus* = envy; *Gandhi*/*Braveheart* = resistance). *Oppenheimer* is a rare attempt at genius as organizational force-multiplying; audiences find it messy.

**Q4. [EXPLAIN]** What does Nash show about mad genius?
**A:** The film's feint: the spy plot and kindly roommate are delusion. Nash said supernatural ideas came "the same way" as mathematical ones; colleagues missed the illness because he was the kind of odd mathematicians are. A grain of truth in the stereotype, not a law.

**Q5. [TF]** True or false: Hawking's best work came after his deterioration.
**A:** **False.** He did his best work *before*; the narrative wants a link that doesn't exist (Hawking Timeline Test). He became an icon (*A Brief History of Time*, *The Simpsons*, zero-g flights) while collaborator Penrose, later a Nobel laureate, stays obscure. Wilczek: idolization "corrosive"; "less would have been more."

**Q6. [SA]** How does the Turing film distort him?
**A:** He was already a Cambridge Fellow with important papers when he reached Bletchley; the film makes him joke-blind and friendless - mathematical brilliance bought with social skills ("The people no one imagines anything of...").

**Q7. [EXPLAIN]** Summarize Michael Laudor and *The Laws of Madness*.
**A:** Schizophrenia, Yale Law Review editor, a 1995 *NYT* profile hitting every deficit beat ("70% of his brain" spent staying in reality), $600K+ memoir deal and $1.5M film rights (Ron Howard; Brad Pitt). Off medication he killed his girlfriend, believing her an impostor. Committed; the book never finished; three years later *A Beautiful Mind* came out. Intelligence could not save him.

**Q8. [SA]** What are the scenius conditions of Silicon Valley?
**A:** No non-compete clauses in California; Stanford graduates founding firms; DoD funding (GPS via Naval Research; internet via DARPA); post-war wealth attracting the world's scientists (including some Nazi ones). Publicly nurtured, privately mythologized.

**Q9. [EXPLAIN]** Critique disruption theory.
**A:** Christensen: cheap scrappy rivals dislodge bloated incumbents - market Darwinism. Reality: incumbents lobby, laws are shaped, antitrust is weak, giants buy rivals (Facebook: thirty companies in two years, including Instagram and WhatsApp).

**Q10. [SA]** What is the "genius asshole" archetype and Wozniak's line?
**A:** Jobs as the Genius Entrepreneur, Wozniak the Genius Engineer; Jobs convinced everyone the assholery was indivisible from the genius. Wozniak: a nicer Apple "may never have made the Macintosh" - is that true, and was it worth it? An untested empirical claim.

**Q11. [SCENARIO]** A film pitch: a brilliant mathematician with a debilitating illness whose torment "gives him his insight". Which test?
**A:** Hawking Timeline Test: does the suffering precede or follow the best work? If it follows, the link is narrative. Also the Laudor case warns the genre selects the survivor stories.

**Q12. [SCENARIO]** A startup says it will "disrupt" an industry dominated by giants. How do you test this?
**A:** "Who bought whom?" - check lobbying, antitrust and acquisition (Facebook's thirty acquisitions) to see whether disruption or consolidation is occurring; remember the founders stand on DARPA, Stanford and a tax code.

## Section 11 - Chapter 17: Thomas Edison

**Q1. [SA]** What was Edison's real conceptual breakthrough?
**A:** The phonograph (1877), an accidental byproduct of hunting telephone diaphragms: a finger on a vibrating membrane suggested a stylus and a record. The lab ignored it for six months and couldn't name it.

**Q2. [EXPLAIN]** What lesson did the myth take from the phonograph's "midnight dinner" origin?
**A:** The real lesson could be that relationships (a collaborative moment) matter; the myth took the second option - that you must stay up past midnight.

**Q3. [SA]** Describe the access-for-hype machine.
**A:** Favoured reporters get exclusives; Edison mentions an imminent breakthrough; few arrive but enough do to hold off critics. One *Herald* writer called him "esteemed manipulator of the fiery lightning" and received eight shares in Edison Electric. Explorers were sent worldwide for filaments as publicity (one claimed 98 days in the same clothes).

**Q4. [SA]** Who preceded Edison on the lightbulb?
**A:** Davy (incandescence, 1802-09), Jobard (carbon rod in vacuum, 1838), Swan (working prototype, 1860). Edison's problem was manufacturing: better vacuum (Sprengel pump), a longer filament (Batchelor's carbonised thread - 40 hours; then paper), reliable supply.

**Q5. [TF]** True or false: Edison's overpromised platinum "for ever" filaments worked well.
**A:** **False.** They burned out in minutes; demos were cancelled and visitors barred. Later: lawsuits, patent battles, mergers, losing the current war to Westinghouse, and being pushed out as the company became General Electric.

**Q6. [MC]** The notes' view of Edison's most underrated innovation:
(a) the phonograph (b) the lightbulb (c) the grid - meters and mains in New York (d) the movie camera
**A:** **(c).** The grid created the Electric Age.

**Q7. [EXPLAIN]** Why did Edison "have it, then didn't"?
**A:** Ore-milling years, litigation, a claimed movie camera actually an employee's, the lab fire, an electric-car battery killed by the Model T, "talked more and invented less". Explanations: scenius changed (industrial-scale chemistry outgrew a small lab); a conceptual innovator who peaked early and calcified (couldn't grasp radio's commercial potential, as Einstein was puzzled by quantum mechanics).

**Q8. [SA]** What was the sage phase?
**A:** Opinions on women, Turks, the soul; the leaked "Edison Test" (Einstein reportedly flunked it - tinkerer beats academic); "there ain't no rules around here" as proto-disruption; his name invoked against the New Deal; employees noticed the myth replacing the man.

**Q9. [SA]** List myth revisions over time.
**A:** Wizard of Menlo Park; deafness as superpower; argument against schooling; family downgraded to poverty for just-deserts; "Tom" Edison as Tom Sawyer (he was "Al"). Also: Tesla left after a year over a reneged bet; neglected first wife; a failson lent the name to the "Magno-Electric Vitalizer."

**Q10. [SCENARIO]** A founder gives exclusives to friendly reporters, repeatedly announces "imminent" breakthroughs, and holds demos that hide the failure mode. Which Edison parallel and test?
**A:** The access-for-hype machine and the **reporter-with-shares test**: who profits from calling this person a genius? Also mistaking hype cadence for progress.

**Q11. [SCENARIO]** An investor insists the founder's name be on every patent. Why?
**A:** In Edison's case investors demanded credit: patents needed a name, and Edison was the brand. The manager and systems-builder is boiled down to a sole inventor and then a parable (*work hard and you can achieve anything*).

## Section 12 - Chapters 18 and 19: Elon Musk and the Conclusion

**Q1. [SA]** State Mac & Conger's three problems.
**A:** SpaceX = physics problem, Tesla = manufacturing problem, Twitter = social/psychological problem. Genius doesn't transfer.

**Q2. [EXPLAIN]** What is "both/and, not either/or" for Musk?
**A:** The man who cut launch costs with reusable rockets also falls for photoshopped headlines. Detractors and fans both can't accept it because both believe the myth (all-round special person, or nothing). Fix: stop treating genius as transferable.

**Q3. [SA]** Give the Twitter numbers.
**A:** Fidelity marked the $44B purchase down 71% to ~$10B; user numbers down; advertisers gone; "Extremely hardcore" email in Nov 2022 with three months' severance for refusers.

**Q4. [SA]** What background facts of Musk's rise does Lewis flag as "leg-up"?
**A:** Zip2 founded in the Bay Area with a $28,000 gift from his father; sold for $307M; pushed out of X.com by a boardroom coup yet made $180M; SpaceX rescued by a $1.6B NASA contract (Dec 2008); Tesla buyers' $7,500 credits.

**Q5. [EXPLAIN]** What is the Stockholm syndrome of the hardcore?
**A:** Employees proud of a boss who sleeps under his desk and shouts; if it's this miserable, there must be a higher purpose (Lewis's newsroom memory: captioning animals dressed as Harry Potter characters). "If a genius is often a sadist, many of the rest of us are secret masochists."

**Q6. [SA]** Who was Mary Beth Brown and why does she matter?
**A:** Twelve years as the "only bridge" between Musk and his interests, praetorian guard and translator; let go in 2014 reportedly after asking for executive-level pay (Musk disputes). Replaced by an operations coordinator who told a Twitter executive Musk is "special in this world... our job to protect him" - reminding her of the Christian cult she grew up in. Everyone hailed as a genius creates a secular cult. "MB" stands for all the rest of us.

**Q7. [EXPLAIN]** How does Musk's story return to Galton?
**A:** Population collapse, "woke mind virus", DEI blamed for crashes, IQ and clever people breeding; the post that only "high T alpha males and autists" ask "is this true?"; "Galton's ideas will never die." Refuted by history: plenty of big butch lads backed Nazi Germany; Bonhoeffer and Sophie Scholl resisted.

**Q8. [MC]** Lewis's secularization arc (Fara) runs:
(a) number -> spirit -> data (b) spirit/possession -> secular saint -> Victorian number -> granular data (c) data -> spirit (d) saint -> spirit
**A:** **(b).** All refuse genius's random, unpredictable nature.

**Q9. [SA]** List the four functions of the myth.
**A:** (1) lets the weird prosper; (2) manages envy (we're just Gauss's classmates); (3) reconciles us to monstrosity paired with achievement; (4) sneaking admiration for the one who gets away with it (Picasso as pure id; Trump as analogue; Larkin).

**Q10. [EXPLAIN]** What are the "two wastes"?
**A:** Some talents scorched by indulgence; far more extinguished for lack of support - cotton fields, sweatshops, marriage bars, segregation, predetermined ideas of who counts. "Before he can remake his society, his society must make him."

**Q11. [EXPLAIN]** What does Lewis say about AI and genius?
**A:** LLMs make creativity easy but are prediction machines lacking originality; great works are unpredictable, wrong by previous rules. A machine paradigm shift would feel meaningless without a mind behind it - "substitute ChatGPT for Dostoevsky and the magic is gone." "We need myths, and myths need people."

**Q12. [SA]** What is the prescription?
**A:** Call the painting, the transistor, *War and Peace* genius - not Shockley or Tolstoy. Celebrate talent and achievement; be humble about where they're found. "There are many seeds of genius in the world. We must nurture as many as we can."

**Q13. [SCENARIO]** A CEO with a record in hardware buys a social-media company and fires the moderating staff. Apply the Three-Problems Test and Support-Structure Inventory.
**A:** Past successes were physics/manufacturing; the new one is social/psychological; expect winging it. Inventory the assistants, grown-ups and structures removed (the MB; Sandberg/Schmidt-type "grown-ups"), check public money underneath, and predict impulsivity. Sycophant count in texts measures remaining moderating structure.

**Q14. [SCENARIO]** Someone proposes a "scientific" ranking of history's greatest minds. Which Chapter 19 anti-pattern?
**A:** Ranking historical figures "scientifically"; also beware debunking into nihilism (denying talent) and expecting AI to satisfy the need for a mind behind the work.

**A:** Lewis does not deny achievement; she relocates it to works and acts, which keeps awe (the "sublime") while removing the licence the class label grants. Evidence: Shakespeare's pipeline of human labour, scenius for Shockley, the grid for Edison, NASA money for SpaceX. The humility is positive: recognition is contingent (Matthew Effect, near-misses), so we should nurture many seeds because exclusion wastes far more than indulgence scorches.
