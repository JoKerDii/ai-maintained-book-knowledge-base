# The Genius Myth Quiz Book
*The Genius Myth: A Curious History of a Dangerous Idea* by Helen Lewis

**How to use:** Cover the answers, answer from memory (out loud or in writing), then check. Anything you miss is a section to reread. The book has an introduction and 19 chapters, so chapters are grouped two or three to a section, each with short-answer (SA), true/false (T/F), and multiple-choice (MC) questions. A cumulative final exam is at the end. Built from the notes in the `lewis-genius-myth` skill, not the book's own wording.

## Section 1: Introduction, Ch 1 (Avonian Willy) and Ch 2 (Secular Saints)

**Q1 (SA).** What is Lewis's core distinction about genius?
**A:** Grant *acts* of genius, but reject a *class* of geniuses. Individuals do extraordinary things, but that doesn't make them a superior sort of human ("Special People" is the book's alternative title).

**Q2 (SA).** What is the "had to was" shift in the word "genius"?
**A:** Greek and Roman *genius* was a visiting spirit or guardian, something you *had*. The Renaissance "great men" and then the Romantics made it something you *were*. That switch enables the "special people" idea and sets up disappointment when the person turns out to be deplorable.

**Q3 (MC).** In the Berners-Lee vs. Musk test, fame tracks:
a) the size of the contribution  b) performing the cultural role of genius (provocative statements, extreme work habits, unusual personal life, self-mythologizing)  c) IQ  d) number of patents
**A:** b. The more original inventor is less famous because he doesn't perform the role.

**Q4 (SA).** Name the four stages of the Shakespeare pipeline in Ch 1, with one detail for each.
**A:** Preservation (the First Folio, assembled by two actor-friends seven years after his death; without it eighteen plays, including *Macbeth* and *The Tempest*, vanish). Popularization (Restoration impresarios like Davenant rewrote freely: flying witches, a happy-ending *Lear*). Canonization (Garrick's 1769 Stratford Jubilee: odes, mulberry-tree relics, zero performances of the plays; bardolatry is born). Monetization/nationalization (Shakespeare as the argument for English pre-eminence, an imperial export, a tourist asset).

**Q5 (T/F).** Lewis argues that reverent treatment is what keeps a great work alive. **A:** False. Irreverent reuse, not reverence, kept Shakespeare alive. Kid-glove treatment can kill a living tradition.

**Q6 (SA).** What is Vasari's method, and what are two of the archetypes it fixed?
**A:** Biography as fable: a whole life honed to a few details that illustrate a moral. Archetypes include the art monk (Michelangelo), the scatter-brained polymath (Leonardo), the prodigy who corrects the master, and the arrogant visionary vindicated (Brunelleschi and the egg).

**Q7 (SA).** What does the Gauss test (Brian Hayes) teach, and what is the invariant in the story?
**A:** Source the anecdote. The schoolboy 1-to-100 story doesn't appear until 1856, gains the arithmetic series in 1938, and evolves toward narrative fitness (whips dropped as readers got squeamish). The invariant is the prodigy, marked from childhood, whose gift is indistinguishable from magic.

## Section 2: Ch 3 (Galton's Good Breeding) and Ch 4 (Terman's Termites)

**Q1 (SA).** Describe Galton's method in *Hereditary Genius* (1869) and its fatal flaw.
**A:** He listed eminent men, counted eminent relatives, and concluded ability is inherited. The flaw is the nepotism confound: he could not separate inherited talent from inherited resources, connections, and opportunity.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the Austen Problem?
**A:** Eminence-based selection (for example, sourcing from a biographical dictionary and cutting short entries, as Havelock Ellis described) loses Jane Austen. A genius needs a *story* as well as achievements; uneventful excellence fails the filter.

**Q3 (T/F).** Gauss supports Galton's thesis, since Galton's statistics depend on him. **A:** False. Gauss's family tree gave no hint of genius and his brother was a laborer, so he is a counter-example to Galton's thesis.

**Q4 (MC).** IQ testing was originally invented by Binet to:
a) rank humanity  b) find children who needed help  c) select future leaders  d) support immigration limits
**A:** b. Binet feared "brutal pessimism" about fixed intelligence. Goddard brought the tests to America and used them for classification.

**Q5 (SA).** What was the result of Terman's Study of the Gifted?
**A:** About 1,500 high-IQ Californian children tracked for life. They were physically healthier than average, 90% college-bound, with 1,500 papers and 100+ patents, but produced no genius by Galton's standard: eminent, not illustrious. Terman himself admitted in 1947 that America hadn't produced a Shakespeare or Newton.

**Q6 (SA).** Which two future Nobel laureates did Terman's team reject, and why does it matter?
**A:** William Shockley and Luis Alvarez (both physics Nobels) were excluded for scoring too low. It shows childhood IQ tests can't identify genius.

**Q7 (SA).** What is the Flynn Effect, and what is its lesson?
**A:** IQ scores rose about 3.3 points per decade across the 20th century (a 1900s child answers "dogs hunt rabbits," a modern one "both are mammals": schooling in abstraction, not smarter brains). Be very cautious comparing groups or eras.

## Section 3: Ch 5 (Cyril and Hans) and Ch 6 (Shockley and the Genius Sperm Bank)

**Q1 (SA).** What was the "too-perfect correlation" tell in Burt's data?
**A:** His twin IQ correlations (0.944 reared together, 0.771 apart) stayed identical to three decimals while his sample grew from 21 to 53 pairs. Invariant statistics across changing samples signal fabrication.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the "genius exemption," and why is it circular?
**A:** Eysenck's defense of Burt: geniuses may fudge figures to confound "vulgar mediocrities." If the result is wrong, then he wasn't a genius, so the defense is circular and disqualifying.

**Q3 (T/F).** Lewis concludes that heritability of intelligence is a myth. **A:** False. Following Harden's middle ground, intelligence is partly heritable and IQ predicts educational and career outcomes. The myth is treating the number as an inerrant ranking of human worth.

**Q4 (SA).** What does "scenius" mean, and what is Paul Graham's Leonardo example?
**A:** Collective genius of a place and time. "To make Leonardo you need more than innate ability; you also need Florence in 1450." Milan, same size and 300 km away, produced no Leonardo. Shockley's Florences were California, Caltech during the quantum revolution, and Bell Labs.

**Q5 (MC).** How did Shockley become "the father of the transistor"?
a) he built the point-contact device  b) Brattain and Bardeen built it; Shockley, excluded from the patent, threatened revolt, was put at the center of publicity, answered the press, and wrote the textbook  c) he won a patent lawsuit  d) Bell Labs named him
**A:** b.

**Q6 (SA).** What is the "omni-expertise cascade," and what is Lewis's advice for rebutting a figure like Shockley?
**A:** A Nobelist in one field is treated as authority in another (a physicist at a genetics conference), escalating extremity under backlash, "just asking questions," spurious precision. Refute him as *wrong*, not evil: moral condemnation alone hands him a martyr's costume.

**Q7 (SA).** What did the Repository for Germinal Choice show, and what did Doron Blake conclude?
**A:** Robert Graham's "Nobel" sperm bank was branded falsely: Shockley was the only laureate to admit donating, and no laureate donations produced a child. Doron Blake (IQ 180, magazine covers, bullied) concluded, "I don't think you can breed for good people."

## Section 4: Ch 7 (Marilyn and Me) and Ch 8 (So You Want to Be a Genius?)

**Q1 (SA).** How does Marilyn vos Savant illustrate "genius as self-fulfilling prophecy"?
**A:** Her childhood score sat unnoticed for thirty years; once *Guinness* printed it, attention produced more attention ("Ask Marilyn," books, public opinions on everything). Nothing about her changed; her life did.

**Q2 (SA).** What are galaxy-brainedness and the high-IQ society pathology?
**A:** Galaxy-brainedness is the willingness to hold opinions on everything (Jarvik calling relativity, the Big Bang, and black holes wrong). High-IQ societies (Mensa, Mega, Triple Nine) suffer constant schisms and lawsuits, showing a big number doesn't confer judgment or humility.

**Q3 (MC).** What do IQ tests mainly select for, per Lewis after sitting the Mensa test?
a) persistence  b) speed  c) creativity  d) self-marketing
**A:** b. Not measured: showing up daily, the ego to insist you're right, and the ability to market yourself as the spirit of the age.

**Q4 (SA).** Explain the Potus problem.
**A:** Great discoveries create the conditions for their own under-appreciation. *The West Wing* made "POTUS" ordinary; Shakespeare's psychological complexity is now every drama's baseline; zero once took centuries and now a child grasps it.

**Q5 (SA).** What is the multiple problem, and what is its number?
**A:** Ogburn and Thomas (1922) documented 148 cases of simultaneous independent invention. Inventions become inevitable once preconditions exist, but we remember one name: the one who claimed credit harder, filed first, or self-mythologized.

**Q6 (SA).** What is the Matthew Effect, and what is the "forty-first seat"?
**A:** Merton: to those who have, more is given; recognition converts into labs, grants, first-author credit, and press. The forty-first seat is the Académie française seat the illustrious never got (Descartes, Pascal, Molière, Rousseau, Diderot, Flaubert, Zola, Proust all missed it).

**Q7 (T/F).** In Lewis's account, Fleming's discovery of penicillin involved no luck in his career choices. **A:** False. He chose medicine because of his brother, St Mary's because of water polo, and bacteriology to stay in the rifle club; the mould story also omits the assistant who connected it to an earlier experiment.

## Section 5: Ch 9 (The Rebel) and Ch 10 (Monsters and Tortured Artists)

**Q1 (SA).** What is the Galileo syndrome (Galileo gambit)?
**A:** Taking opposition as evidence of insight: "if everyone says I'm wrong, I must be right." Lewis's line: "You're probably not Galileo."

**Q2 (MC).** The "hoofbeats to horses" heuristic says:
a) heretics are usually right  b) assume the mainstream/obvious explanation while staying open  c) never trust experts  d) zebras are common
**A:** b.

**Q3 (T/F).** Galileo was martyred and died in disgrace for his telescope. **A:** False. He sought papal permission, died in his bed, and was above all a superb lens-maker (3x to 8x to 15x to 20x magnification), also an empiricist, self-publicist, and politician. "*Eppur si muove*" first appears in 1757, a century after the trial.

**Q4 (SA).** What is the Semmelweis reflex, and why is it a problem for the myth?
**A:** Authorities' knee-jerk rejection of challenges (handwashing rejected; he died in an asylum). It is real but rare, and it is the exception that makes the myth. Most "they laughed at him" stories (Edison's phonograph was an instant hit; Columbus didn't prove the earth round) are false.

**Q5 (SA).** What does "benefit of clergy" mean in Orwell's use, and what is the propaganda test?
**A:** The medieval exemption of clerics from civil courts, transferred to artists: being an artist is no excuse for abuse. Propaganda test: separation of art and artist is impossible when the art is propaganda for the crime (Polanski's "celebrations of sexual sadism"; Goode's plays).

**Q6 (SA).** What does Simon Stephens's "contradiction, not causality" mean?
**A:** The gift did not cause the crime; they coexist. Alcoholics who make great art don't make it *because* of alcoholism. Goode was a criminal *and* a kind mentor, an abuser *and* a maker of good art. "The cult of the individual is a lie... we are *dividuals*."

**Q7 (SA).** How did the "special person" aura work in the Chris Goode case?
**A:** It stifled doubt: critics who feared being philistines praised work they found "boring... or theatrically unfruitful," objections were read as pathology, queerness and left politics served as a shield, and an all-male, largely unpaid ensemble was recruited with nudity and sexual contact written into the advert. Afterward he was erased from the internet rather than reckoned with.

## Section 6: Ch 11 (Great Wives) and Ch 12 (Keepers of the Flame)

**Q1 (SA).** What did Sofia Tolstaya do for Tolstoy?
**A:** Married at 18 to a 34-year-old, she had sixteen pregnancies, copied *War and Peace* seven times, edited, kept diaries he mined, supplied material, and kept working in bed after childbirth.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the "reader thesis" (A. N. Wilson)?
**A:** What bound the Tolstoys was that she was his *reader*; the marriage broke when a rival reader, the disciple Chertkov, replaced her. Swap Sofia for Chertkov and you swap *Anna Karenina* for unreadable tracts.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the Wife Glitch, and what is "Yoko-ing"?
**A:** Schaffer's Wife Glitch: someone who anticipates needs, orders the home, tends raw emotion, and fills weaknesses with her strength, "and means it." Yoko-ing is blaming the wife for the decline she was holding back.

**Q4 (T/F).** Einstein's first wife Mileva Marić was a fellow physics student and early sparring partner. **A:** True. The notes describe his contract for her (laundry kept, meals in his room, stop talking when told), after which she was replaced and the "Einstein priests" wrote her out.

**Q5 (SA).** How did *Life* magazine and Lee Krasner shape Pollock's legacy?
**A:** *Life*'s 1949 "Is he the greatest living painter?" and the 1951 "Irascibles" photo created abstract expressionism as a movement (excluding Krasner). As widow she sold one or two Pollocks a year, preferably to museums, and almost single-handedly forced up prices ($2M for *Blue Poles* in 1973; $140M for *No. 5* decades later).

**Q6 (SA).** What was Jo van Gogh-Bonger's playbook?
**A:** Widowed at about 29 with a baby, she (1) hung the work where people live, (2) paired paintings with letters so the life sold the art, (3) converted a hostile critic, (4) organized a 484-work show in 1905, (5) bought back *Starry Night* because "uncomfortable is good," and (6) kept family on the board (three descendants sit on the museum board). Her diary was withheld until 2009.

**Q7 (MC).** Which of these is NOT given as a keeper's tool in Ch 12?
a) sanitizing (Burns)  b) copyright control (Beckett's estate forbidding women in male roles)  c) rationing access to papers  d) public auditing of estates
**A:** d.

## Section 7: Ch 13 (A Cover Version) and Ch 14 (Alchemy)

**Q1 (SA).** What is the shared premise of *Yesterday* and *Cover Version*, and how do they differ?
**A:** A man is the only person who remembers the Beatles. *Yesterday* (Curtis): great talent cannot be repressed. *Cover Version* (Barth): the songs alone don't land without the personalities, era, manager, producer, and luck.

**Q2 (SA).** Which two figures does Lewis say *Yesterday* omits, and why does it matter?
**A:** George Martin (producer) and Brian Epstein (committed manager). The film's manager is a cynical shark who could have made the star anyway, an insult to Epstein's integrity.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the counterfactual test, in one question?
**A:** "Would I buy this from a stranger?" Value the work, not the name. Think of a hit as work x persona x moment x sponsor x luck.

**Q4 (T/F).** The Decca rejection proves the Beatles were an unrecognized revolution. **A:** False in Lewis's account. The audition setlist was mostly covers plus three forgettable originals, and George Martin heard *potential*, not a revolution.

**Q5 (SA).** Give two near-misses from Craig Brown's account of the Beatles.
**A:** (any two) Paul's parents met because bombers flew on one night and not the next; Ringo nearly died of peritonitis; Paul failed Spanish and stayed down a year, meeting George; they dodged conscription; they found Epstein as they considered quitting; Ringo nearly emigrated but the forms were too many.

**Q6 (SA).** Distinguish conceptual and experimental innovators (Galenson). Who is an example of each?
**A:** Conceptual: big-idea makers who peak early and are most often called geniuses (Picasso). Experimental: slow refiners over decades (Cezanne). Culture glorifies the conceptual because it explains why we aren't geniuses.

**Q7 (SA).** According to Simonton, when should a genius die for the myth, and how did the Beatles fit?
**A:** Young (Mozart) or very old (Goethe), never at sixty. The Beatles ended at their peak (George was 27) and refused to reform; Lennon was murdered in 1980 and sainted; McCartney lived long enough to become patriarch and manager of the catalogue.

## Section 8: Ch 15 (The Deficit Model) and Ch 16 (Disruptors)

**Q1 (SA).** What is the deficit model?
**A:** The idea that genius extorts a price: for every credit a debit, with oddness as the currency (Beethoven deaf, Milton blind, Mozart a child who couldn't handle adulthood). The flaw makes the gift feel earned.

**Q2 (SA).** Name the three biopic tropes Lewis identifies.
**A:** Inspiration as a blinding flash, genius as an unworldly being, and suffering as payment. Also: the lone rebel against an uncaring regime, and the great man as the sun with everyone orbiting.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the Hawking timeline test?
**A:** Ask whether the suffering precedes or follows the work. Hawking did his best work *before* his deterioration, so the link between illness and insight is narrative, not causal.

**Q4 (SA).** What is the "shadow film" *The Laws of Madness* and what does it teach?
**A:** Michael Laudor (schizophrenia, Yale Law Review editor) got a 1995 *NYT* profile hitting every deficit beat, a $600K+ memoir deal and $1.5M film rights. Off his medication, he killed his girlfriend, believing her an impostor. Intelligence did not save him, though the myth said it could. It is the corrective to *A Beautiful Mind*.

**Q5 (T/F).** Silicon Valley's success was built largely without public institutions. **A:** False. Its scenius rested on no non-competes in California, Stanford, and Department of Defense funding (GPS via Naval Research; the internet via DARPA). It was publicly nurtured and privately mythologized.

**Q6 (MC).** Disruption theory (Christensen), as Lewis critiques it, ignores:
a) cheap rivals  b) lobbying, shaping of laws, weak antitrust, and giants buying rivals (Facebook bought about thirty companies in two years)  c) venture capital  d) customers
**A:** b.

**Q7 (SA).** What is the "genius asshole" archetype?
**A:** Jobs as the "Genius Entrepreneur," who convinced everyone that the assholery was indivisible from the genius (Wozniak: a nicer Apple "may never have made the Macintosh"). Lewis treats it as a story the geniuses tell about themselves, an empirical claim nobody has tested.

## Section 9: Ch 17 (Thomas Edison) and Ch 18 (Elon Musk)

**Q1 (SA).** What did Lewis identify as Edison's real conceptual breakthrough, and how did it arise?
**A:** The phonograph (1877), an accidental byproduct while the lab was hunting telephone diaphragms; a finger on a vibrating membrane suggested a stylus and a record.

**Q2 (SA).** How was the lightbulb "de-mythed"?
**A:** Incandescence (Davy, 1802-09), a carbon rod in vacuum (Jobard, 1838), and a working prototype (Swan, 1860) preceded him. Edison's problem was not conceptual: a better vacuum (Sprengel pump), a longer filament (Batchelor's carbonised thread: 40 hours), and reliable supply. The grid was his most underrated innovation.

**Q3 (SA).** What was the "access-for-hype machine"?
**A:** Favoured reporters got exclusives; Edison mentioned imminent breakthroughs; reporters got a story, Edison got publicity. One *Herald* writer received eight shares in Edison Electric. A genius created by an admiring group with a commercial interest in his veneration.

**Q4 (SA).** What are Mac and Conger's "three problems," and what is the lesson?
**A:** SpaceX was a physics problem, Tesla a manufacturing problem, Twitter a social and psychological one. Genius is not a transferable skill.

**Q5 (SA).** What numbers and facts illustrate the Twitter outcome and the taxpayer scaffolding under Musk's firms?
**A:** Fidelity marked the $44B purchase down 71% to about $10B. SpaceX was rescued by a $1.6B NASA contract (Dec 2008) and Tesla buyers got $7,500 credits.

**Q6 (SA).** Who was Mary Beth Brown, and what does "MB" stand for in Lewis's argument?
**A:** Musk's assistant for twelve years, the "only bridge" between him and his interests, let go in 2014 reportedly after asking for executive-level pay (Musk disputes). "MB" stands for all the support structure ("the rest of us") that moderates a genius; shedding her and the grown-ups preceded the Twitter chaos.

**Q7 (T/F).** Lewis says Musk has shown no acts of genius at all. **A:** False. Reusable rockets (and making EVs desirable) are acts of genius; the problem is his becoming a special person, an omni-expert radicalized by his own platform. It is both/and, not either/or.

## Section 10: Ch 19 (Conclusion: An Idea That Won't Stay Dead)

**Q1 (SA).** What is the secularization arc (Fara)?
**A:** Greek/Roman possession, then the Romantic secular saint, then the Victorian number, then today's granular data; each an attempt to pin down genius's random, unpredictable nature.

**Q2 (SA).** List the four functions of the myth in Lewis's functional account.
**A:** (1) A mechanism for letting the weird prosper; (2) a mechanism for managing envy (we're just Gauss's classmates); (3) a way to reconcile ourselves to monstrosity paired with achievement; (4) sneaking admiration for the one who gets away with it.

**Q3 (SA).** What are the "two wastes" (Gould, Spencer)?
**A:** Some talents are scorched by indulgence; far more are extinguished for lack of support (cotton fields, sweatshops, marriage bars, segregation). "Before he can remake his society, his society must make him."

**Q4 (SA).** Why does Lewis think AI doesn't remove the need for genius myths?
**A:** LLMs are prediction machines lacking originality; great works are unpredictable, wrong by the previous rules. Substitute ChatGPT for Dostoevsky "and the magic is gone." We need myths, and myths need people.

**Q5 (SA).** State Lewis's prescription.
**A:** Attach "genius" to acts and works, not persons (the painting, the transistor, *War and Peace*, not Shockley or Tolstoy). Celebrate talent, be humble about where it's found and whether it's recognized, and nurture as many seeds as we can.

**Q6 (T/F).** Lewis advises debunking the myth entirely and denying talent and achievement. **A:** False. She warns against "debunking into nihilism" and notes the myth is load-bearing: remove it only if you replace its functions.

## Cumulative Final Exam

1. **State Lewis's thesis in one sentence and explain "had to was."**
   *A:* Grant acts of genius, deny the class. Genius shifted from something you had (a visiting spirit) to something you are, which creates "Special People" who get latitude nobody else gets.

2. **Run the six-step "distortion audit" on any famous figure. Name the steps.**
   *A:* Potus (what did the world lack before them?); multiples (who else got there?); adjacent possible (which components had just arrived?); Matthew Effect (how did recognition compound, who got the forty-first seat?); weirdness (which eccentricities were folded into the legend?); luck (which trivial decisions routed them here?).

3. **What did Terman's study, Burt's data, and the Mensa-type societies each show about IQ and genius?**
   *A:* Terman: high childhood IQ predicts a good life, not genius (missed Shockley and Alvarez). Burt: identical correlations across changed samples signal fraud, and the "genius exemption" defended it. High-IQ societies: schisms show a number confers no judgment or works of genius.

4. **How did Galton, Shockley, and Musk each end up in "Galton's territory"?**
   *A:* Galton founded eugenics from the idea of inherited, countable genius; Shockley became an omni-expert eugenicist and donated to the genius sperm bank; Musk's myth led him to population collapse, "woke mind virus," and IQ/breeding talk. The notes say the myth "ends where it began: Galton."

5. **Explain scenius with two examples from the book and what the myth leaves out.**
   *A:* Florence in 1450 (Leonardo); Shockley's Caltech and Bell Labs; Silicon Valley (Stanford, DARPA, no non-competes). The myth credits only the individual and omits place, time, identity, and public funding.

6. **Why is "they all laughed at him" suspect, and what test should you apply when you feel like Galileo?**
   *A:* Most such stories are false (Edison's phonograph was an instant hit; Columbus didn't prove the earth round) and Semmelweis is the rare exception that fuels the myth. Hoofbeats to horses: assume the obvious, stay open, and set an update trigger before the evidence arrives; you're probably not Galileo.

7. **What do the great-wife, widow, and Beatles-support chapters share?**
   *A:* Genius runs on invisible others: Sofia Tolstaya (reader, copyist, editor), Krasner and Jo van Gogh-Bonger (legacy managers), George Martin and Brian Epstein (producer and manager). Ask "who cooked the dinner?" and who holds the papers.

8. **What are the propaganda test and the Hawking test for?**
   *A:* Propaganda test: whether art and artist can be separated (not if the art argues for the perpetrator's perversion). Hawking test: whether suffering preceded or followed the work, to check whether a suffering-insight link is narrative.

9. **Compare Edison and Musk using three shared features.**
   *A:* (any three) a media-built myth (reporters with equity; fans and platform); conceptual early peak followed by calcification or omni-opinion ("the Edison horizon"); "hardcore" long-hours culture; reliance on hidden support (teams, the grid, NASA contracts, an MB); playing the genius in public ("they want it").

10. **Applied task:** Pick a living "genius" in the news. Run the distortion audit and the three-problems test, list who cooked the dinner, and write one sentence that attaches "genius" to an act rather than the person.
