## How to use this quiz book

1. Work one chapter at a time, right after reading or reviewing it. Cover the answers. Answer out loud or in writing, with a number wherever a number is asked for.
2. Question types: **SA** short answer, **T/F** true or false (say why), **MC** multiple choice, **EXP** explain in your own words, **SCN** scenario or application.
3. Score yourself honestly: full credit only if you hit the key idea and the number or story where one is asked for. Mark each miss and reread that chapter's notes.
4. Then do the 20-question cumulative final exam at the end, a few days later if you can (spaced retrieval beats rereading).
5. Fitting for this book: write down a confidence percentage before you check each answer, then see whether your 70% answers are right about 70% of the time.

## Chapter 1: An Optimistic Skeptic

**Q1 (SA).** What does it mean to be an "optimistic skeptic" about forecasting?
**A:** Holding two truths at once. The skeptic half: nonlinear systems (Lorenz's butterfly, the Arab Spring sparked by one street vendor) impose hard limits, and accuracy falls toward chance 3-5 years out. The optimist half: much of reality is predictable to some degree under some circumstances, and forecasters can improve if they measure.

**Q2 (T/F).** The "dart-throwing chimp" finding shows that all expert forecasts are useless.
**A:** False. The finding was about the *average* expert in Expert Political Judgment (1984-2004) on many, especially long-range, questions. On shorter horizons some people show real, testable foresight. "All expert forecasts are useless" is the garbled telephone version of the result.

**Q3 (MC).** Weather forecasts are reliable at 1-2 days and chimp-level at about 8 days. Which idea does this best illustrate? (a) Dart-throwing chimp (b) Clocks vs. clouds as a spectrum (c) Wrong-side-of-maybe (d) Extremizing
**A:** (b). Predictability is not a yes/no property; it depends on *what* you predict, *how far out*, and *under what circumstances*. Laplace's clock-like demon and Lorenz's cloud are the two ends of a spectrum.

**Q4 (SA).** Name the loop meteorologists run that most forecasting domains never run, and say why most domains skip it.
**A:** Forecast, measure, revise. Without measurement there is no revision, and without revision no improvement. Most domains skip it because the consumers of forecasts do not demand evidence of accuracy.

**Q5 (SA).** Give the key facts about the Good Judgment Project (GJP): size, question type, and headline results.
**A:** 20,000+ volunteers, about 500 IARPA questions over four years, more than 1 million judgments, questions mostly 1-12 months out. GJP beat the control group by 60% in year 1 and 78% in year 2, beat university rivals by 30-70%, and beat intelligence analysts with classified access. About 2% of forecasters were "super."

**Q6 (EXP).** The notes list a "jumble of forecasting goals." Name at least four goals besides accuracy and explain why this jumble matters.
**A:** To entertain (Cramer's "booyah"), to galvanize action (activist warnings), to impress (bank pundits on 2050), to comfort (partisan warm baths). Because accuracy is rarely the sole goal, nobody insists on measuring it, so the feedback loop never starts.

**Q7 (EXP).** Why does the difference between 60/40 and 40/60 matter?
**A:** Small sustained edges compound. The notes say it separates poker heavyweights from amateurs; Aaron Brown: it is "the difference between a consistent winner and the guy going broke." So forecasters must learn to distinguish small differences in probability.

**Q8 (T/F).** A one-hour tutorial on basic forecasting concepts has no measurable effect on accuracy.
**A:** False. A one-hour tutorial on basic concepts (the Ten Commandments) improved accuracy about 10% across a tournament year.

**Q9 (SCN).** A manager's company pays a consultancy for annual market forecasts. Nobody has ever checked how accurate the past forecasts were. Which concept applies and what do you ask for?
**A:** "Buying forecasts without asking for accuracy," which the notes compare to swallowing untested medicine. Ask for dated, defined, numeric past forecasts scored against outcomes (forecast-measure-revise). Until then the track record is unknown, like Tom Friedman's, which has never been tested.

**Q10 (SCN).** Two candidates could advise your team on geopolitical risk. One is famous, confident, and tells compelling stories; the other is unknown but has ~300 scored, dated forecasts with an excellent record. How should you choose, and what story from the chapter supports your choice?
**A:** Choose on scored results, not fame or confidence. Bill Flack (retired USDA employee in Kearney, Nebraska, who dropped out of a math PhD) answered about 300 questions, each dated, recorded and scored by independent observers; Friedman advises presidents but was never scored. The difference is not that Bill is smarter; it is that his forecasts were scored. Reliance on Bill is reasonable, and any intelligent, open-minded, hardworking person can learn what he does.

## Chapter 2: Illusions of Knowledge

**Q1 (SA).** What is the "tip-of-your-nose perspective" and can you switch it off?
**A:** System 1's fast, automatic, subjective view of the world. You cannot switch it off; you can only monitor it and have System 2 check it when time and stakes allow.

**Q2 (SA).** What does WYSIATI stand for, and what does it imply about evidence quality?
**A:** "What You See Is All There Is." System 1 jumps to conclusions from little evidence and is insensitive to the quality, and the absence, of evidence. System 2 is the slow, expensive interrogator (the bat-and-ball "ten cents" shows System 2 often does not get involved).

**Q3 (EXP).** Explain "bait and switch" (attribute substitution) with an example from the chapter.
**A:** Faced with a hard question we silently answer an easier one. "Should I fear the shadow?" becomes "Can I recall a lion attack?" Archie Cochrane swapped "Do I have cancer?" for "Is this the sort of man who would know?"

**Q4 (SA).** Why did medicine stagnate for millennia, and what ended the stagnation?
**A:** Physicians trusted the view from the tip of their nose (the notes cite Galen: the treatment cures all except the incurable, who die). Progress began when they accepted doubt and ran controlled trials. The fix was "a tablespoon of doubt."

**Q5 (SA).** Why does random assignment matter in a randomized controlled trial (Bradford Hill, 1921 template)?
**A:** Random assignment balances unknown differences between groups, so outcomes can be attributed to the treatment rather than to who happened to get it.

**Q6 (EXP).** Retell Archie Cochrane's 1956 story and the lesson.
**A:** Cochrane, a founder of evidence-based medicine, found an armpit lump. An eminent specialist removed it and, before the pathologist reported, cut out his pectoralis minor and told him he was dying. Cochrane sobbed and made peace with it; the pathology showed no cancer. He "never doubted the surgeon's words." The story fit and he substituted "is this the kind of person who would know?" Lesson: nobody is immune to the tip-of-the-nose view; the defense is a habit: pause, ask what else could explain this, and wait for the pathologist.

**Q7 (EXP).** What was the "Cochrane trick" with the cardiologists, and what did it reveal?
**A:** He told cardiologists that preliminary results favored the cardiac units; they demanded he stop the "unethical" trial. Then he revealed he had reversed the numbers: home care was actually ahead. Their reaction, not the data, exposed their unwarranted certainty (the "God complex").

**Q8 (MC).** According to the Kahneman-Klein reconciliation, intuition can be trusted when: (a) the person is senior (b) the environment has valid cues and enough practice to learn them (c) the person feels confident (d) the answer comes fast
**A:** (b). Examples: fireground commanders and chess. It fails in cue-poor settings such as stock picking. Even Magnus Carlsen "double-checks." So "blink vs. think" is a false dichotomy.

**Q9 (SCN).** A product lead has read three glowing customer emails and declares "the market loves this." Which concepts apply, and what is the scientist's test?
**A:** WYSIATI (the emails felt like all there was) plus confirmation bias (grab the first plausible story, gather support). The scientist's test: ask "What would convince me I am wrong?" If you cannot answer, you are too attached. Also ask "has this been tested, or does it just feel right?"

**Q10 (SCN).** A government rolls out a "short, sharp shock" policy nationally with no comparison group and claims it worked. What is the notes' verdict?
**A:** Thatcher's "short, sharp shock" is the notes' example of an uncontrolled experiment that yields only the illusion of insight. Without a controlled comparison you cannot attribute the outcome to the policy.

## Chapter 3: Keeping Score

**Q1 (SA).** What four things make a forecast scorable?
**A:** (1) A clear time frame, (2) explicitly defined terms, (3) a numeric probability, (4) many forecasts so calibration can be computed.

**Q2 (SA).** Give Kent's numeric scale.
**A:** Certain 100%; almost certain 93% ±6; probable 75% ±12; chances about even 50% ±10; probably not 30% ±10; almost certainly not 7% ±5; impossible 0%. Kent: "I'd rather be a bookie than a goddamn poet."

**Q3 (EXP).** What was wrong with "serious possibility" in NIE 29-51 and the Bay of Pigs "fair chance"?
**A:** Words are elastic. "Serious possibility" meant 80/20 to one analyst and 20/80 to another. In the Bay of Pigs memo "fair chance" was meant as 3-to-1 *against*, but Kennedy heard optimism.

**Q4 (T/F).** If a forecaster says 70% and the event does not happen, the forecast was wrong.
**A:** False. That is the wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy. A 70% forecast also says 30% no. You need many forecasts to judge. The fallacy is why forecasters retreat into rubbery words like "a fair chance."

**Q5 (SA).** Define calibration and resolution. What does it mean to be "well-calibrated but cowardly"?
**A:** Calibration: events you call 70% happen 70% of the time (above the diagonal = underconfident, below = overconfident). Resolution: decisiveness, high probabilities on things that happen and low ones on things that do not. "Cowardly" means calibrated but hovering at 40-60%.

**Q6 (SA).** Explain the Brier score: scale endpoints and what 0.5 means.
**A:** Glenn Brier, 1950: squared distance between forecast and outcome. 0 = perfect, 0.5 = random or hedged 50-50, 2.0 = 100% confident and always wrong. It punishes hubris like a bet at 19-to-1 odds.

**Q7 (EXP).** Why is "a Brier of 0.2" not meaningful on its own? Use the Springfield/Phoenix example.
**A:** It depends on difficulty. 0.2 is excellent in Springfield (hard weather) and poor in Phoenix ("always sunny" scores near 0). Compare against "no change" and "recent rate of change" rules and against other forecasters on the same questions over the same periods, which is why tournaments are needed.

**Q8 (MC).** In the Expert Political Judgment study (284 experts, about 28,000 predictions), who did better? (a) Hedgehogs in their own specialty (b) Foxes, on both calibration and resolution (c) Famous experts (d) Experts with a Big Idea
**A:** (b). Hedgehogs (one Big Idea, "green-tinted glasses," "furthermore, moreover," certain-or-impossible, "just wait") did worse even in their own specialty and worse than chance on long horizons. Foxes (many tools, "however, on the other hand," probabilities, changing their minds) won. Fame correlated inversely with accuracy.

**Q9 (EXP).** What is the dragonfly eye and what is Galton's ox story about?
**A:** Aggregating many perspectives into one image. Galton's ox: the crowd's average was 1,197 lb vs. the true 1,198 lb. It works because valid information points one way while errors cancel. Foxes do inside one skull what crowds do collectively.

**Q10 (SCN).** A think-tank letter warns a central bank policy "risks currency debasement and inflation." How would a superforecaster rewrite it, and why bother?
**A:** Add time frame, defined measure and numbers, e.g. "If the Fed continues large-scale asset purchases through 2011, probability US CPI inflation exceeds 4% year-over-year in any month before 31 Dec 2013 is 65%; probability the trade-weighted dollar falls more than 15% from its November 2010 level is 40%." Now it can be scored, and "note the word risk, note the absence of a date" is no longer an escape. Choosing 4% vs. 3% and CPI vs. "Wall Street inflation" is where the real forecasting work lives.

**Q11 (SCN).** A news columnist declares a prediction market "wrong" because its 75% outcome did not occur. Which fallacy, and what is the correct reading?
**A:** Wrong-side-of-maybe (the notes cite Leonhardt on Obamacare). A 75% forecast means a 25% chance of the other outcome; one miss says little. Judge it with calibration over many forecasts.

## Chapter 4: Superforecasters

**Q1 (EXP).** What does "reasonable vs. correct" mean, and how does it apply to the Iraq WMD estimate?
**A:** Judge a decision by whether it was reasonable given what was knowable then, not by the outcome. The IC's WMD conclusion was reasonable *and* wrong. The sin was the unreasonable certainty ("slam dunk"); an estimate of 60-70% instead of "Baghdad has..." might have stopped the invasion.

**Q2 (SA).** What was striking about NIE 2002-16HC's process?
**A:** It used statements of no surprise ("Iraq has... Baghdad has..."), had no red teams, no devil's advocates, and no paper proposing the view now believed true. The absence of any dissent was the scandal.

**Q3 (EXP).** Why is accountability for process (checklists) not enough?
**A:** The notes compare it to hand-washing without checking whether treatment works. Checking "did you consider alternatives?" is not the same as tracking accuracy. Only systematic accuracy tracking is meaningful accountability.

**Q4 (SA).** Describe the IARPA tournament design.
**A:** Five teams, daily forecasts at 9 a.m., Sept 2011 to June 2015, about 500 Goldilocks-zone questions (months, not years). Teams had to beat the control crowd by 20% in year 1 rising to 50% by year 4. Internal randomized experiments tested training, teaming and markets.

**Q5 (SA).** What was the winning recipe for aggregation?
**A:** Identify the top ~2% by accuracy and update frequency, weight them more, then extremize the aggregate (push 70% toward 85%, 30% toward 15%) to simulate what the crowd would believe if everyone held everyone's information.

**Q6 (SA).** Tell the Doug Lorch numbers.
**A:** Retired IBM programmer. Year 1 Brier 0.22 (5th of 2,800). Year 2 Brier 0.14 (best); beat a prediction market by 40% and the control crowd by 60%, alone exceeding IARPA's fourth-year target.

**Q7 (SA).** Give the first-cohort superforecaster numbers.
**A:** 58 superforecasters, collective Brier 0.25 vs. 0.37 for everyone else. By year 4 they beat regulars by more than 60%; at 300 days out they were more accurate than regulars at 100 days. About 30% better than intelligence analysts with classified access.

**Q8 (EXP).** How does regression to the mean separate luck from skill, and what did the tournament show?
**A:** Fast regression means chance; slow means skill. Father-son height correlation is about 0.5; superforecasters' year-to-year correlation about 0.65. About 30% drop out of the top 2% each year and ~70% remain; the chance of that consistency among coin-flippers is under 1 in 100 million. Mostly skill, no infallibles.

**Q9 (EXP).** Why did the supers as a group *increase* their lead in years 2 and 3, which luck alone should not allow?
**A:** An offsetting push: being named "super" and placed on elite teams raised performance enough to mask the regression luck would otherwise produce. Individually, 0.65 correlation and 30% turnover are exactly what skill-plus-luck predicts.

**Q10 (SCN).** A fund manager is praised because she "called the 2008 crash." What question does the chapter tell you to ask?
**A:** "How many were throwing darts?" (survivorship reasoning). Without knowing how many people were calling crashes you cannot tell luck from skill. Check year-to-year persistence. Langer's illusion of prediction: Yale students shown early lucky streaks on rigged coin flips thought they had skill.

**Q11 (SCN).** A boss wants to replace a risk review with a signed checklist saying "alternatives considered." Which concepts apply?
**A:** Accountability for process vs. accuracy (hand-washing analogy) and unanimity as a warning sign. Keep the process, but also score actual forecasts' accuracy. Absence of a dissenting paper was the WMD scandal.

## Chapter 5: Supersmart?

**Q1 (SA).** How smart are superforecasters relative to regular forecasters?
**A:** Regulars scored above about 70% of the population, supers above about 80% (top ~20%, not top 1%). The big jump is public to forecaster, not forecaster to super. It is a threshold, not a gradient. McNamara's "best and brightest" escalated Vietnam without analyzing assumptions.

**Q2 (SA).** What is Fermi-izing, and why does it "dare us to be wrong"?
**A:** Decompose an unanswerable question into sub-questions ("what would I need to know?"), separate knowable from unknowable, and make explicit guesses. It exposes guesses so errors can be inspected rather than hidden in a black box.

**Q3 (EXP).** Why outside view before inside view?
**A:** Anchoring: we under-adjust from wherever we start, so a meaningful anchor (the base rate of "things of this sort in situations of this sort") beats a meaningless one. Case details then adjust it.

**Q4 (SA).** What does refining the reference class mean? Give the pet example.
**A:** Use a closer comparison class when one exists: all US households own pets 62%; single-family detached homes ~73%.

**Q5 (SA).** How do you get an outside view when no base rate exists? Give the exhumation example.
**A:** Fermi-ize it. For "a famous dead man exhumed on suspicion," the probability must be well above 0 and well below 100; say 20-80%, midpoint 50%.

**Q6 (EXP).** Walk through David Rogg's early-2015 forecast.
**A:** Question: will Islamist militants attack in one of eight Western European countries between 21 Jan and 31 Mar 2015 (69 days), just after *Charlie Hebdo*? Outside view: six attacks in five years on a Wikipedia list = 1.2/year. Inside view: ISIS's rise made pre-2010 data stale (dropped it), base rate 1.5/year ("still low"); post-*Hebdo* security surges cut the other way; net raise by about one-fifth to 1.8/year. 69/365 × 1.8 ≈ 0.34 = 34%. He posted the whole chain on the team forum to invite disagreement.

**Q7 (SA).** What is the "crowd within" and the question-flipping trick?
**A:** Assume your first answer is wrong and produce a second; it is nearly as good as a second person. Flip the wording (e.g. "Will South Africa *deny* the Dalai Lama a visa?") to counter confirmation bias.

**Q8 (SA).** Define active open-mindedness (AOM) and name the Doug Lorch example.
**A:** Jonathan Baron's idea: contrary evidence deserves attention and changing one's mind is not weakness; beliefs are hypotheses, not treasures. Lorch built a program selecting his reading for ideological and geographic diversity.

**Q9 (SCN).** A manager reacts to a new competitor product launch by saying "this will crush us, 80%!" Which method is skipped and what would you do?
**A:** The outside view. Start with how often incumbents lose to products "of this sort in situations of this sort," then adjust for specifics (hypothesis by hypothesis: what would have to be true?). The notes' anti-pattern: diving into story-friendly details before checking how common the outcome is (Peggy Noonan on Bush's rebounding approval, when all ex-presidents' approval rises).

**Q10 (SCN).** Your engineer estimates a task at one hour. Apply the correction from the chapter.
**A:** Summers's planning-fallacy fix: double the estimate and move up one time unit (an hour becomes two days).

**Q11 (T/F).** On the Arafat question, "Did Israel poison Arafat?" and "Will Swiss or French labs find elevated polonium?" are the same question.
**A:** False; that is bait and switch. The forecastable question is about lab findings, not the emotionally salient who-did-it.

## Chapter 6: Superquants?

**Q1 (SA).** What is the "three-setting dial" and why is it flawed for forecasting?
**A:** Tversky's joke: "gonna happen," "not gonna happen," "maybe." It is evolutionarily sensible (lion: run, stay alert, relax) but yes and no express certainty and nothing is certain. The only honest setting is maybe, subdivided finely.

**Q2 (EXP).** Explain "right but unreasonable; wrong but reasonable" using *Zero Dark Thirty* and the WMD estimate.
**A:** Maya's "100%" was right and unreasonable; the IC saying "60-70% WMD" would have been wrong and reasonable. Outcomes do not retroactively fix or break the reasoning.

**Q3 (SA).** What was the real Panetta's attitude to disagreement vs. the fictional Panetta's?
**A:** The fictional Panetta wanted unanimity; the real one wanted honest, diverse estimates (30% to 90%+) and treated them as gift-wrapped wisdom of the crowd. Universal agreement is a groupthink flag.

**Q4 (SA).** What does "fifty-fifty" usually mean, and what did Obama say?
**A:** Usually "I don't know"; frequent users of 50% are measurably less accurate. Obama said "this is a coin flip" when the median estimate was ~70%, which may have been executive triage or a retreat to the ignorance prior that discarded information.

**Q5 (SA).** Distinguish epistemic from aleatory uncertainty, and say what supers do on cloudlike questions.
**A:** Epistemic = knowable in principle (clocklike); aleatory = unknowable (cloudlike, e.g. rain in Philadelphia a year out). On cloudlike questions supers stay within 35-65% and move out tentatively.

**Q6 (SA).** What did Mellers find about granularity?
**A:** Accuracy ranked ones > fives > tens. Rounding supers' forecasts to the nearest 0.05 hurt their scores; rounding regulars to 0.2 barely mattered, so the precision is real, not bafflegab.

**Q7 (EXP).** What is the "how, not why" principle?
**A:** "Everything happens for a reason" thinking correlates with well-being but negatively with Brier scores. Probabilistic thinkers take the outside view even on identity-defining events: "Why me? Why not me?"

**Q8 (MC).** The "certainty premium" means people will pay: (a) the same to go 10%-5% as 5%-0% (b) 2-3x more to go from 5% to 0% risk than from 10% to 5% (c) nothing for risk reduction (d) more for 50% reductions
**A:** (b). It shows our pull toward certainty. Related: people prefer confident advisers even with identical records, and middling probabilities read as laziness (confidence-competence conflation).

**Q9 (SCN).** At a bin Laden-style meeting, advisers' estimates are 95%, 95%, ~80%, 60%, 40%, 30%. The leader wants one number. What should happen?
**A:** Don't demand consensus (disagreement means independent thinking). Aggregate: median ~70%; consider weighting by track records; extremize modestly if advisers hold different scraps of information (nudging to 80-85%). Separate the forecast from the decision rule (if the president would act above, say, 30%, the exact number matters less). Do not say "fifty-fifty," which discards the room's information.

**Q10 (SCN).** An analyst says "nothing is 100%" in the briefing and then calls the outcome "a slam dunk." Name the anti-pattern and the Rubin rule.
**A:** "Nothing is 100%" stated, then "slam dunk" practiced. Rubin's rule: "absolutely" is unacceptable; say 60% and argue whether it's 59. Also: treating 80% as "it will happen" is an error (Rubin had to pound the table).

## Chapter 7: Supernewsjunkies?

**Q1 (SA).** How much more accurate were supers' *initial* forecasts than others', and why does that matter?
**A:** About 50% more accurate. Supers win not only by updating but by starting better. But updating is as hard as forecasting.

**Q2 (EXP).** What is underreaction through belief perseverance? Use General DeWitt and the Jenga tower.
**A:** Commitment freezes belief. DeWitt, 1942: "the fact that no sabotage has occurred is a disturbing and confirming indication that it will." The deeper a belief sits in your Jenga tower (identity, expertise, public statements), the harder it is to pull. Supers have little ego invested per question.

**Q3 (EXP).** What is the dilution effect and what financial evidence is given?
**A:** Irrelevant details (Robert plays tennis; David skis) sway judgment that should ignore them. Traders churn on noise: highest-turnover households earned 11.4% vs. 17.9% for the market. Overreaction comes from lack of commitment.

**Q4 (SA).** Tell the Tim Minto numbers.
**A:** Year-3 champion, Brier 0.15; on a Syrian-refugee question he updated 34 times with an average move of 3.5 points; final Brier 0.07. Frequent and small captures new information without discarding the old.

**Q5 (SA).** State Bayes' theorem in the form used in the notes and how supers use it.
**A:** Posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds. They know it but rarely compute it; they update in proportion to the diagnostic weight of evidence (how much more likely is this if the hypothesis is true than false).

**Q6 (EXP).** What did Ulfelder's Hagel analysis show?
**A:** Base rate 23 of 24 nominees confirmed (96%). Even granting the critic generous likelihoods (1 in 5 successful nominees botch hearings; 19 in 20 doomed ones do), the posterior fell only to 83%. Hagel was confirmed two weeks later.

**Q7 (EXP).** When should you break the many-small-steps rule? Use Lorch's example.
**A:** When the basis of the forecast collapses (Orwell's sixth rule). Lorch's 95% rested on a month-old report clearly failing; the right move was a fresh forecast (95% to 55% to 15%), then resume small steps.

**Q8 (SA).** Which bait-and-switch caused Flack's updating failure on Yasukuni?
**A:** He answered "would *I* visit Yasukuni if I were PM?" and so discounted the insider tip that Abe would. Abe went. Ignoring a signal because the actor's choice seems irrational to you is the anti-pattern.

**Q9 (SCN).** Worked example: Silver gives Republicans 60% for the Senate; next day a Colorado poll moves the GOP candidate 45% to 55%. How do you update and what is the method?
**A:** Bracket it. More than zero (real information), no more than ~10 points (one race among many). Ask how many winnable races beyond the minimum, whether Colorado's drivers appear elsewhere, how predictive September polls are. Bracket narrows 2-9, then 3-7, settle at +4: 64%.

**Q10 (SCN).** A team lead has publicly staked her reputation on a project timeline. New data suggests slippage. Which bias should she expect in herself?
**A:** Underreaction from Jenga-block bias: updating would topple something about who she is. Compensate deliberately; write forecasts so they can be discarded cheaply; ask for the likelihood ratio of each item.

**Q11 (SCN).** You are swamped with 40 open questions and ignore the stale ones. What risk does the chapter flag?
**A:** Failing to sweep out stale forecasts (Frankel's 82% that should have been 99%). Method: alerts on key terms (Devyn Duffy: 2,271 forecasts on 140 questions in one season).

## Chapter 8: Perpetual Beta

**Q1 (SA).** What is "perpetual beta" and how strong a predictor is it?
**A:** The cycle try, fail, analyze, adjust, try again, rooted in growth mindset plus grit. It predicts success about 3x as strongly as intelligence: "75% perspiration, 25% inspiration."

**Q2 (SA).** Contrast fixed and growth mindset (Dweck), and name Mary Simpson's reason for joining GJP.
**A:** Fixed: abilities are innate; the brain engages with right/wrong but not with information that could improve the answer. Growth: abilities come from effort. Simpson, an economics PhD who missed 2008 and lost savings, joined because "I should be better at this."

**Q3 (EXP).** What did Keynes's career show?
**A:** He lost nearly everything in 1920 and again in 1929, subjected his assumptions to withering scrutiny, and invented something close to value investing. His line: "there is no harm in being sometimes wrong, especially if one is promptly found out."

**Q4 (SA).** Why doesn't practice alone suffice (Polanyi's bicycle)?
**A:** Tacit knowledge requires doing, but practice must be informed (the training booklet gave ~10% and interacted positively with practice) and must have feedback. "Free throws in the dark" breeds confidence, not skill.

**Q5 (EXP).** What two obstacles destroy forecasting feedback?
**A:** (1) Ambiguous language: vague forecasts stretch to fit outcomes (Forer effect; astrology boilerplate rated 4.2/5 as a personal profile). (2) Time lag plus hindsight bias: experts recalled 1988 Soviet-collapse estimates 31 points too high; one moved 20% to 70%.

**Q6 (SA).** Why are meteorologists and bridge players well calibrated but police lie-detectors not?
**A:** Feedback is prompt and unambiguous for the first two. Police confidence grows with experience while accuracy doesn't. Calibration is domain-specific; bridge skill doesn't transfer to politics.

**Q7 (EXP).** Why postmortem successes? Give the Duffy example and the counterfactual asymmetry.
**A:** To check luck vs. skill: "we lucked out" (Duffy on Guinea). Experts embrace "I was almost right" counterfactuals and reject "I was almost wrong" ones.

**Q8 (SA).** What does a Brier of 1.46 on Abe/Yasukuni illustrate?
**A:** Unambiguous failure and therefore a learning opportunity ("lights on"). Also: Beugoms now leaves comments for his future self since sparse notes made postmortems impossible.

**Q9 (SCN).** Reconstruct Duffy's Arafat-polonium postmortem as a template for your team after a failed estimate.
**A:** What we forecast (low probability of detection); what we assumed (polonium's short half-life makes detection virtually impossible); source (our own physics intuition, unchecked); what we didn't do (consider decay products, ask someone with radiochemistry expertise; a teammate had read the Swiss report); luck or process? Process. Adjustments: don't assume expertise you lack, find an expert, schedule re-examination of foundational assumptions. Contrast with "I saw it coming all along" after Gorbachev.

**Q10 (SCN).** A colleague says "I'm just bad at forecasting" after one miss. Respond using the chapter.
**A:** Identity statement = fixed mindset anti-pattern. Also avoid over-learning from a minor technical slip on a sound approach, or under-learning from one exposing a flawed assumption. Add grit: Sloane forecasted through brain-cancer treatment; Kilkenny e-mailed the UN agency (in English after a French reply) to learn how refugee data were compiled.

## Chapter 9: Superteams

**Q1 (SA).** Define groupthink (Janis) and the Kennedy fixes.
**A:** Cohesive groups maintain esprit de corps by unconsciously developing shared illusions that interfere with critical thinking; agreement is taken as proof. Fixes: speak as generalists licensed to question anything; watchdogs/devil's advocates (Bobby Kennedy, Sorensen); suspend protocol and hierarchy; bring in outsiders; the leader leaves the room and hides his preference so ten alternatives are discussed.

**Q2 (EXP).** Contrast the Bay of Pigs and the Cuban missile crisis as process audits.
**A:** Bay of Pigs (April 1961): plan on the *New York Times* front page three months earlier; escape route 80 miles across swamp from an earlier landing site; cabinet never asked "how far?". Missile crisis (Oct 1962): same people, new rules. Outcome: blockade and negotiated withdrawal; Kennedy later estimated one-third to one-half chance it could have gone nuclear. Process, not personnel, differs.

**Q3 (SA).** Give the team result numbers.
**A:** Teams +23% vs. individuals (year 1); a newly minted super on a superteam became 50% more accurate; ordinary teams beat the crowd by ~10%; prediction markets beat ordinary teams by ~20%; superteams beat markets by 15-30% (caveat: markets lacked liquidity and real money).

**Q4 (EXP).** Why does the wisdom of crowds fail in tulips and 2005 housing?
**A:** It needs independent errors that cancel; loudmouths, bullies, credentials, and premature consensus make errors pile up instead.

**Q5 (SA).** What is precision questioning? Give the example.
**A:** Targeted questions (Matthies & Worline): not "you're silly" but "what do you mean by 'pastime'?" and "what evidence, over what time frame?" Bay of Pigs would have died at "eighty miles of swamp and jungle?"

**Q6 (SA).** What is a premortem?
**A:** Assume the plan has failed and explain why, making doubts about the leader's plan safe to voice.

**Q7 (EXP).** What does "team AOM is emergent" mean?
**A:** A team's open-mindedness is a property of its communication patterns, not the sum of members'. Open-minded people who don't engage are less than the sum; opinionated people engaging in pursuit of truth are more.

**Q8 (SA).** Who are givers and what did they do?
**A:** Adam Grant's givers (vs. matchers, takers) deliberately model sharing and lift the team: Marty Rosenthal's "leading from behind," Doug Lorch's tools, Tim Minto's analyses.

**Q9 (EXP).** When does extremizing help and when does it not?
**A:** Only to the extent members hold information others lack. It barely helped information-sharing superteams and greatly helped regular teams. Scott Page: aggregation gains come from different perspectives; fine-tune the ability/diversity mix.

**Q10 (SCN).** A leader opens a strategy meeting by stating her preferred option; everyone agrees within minutes. Diagnose and redesign.
**A:** Leader announces preference first plus quick unanimity = groupthink symptoms. Redesign: collect independent judgments first, leader's view held to the end or absent, assign a devil's advocate, run a premortem, ask precision questions, invite push-back and thank critics.

**Q11 (SCN).** Your team is very polite; critique is rare ("dancing around"). Which concept and fix?
**A:** Weak team AOM and "dancing around." Fix with explicit invitations ("I want push-back"), thanks for criticism, psychological safety (Edmondson: the nurse tells the surgeon about the sponge), and watch for "my" vs. "our." Beware CEO disease and cognitive loafing in superteams.

## Chapter 10: The Leader's Dilemma

**Q1 (EXP).** What is the dilemma and how does mission command dissolve it?
**A:** Leaders must be confident and decisive; superforecasters humble and open to changing their minds. Mission command (Auftragstaktik): deliberate with doubt, then act with resolve; push decisions to those who see the ground; intellectual humility (about the problem) differs from self-doubt.

**Q2 (SA).** State Moltke's axioms.
**A:** "In war, everything is uncertain"; no plan extends with certainty beyond the first encounter with the enemy's main strength (the original of "no plan survives contact"); impossible to lay down binding rules. So train officers to think, discuss, criticize, and with good reason disobey (Seydlitz: "after the battle my head is at his disposal, but meanwhile I will make use of it").

**Q3 (EXP).** Describe the deliberation vs. implementation distinction.
**A:** Time-box deliberation; an imperfect decision in time beats a perfect one too late. Once decided, "half measures are out of place"; adhere until circumstances make a new decision unavoidable. The art is "the timely recognition of the moment when a new decision is required."

**Q4 (SA).** What does Auftragstaktik require of orders, and what are its endorsements?
**A:** Say *what* and *why*, never *how*. Decisions "can be shaped only on the spot." US doctrine in 1982; Patton: "Tell them what to do, and they will surprise you with their ingenuity." Business echoes: 3M's Coyne, Bezos's "have backbone; disagree and commit," Walmart's leadership academy.

**Q5 (EXP).** What do Eben Emael (1940) and Hitler's Normandy order illustrate?
**A:** Eben Emael: the commander's glider landed 100 km off; a sergeant took charge and knocked out the guns; another stray sergeant improvised a ground attack and took 121 prisoners (improvisation under mission command). Hitler: reserves at Normandy could move only on his personal order and no one would wake him (command-and-control failure).

**Q6 (EXP).** What lesson does Eisenhower's D-Day note teach?
**A:** He wrote the note taking responsibility for failure before the landing, then projected calm assurance. Morale comes from composure, not claims of certainty.

**Q7 (SA).** What are Annie Duke's "two humilities"?
**A:** Humility in the face of the game (complex, never solved, keep learning) is compatible with confidence in the face of opponents. Lincoln: "with firmness in the right, as God gives us to see the right."

**Q8 (EXP).** Why separate facts from values, and which examples are given?
**A:** To forecast well you must credit competence in what you despise (the Wehrmacht was evil and, man for man, highly effective). Moral and cognitive weakness don't go together. Lorch's wishful Aleppo forecast and Frankel's hope-tinted North Korea forecast show values leaking into probabilities.

**Q9 (SCN).** Rewrite a command-and-control order into a mission-command order for the same task, from the chapter's worked example.
**A:** C&C: "Approach from the southwest, skirt the factory, seize the canal bridge, occupy the town hall." Mission: "Take the town. Your purpose is to stop enemy reinforcements reaching the region; the town severs the key road." The captain who finds the bridge blown or a second intact bridge can act on the purpose and destroy it.

**Q10 (SCN).** A manager says "I can't admit uncertainty to my team or they'll lose confidence." What do you advise?
**A:** Confidence is not certainty. Deliberate with doubt, decide, execute with resolve, project composure (Eisenhower), and schedule a review of when a new decision is required. Keep separate ledgers: "I may be wrong about this problem" (always) vs. "I'm not up to this" (rarely). Petraeus: "the bold move is the right move except when it's the wrong move."

## Chapter 11: Are They Really So Super?

**Q1 (SA).** Summarize Kahneman's and Taleb's critiques.
**A:** Kahneman: System 2 vigilance is exhausting and illusions cannot be switched off. Taleb: only unforecastable black swans matter. The notes say both are partly right, but supers show resistance to scope insensitivity, black swans are often gray, and planning for surprise needs probabilities.

**Q2 (EXP).** How does General Flynn's claim illustrate WYSIATI?
**A:** His "pretty unprecedented" societal-conflict claim contradicted every dataset on declining battle deaths since the 1950s, but the reports on his desk felt like all there was. Like the Müller-Lyer illusion, knowing does not un-see; check with a ruler (the base rate).

**Q3 (SA).** Give the scope-insensitivity numbers.
**A:** People would pay ~$10 to clean lakes in one region or all 250,000 lakes; ~$80 to save 2,000, 20,000, or 200,000 birds. Assad's fall: regulars 40% at three months and 41% at six; supers 15% and 24% (no one saw both versions).

**Q4 (SA).** How can scope insensitivity be trained against?
**A:** The scope stress-test: mentally vary the cutoff date (3, 6, 12 months) and threshold (10% higher or lower) and see whether the probability changes sensibly. System 2 corrections can become System 1 habits like a golf swing; software tools help.

**Q5 (EXP).** Strict vs. modest black swans, and how do 9/11 and WWI fit?
**A:** Strictly inconceivable events are rare; 9/11 was war-gamed in 1998 and WWI feared for a decade, so these are gray. Modestly, highly improbable consequential events exist, and tournaments of months-long questions can't test skill at 0.1% events: "we don't know."

**Q6 (EXP).** How can a black swan be decomposed?
**A:** Its consequences unfold in forecastable steps (for 9/11: the ultimatum to the Taliban, their refusal, the invasion), each a scorable short-range question. Duncan Watts: the more you want to explain a black swan, the wider you must draw its boundary.

**Q7 (SA).** What is Eisenhower's line and what is the point about planning for surprise?
**A:** "Plans are useless, but planning is indispensable." Resilience is costly (Tokyo earthquake codes make sense, Kansas's would not; two-war doctrine rests on a probability judgment), so state the probability explicitly even if a guess.

**Q8 (EXP).** What is the Lin Wells memo?
**A:** April 2001: every decade since 1900 looked nothing like the previous decade's expectations, "so we should plan accordingly." Five months later, 9/11. Twenty-year Quadrennial Defense Reviews are ritual beyond the forecasting horizon.

**Q9 (SCN).** The 1914 Foreign Office official thinks war deaths are bell-curve distributed. Apply the fat-tail lens.
**A:** With a bell curve, a ten-million-death war looks like a one-in-millions freak. With a wealth-like distribution (billionaires at one in 700,000 rather than one in trillions), the same catastrophe is thousands of times more probable (like a one-in-500 lottery vs. one in five million). A rational official would buy more insurance. Procedure: decide whether the variable is height-like or wealth-like first.

**Q10 (SCN).** A risk officer says "nothing in our industry has ever been this chaotic" without data. What do you do?
**A:** Google the base rate (before calling something unprecedented). Treating a feeling of chaos as evidence of chaos is the anti-pattern. Also beware hindsight bias, which makes the past look predictable and the future more predictable than it is (Japan's 1980s "inevitable" dominance; China today). Adversarial collaboration (Kahneman-Klein, Kahneman-Mellers) is how disputes can be settled.

## Chapter 12: What's Next?

**Q1 (SA).** What does kto-kogo mean and how is it illustrated?
**A:** Lenin's "who, whom": argument and evidence as adornments on a power struggle. Examples: Nate Silver went from prophet to stumblebum among Democrats when his 2014 Senate forecast turned unwelcome; a Santander analyst was fired for an accurate but unwelcome forecast; Dick Morris defended his Romney-landslide call as duty, not belief.

**Q2 (EXP).** Why is change possible anyway? Use Codman.
**A:** Codman's End Result System (record ailment, treatment, outcome; publish) got him expelled from Mass General and Harvard, then was adopted by the profession. Evidence-based medicine, policy, philanthropy (Gates Foundation) and sports analytics show the shift to measurement. IARPA funding the tournament despite the wrong-side-of-maybe risk is the surprising proof.

**Q3 (EXP).** What is the wrong-side-of-maybe trap for institutions?
**A:** Numbers expose you when a 70% call fails, while fuzz protects you. Blame-game ping-pong (missed 9/11, over-alarm; WMD false alarm, under-alarm) prevents long-term investment in accuracy.

**Q4 (T/F).** Brier scoring is a finished, perfect tool.
**A:** False. It is a work in progress: it treats misses and false alarms equally, though asymmetric costs can be built in by telling forecasters the rules in advance. Imperfect scores still beat judging by titles, confidence, storytelling, book sales and Davos invitations, as imperfect credit scores beat whimsical loan officers.

**Q5 (SA).** What is Bayesian question clustering?
**A:** Decompose an unscorable big question ("How does this all turn out?") into many small pertinent scorable questions whose answers accumulate like pointillist dots.

**Q6 (SA).** What is the smack-the-forehead test and which question passes it?
**A:** A good question is one that, read later, makes you say "if only I had thought of that." Friedman's 2002 "Is Iraq the way it is because Saddam is, or vice versa?" passes.

**Q7 (EXP).** Superquestioners vs. superforecasters.
**A:** Probably different psychologies: hedgehog-like incisiveness generates questions, foxy eclecticism answers them. Read Friedman's vague petro-state column as a question, not a forecast. Aim for a Tom-Bill symbiosis, not a contest.

**Q8 (EXP).** Describe adversarial collaboration for policy debates with the Keynesians vs. Austerians case.
**A:** Four years passed and no one budged; the 2010 letter's signatories said in 2014 they'd change nothing. Instead identify disagreements, agree on precise forecast questions (how much inflation, by what benchmark, over what period), run many, and accept split decisions as information.

**Q9 (SCN).** Build a question cluster for "How does the Korea crisis turn out?" (2013).
**A:** (1) Multistage rocket launch between 7 Jan and 1 Sep 2013? (2) Another nuclear test in the window? (3) Rebuff six-party talks? (4) Fire artillery at the South? (5) Vessel fires on a South Korean vessel? Each has a date, a definition and a number; each yes nudges "ends badly" up. Scores show which forecasters and mental models deserve weight.

**Q10 (SCN).** A hospital boasts 100% survival. What does the chapter say?
**A:** Metric capture: it can hit 100% by refusing sick patients (the humanists' objection, Wieseltier, is partly right: numbers must be scrutinized). Improve scores; don't abandon them.

**Q11 (SCN).** An executive refuses to let her forecasts be scored. What does the chapter suggest?
**A:** Treat the refusal as information about incentives, not competence. Use "I was wrong" headlines as a measure of a field's health. Drezner's pledge: "make a clear prediction and offer a confidence interval... I want to keep score."

## Chapter 13 (Appendix): Ten Commandments for Aspiring Superforecasters

**Q1 (SA).** How many guidelines are in the appendix, and what is the last?
**A:** Eleven (despite the "Ten" title). The eleventh: don't treat commandments as commandments ("two cases will never be exactly the same," Moltke). They raised accuracy ~10% in training.

**Q2 (SA).** Commandment 1, Triage: what do you skip and why?
**A:** Skip clocklike questions (rules of thumb suffice) and cloudlike ones (even models can't beat the chimp); work the Goldilocks zone. Example: "Who wins in 2028?" is impossible; narrowing 2016 to plausible contenders is not.

**Q3 (SA).** Commandment 2 and the Peter Backus example.
**A:** Break intractable problems into tractable sub-problems (Fermi-ize). Backus's ~26 compatible women in London shows daring to be wrong with explicit guesses.

**Q4 (SA).** Commandment 3: how to balance views, and what is Summers's rule?
**A:** Nothing is 100% unique; find comparison classes even for Kony or Greece. Summers doubled estimates and moved up a time unit, then adjusted for exceptions.

**Q5 (SA).** Commandment 4: typical update and when to jump?
**A:** Belief updating is dental hygiene; hunt for nonobvious lead indicators ("what would have to happen before X?"); usually 0.4 to 0.35, but jump on truly diagnostic signals.

**Q6 (EXP).** Commandment 5, clashing causal forces.
**A:** Doves and hawks each list in advance the signs that would move them; synthesize into a dove-hawk hybrid. Synthesis is an art with no paint-by-numbers rules.

**Q7 (EXP).** Commandments 6 and 7: granularity and balancing confidence.
**A:** Distinguish as many degrees of doubt as the problem permits, but no more (60/40 vs. 40/60; "slam dunk" = infinite odds; Obama used granularity on March Madness but said "fifty-fifty" on bin Laden). Score on both calibration and resolution; tamp down both misses and false alarms rather than overcorrecting the latest one.

**Q8 (EXP).** Commandments 8-10.
**A:** 8: look for the errors behind mistakes, but beware hindsight; postmortem successes; don't over-learn from a minor slip. 9: bring out the best in others (perspective taking, precision questioning, constructive confrontation; Lasorda's dove: too tight kills, too loose loses). 10: master the error-balancing bicycle; you can't learn from a manual; deliberate practice with unambiguous feedback.

**Q9 (SCN).** "When will the migration project finish?" Apply the commandments.
**A:** Triage: forecastable. Fermi-ize: modules × hours ÷ available hours. Outside view: how late were the last five projects? If typically 2x over, anchor there before adjusting. Lead indicators (first-module date, defect rate), small updates. Clashing forces (new hires ramping vs. holiday season). State "70% by 30 June, 90% by 31 August." Don't waffle at 50%. Schedule the postmortem and record reasoning. Team attacks the estimate with precision questions. If this project is genuinely unlike the last five, adjust and don't apply the 2x rule blindly.

**Q10 (SCN).** Someone has memorized all the commandments but never makes a scored forecast. Verdict?
**A:** "Reading about forecasting instead of forecasting" is the anti-pattern; commandment 10 says practice with feedback. Each commandment is a dial between two errors; use the list as a pre-flight checklist, not a law code.

# Cumulative Final Exam (20 questions)

**F1 (SA).** What two truths define the optimistic skeptic?
**A:** Skeptic: nonlinear systems limit prediction, with accuracy near chance at 3-5 years. Optimist: much is predictable somewhat, and measured practice improves forecasting (Ch 1).

**F2 (SA).** List the four requirements of a scorable forecast and say which famous document failed all of them.
**A:** Time frame, defined terms, numeric probability, many instances. The Bernanke letter ("risk," no date) failed; "serious possibility" in NIE 29-51 shows the word problem (Ch 3, 12).

**F3 (T/F).** Superforecasters are in the top 1% of intelligence.
**A:** False. Regulars were above ~70% of the population, supers above ~80%. The threshold effect: method and mindset matter more (Ch 5, 8).

**F4 (MC).** Which best captures the correct reading of a 70% forecast when the event doesn't occur? (a) The forecaster was wrong (b) The forecaster was right (c) One case is insufficient; judge calibration across many forecasts (d) Use the Forer effect
**A:** (c). Wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy (Ch 3, 12).

**F5 (SA).** Give three Brier benchmarks.
**A:** 0 perfect; 0.5 random; 2.0 maximally wrong. Examples from notes: regulars 0.37, first-cohort supers 0.25, Lorch 0.22 then 0.14, Minto 0.07 on one question (Ch 3, 4, 7).

**F6 (EXP).** Explain how the Iraq WMD estimate links Ch 4 and Ch 6.
**A:** It was reasonable and wrong; the sin was certainty ("slam dunk"). Ch 6's Maya says "100%" was right and unreasonable. Both teach: judge reasoning, not outcome, and avoid the three-setting dial (Ch 4, 6).

**F7 (SA).** Outline the eight-step forecasting sequence.
**A:** Triage; Fermi-ize; outside view; inside view; synthesize (others, markets, crowd within, flipped wording); granular number; update; postmortem (Ch 5, 7, 8, 13).

**F8 (SA).** How are tip-of-your-nose, WYSIATI, and scope insensitivity related?
**A:** The subjective view (Ch 2) that cannot be switched off runs on WYSIATI, which causes scope insensitivity (Assad 40%/41% for regulars vs. 15%/24% for supers) (Ch 2, 11).

**F9 (SCN).** A team's forecast is 70% with individual estimates between 55% and 85%, and members hold distinct private information. What do you do and why?
**A:** Aggregate (preferably weighting by track record) and extremize toward ~80-85% because distinct info means the aggregate underestimates what a fully informed crowd would believe. If members share all information, extremizing helps little (Ch 4, 6, 9).

**F10 (EXP).** Why is the 0.65 year-to-year correlation important, and why did supers still lose ~30% yearly?
**A:** Fast regression = luck, slow = skill; 0.65 shows mostly skill. Yet skill plus luck guarantees some turnover; no one is infallible (Ch 4).

**F11 (SA).** Contrast over- and underreaction with a number from each.
**A:** Underreaction: DeWitt's sabotage reasoning, Jenga tower. Overreaction: dilution effect; high-turnover households earned 11.4% vs. 17.9%. Middle path: Minto's 34 updates of avg 3.5 points (Ch 7).

**F12 (EXP).** Why do vague words block learning?
**A:** Ambiguous forecasts stretch to fit outcomes (Forer effect), and hindsight bias rewrites memory (31 points). Feedback needs unambiguous forecasts and recorded reasoning (Ch 3, 8).

**F13 (SCN).** Your organization's leader says "I have no idea, it's fifty-fifty" about a decision when advisers estimate 30-90%. Diagnose.
**A:** Retreat to the ignorance prior that discards information (Obama's bin Laden coin flip). Aggregate, extremize, then state the decision threshold separately (Ch 6).

**F14 (EXP).** Connect groupthink (Ch 9) and the WMD estimate (Ch 4).
**A:** Both feature unanimity taken as proof: no red team or dissent paper on WMDs; Bay of Pigs cabinet "no one objected." Remedies: devil's advocates, premortems, independent judgments, outsiders (Ch 4, 9).

**F15 (EXP).** How does mission command relate to superteams?
**A:** Both decouple authority from announcing answers: Kennedy withheld his view and imported dissent; mission command gives goal and intent, not method. Both deliberate with doubt, then act with resolve (Ch 9, 10).

**F16 (SCN).** An analyst dismisses all forecasting because "black swans" exist. Respond.
**A:** Most are gray (9/11 war-gamed in 1998), their consequences unfold in forecastable steps, and planning for surprise needs probabilities. But tournaments can't test 0.1% events and fat tails should be assumed (Ch 11).

**F17 (SA).** What numbers describe the team and market results?
**A:** Teams +23%; supers on superteam +50%; ordinary teams beat the crowd ~10%; markets beat ordinary teams ~20%; superteams beat markets 15-30% (Ch 9).

**F18 (EXP).** Synthesis: why is the book optimistic about institutions despite kto-kogo?
**A:** Codman's End Result System was eventually adopted; evidence-based medicine, Gates Foundation evaluations, sports analytics, and IARPA funding the tournament. Tools: scoring, question clustering, adversarial collaboration (Ch 12).

**F19 (SCN).** Plan a one-year personal training regimen using the book.
**A:** Learn the Ten Commandments (the hour tutorial gave ~10%); make dated, defined, numeric forecasts; log reasoning at forecast time; update in small steps; postmortem wins and losses; join a team that welcomes push-back; seek feedback in your domain (calibration is domain-specific). Perpetual beta predicts ~3x as strongly as intelligence (Ch 1, 8, 9, 13).

**F20 (EXP).** In your own words, what is the single most important idea of the book and which three chapters best support it?
**A:** Forecasting skill is measurable and learnable, produced by scored practice, probabilistic thinking and open-minded updating rather than by credentials or confidence. Best supported by Ch 3 (scoring), Ch 4 (tournament evidence), Ch 8 (perpetual beta). Any well-reasoned alternative answer with chapter support is acceptable.
