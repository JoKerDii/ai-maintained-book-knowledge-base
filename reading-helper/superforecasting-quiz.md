# Superforecasting Quiz Book
*Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction* by Philip E. Tetlock & Dan Gardner

**How to use:** Cover the answers, answer from memory (out loud or in writing), then check. Anything you miss is a section to reread. Each chapter has short-answer (SA), true/false (T/F), and multiple-choice (MC) questions. A cumulative final exam is at the end.

---

## Chapter 1: An Optimistic Skeptic

**Q1 (SA).** What does it mean to be an "optimistic skeptic" about forecasting?
**A:** Hold two truths at once. *Skeptic:* nonlinear systems (Lorenz's butterfly, the Arab Spring sparked by one street vendor) impose hard limits, and accuracy falls toward chance 3–5 years out. *Optimist:* much of reality is predictable to some degree under some circumstances, and forecasters can improve if they measure their results.

**Q2 (MC).** Clocks vs. clouds is best understood as:
a) two kinds of people  b) a strict dichotomy of systems  c) a spectrum where predictability depends on what you predict, how far out, and under what circumstances  d) a way to rank forecasters
**A:** c. Weather, for example, is reliable at 1–2 days and chimp-level around 8 days.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the loop that makes weather forecasters improve, and why do most other domains never run it?
**A:** Forecast, measure, revise. Most domains skip it because consumers don't demand evidence of accuracy, and accuracy is rarely the sole goal (see Q4).

**Q4 (SA).** Name some goals forecasts serve other than accuracy.
**A:** To entertain (TV pundits), to galvanize action (activist warnings), to impress (bank outlooks on 2050), and to comfort (partisan reassurance). Because accuracy isn't the only goal, nobody insists on measuring it.

**Q5 (SA).** What was the Good Judgment Project and what did it find?
**A:** A research team in the IARPA forecasting tournament (2011–15) with 20,000+ volunteers, ~500 questions mostly 1–12 months out, and over a million judgments. It beat the control group by about 60% in year 1 and 78% in year 2. A small group of "superforecasters" did far better than ordinary forecasters.

**Q6 (T/F).** The "dart-throwing chimp" finding shows that all experts are useless. **A:** False. It described the average expert on multi-year questions. On shorter horizons some people show real, testable foresight, and what makes them good is what they do, not who they are.

## Chapter 2: Illusions of Knowledge

**Q1 (SA).** Why did medicine stagnate for so long, and what finally changed it?
**A:** Doctors trusted the view from the tip of their nose and their own confidence. Progress began when they accepted doubt and ran controlled trials (Archie Cochrane's push for randomized trials). The book argues forecasting today resembles pre-scientific medicine.

**Q2 (SA).** What are System 1 and System 2, and what is WYSIATI?
**A:** System 1 is fast, automatic, intuitive judgment. System 2 is slow, deliberate checking. WYSIATI ("What You See Is All There Is") means System 1 builds a coherent story from whatever evidence is at hand and ignores what it doesn't have.

**Q3 (SA).** Explain "bait and switch" (attribute substitution) with an example.
**A:** Faced with a hard question, we silently answer an easier one. "Should I fear that shadow?" becomes "Can I recall a lion attack?" Cochrane's doctor swapped "Do I have cancer?" for "Is this the sort of man who would know?"

**Q4 (SA).** What is the "scientist's test"?
**A:** Ask, "What would convince me I am wrong?" If you can't answer, you are too attached to your belief.

**Q5 (MC).** According to the Kahneman–Klein reconciliation, intuition is reliable when:
a) the person is an expert  b) the person is confident  c) the environment offers valid cues and enough practice to learn them  d) the decision is fast
**A:** c. It works for firefighters and chess players. It fails for stock picking, where valid cues are absent.

**Q6 (T/F).** Knowing about an illusion switches it off. **A:** False. Like the Müller-Lyer lines, you can only check it with a ruler. Illusions can be monitored by System 2, not removed.

## Chapter 3: Keeping Score

**Q1 (SA).** What four things make a forecast scorable?
**A:** (1) A clear time frame, (2) explicitly defined terms, (3) a numeric probability, (4) many forecasts so calibration can be computed.

**Q2 (SA).** What is the wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy?
**A:** Judging a probabilistic forecast as "wrong" because the less likely outcome occurred, e.g., calling a 70% forecast wrong when the 30% happens. It pushes forecasters toward vague words that can be stretched after the fact.

**Q3 (SA).** What problem did Sherman Kent's scale address?
**A:** Words like "serious possibility" meant 80/20 to one analyst and 20/80 to another. Kent mapped words to numeric ranges (e.g., probable ≈ 75% ± 12, almost certain ≈ 93% ± 6). "I'd rather be a bookie than a goddamn poet."

**Q4 (SA).** Define calibration and resolution. Why do you need both?
**A:** Calibration: when you say 70%, it happens 70% of the time. Resolution: assigning high probabilities to what happens and low to what doesn't, rather than hovering at 40–60%. Calibration alone can be achieved cowardly; you need to be well calibrated *and* brave.

**Q5 (MC).** On the Brier score used in the book, a perfect score is ___ and always saying 50% scores ___.
a) 1.0 / 0.5  b) 0 / 0.5  c) 0 / 2.0  d) 0.5 / 1.0
**A:** b. 2.0 is maximally wrong (100% confident every time, always on the wrong side). Lower is better.

**Q6 (SA).** Why is a raw Brier score hard to interpret alone?
**A:** It depends on question difficulty. A 0.2 may be excellent on hard questions and poor on easy ones, so you need benchmarks and comparison on level playing fields.

**Q7 (SA).** What did Tetlock's earlier expert-judgment research find about foxes and hedgehogs?
**A:** Hedgehogs squeeze everything through one Big Idea and are confident and declarative. Foxes use many tools, think in probabilities, and revise. Foxes forecast better and the famous hedgehogs worse. Aggregating many perspectives (the dragonfly eye) is the mechanism.

## Chapter 4: Superforecasters

**Q1 (SA).** What is the difference between a reasonable and a correct judgment? Apply it to the Iraq WMD estimate.
**A:** Reasonable means justified by what was knowable at the time; correct means the outcome matched. The intelligence community's WMD conclusion was reasonable and wrong. The sin was the "slam dunk" certainty. A 60–70% estimate might have changed the outcome.

**Q2 (SA).** Why is checklist-style accountability insufficient?
**A:** It's like checking hand-washing without checking whether patients get better. Only systematic tracking of accuracy is meaningful accountability.

**Q3 (SA).** How did the GJP build its winning aggregate?
**A:** It identified the top ~2% by accuracy and update frequency, weighted their forecasts more, then *extremized* the aggregate (e.g., push 70% toward 85%) to simulate what the crowd would believe if everyone shared all information.

**Q4 (SA).** How do we know superforecasters aren't just lucky?
**A:** The regression-to-the-mean test. Fast regression means luck, slow regression means skill. Their year-to-year correlation is ≈ 0.65, with about 70% staying in the top group each year, which indicates skill.

**Q5 (T/F).** Superforecasters beat intelligence analysts with classified information. **A:** True, by roughly 30%, and they also beat regular forecasters by roughly 60% and prediction markets.

**Q6 (MC).** Which is the *most* accurate description of the superforecasters' edge?
a) superior IQ  b) insider information  c) habits and methods that can be learned  d) luck
**A:** c.

## Chapter 5: Supersmart?

**Q1 (SA).** Are superforecasters geniuses? What does intelligence contribute?
**A:** No. They're smart (above ~80% of the population versus ~70% for regular forecasters) but not off the charts. Intelligence and knowledge help only above a modest threshold; method and disposition do the rest.

**Q2 (SA).** What is Fermi-izing and why does it help?
**A:** Breaking an unanswerable question into sub-questions, separating the knowable from the unknowable, and making explicit guesses. It exposes assumptions and errors instead of hiding them in a black box ("it dares us to be wrong").

**Q3 (SA).** Why start with the outside view, and what is anchoring's role?
**A:** The base rate for the comparison class is the best anchor. We under-adjust from whatever starting point we have, so a meaningful anchor beats a meaningless one. Start outside (how often does this kind of thing happen?), then adjust for the case's specifics.

**Q4 (SA).** How do you refine a reference class? Give the book's example.
**A:** Narrow the class to better match the case. US households owning pets: 62%; single-family detached homes: ~73%.

**Q5 (SA).** What does it mean to treat the inside view as investigation rather than an amble?
**A:** List the hypotheses (each pathway to "yes"), ask "what would have to be true?" for each, and research the components like a detective.

**Q6 (SA).** What is "the crowd within"?
**A:** Assume your first answer is wrong and make a second estimate from a different angle. Averaging the two beats either alone.

**Q7 (SA).** What is active open-mindedness?
**A:** Treating beliefs as hypotheses to be tested, not treasures to guard; deliberately seeking views that clash with yours and synthesizing them.

## Chapter 6: Superquants?

**Q1 (SA).** What is the "three-setting dial" and why does it fail forecasters?
**A:** Gonna happen / not gonna happen / maybe. It's evolutionarily sensible (lion: run, stay alert, relax) but yes and no express certainty, and almost nothing is certain. The honest setting is *maybe*, subdivided finely.

**Q2 (SA).** What does a frequent "50%" usually signal?
**A:** "I don't know." People who use 50% often are measurably less accurate.

**Q3 (MC).** Epistemic uncertainty is:
a) unknowable even in principle  b) knowable in principle but unknown  c) pure randomness  d) the same as aleatory
**A:** b. Aleatory uncertainty is unknowable (e.g., rain in Philadelphia a year out). On cloudlike questions supers stay within roughly 35–65% and move out tentatively.

**Q4 (SA).** What does granularity have to do with accuracy?
**A:** Forecasters who distinguish more degrees of doubt (ones place) are more accurate than those using fives or tens. Rounding supers' forecasts to coarser values hurt their scores. Finer numbers carry real information.

**Q5 (SA).** What did the bin Laden compound debate illustrate?
**A:** Panetta received honest, diverse estimates (about 30% to 90%+) and treated them as the wisdom of the crowd. Obama's "coin flip" may have been triage but also discarded information. Right-but-unreasonable (Maya's "100%") versus wrong-but-reasonable is judged by the reasoning.

**Q6 (SA).** What's the difference between thinking in probability versus in fate?
**A:** Probabilistic thinkers ask "how" something happened and accept randomness. Fate-thinkers ask "why" and see events as meant to be; fate-thinking correlates with worse Brier scores.

## Chapter 7: Supernewsjunkies?

**Q1 (SA).** Are superforecasters better only because they update more?
**A:** No. Their initial forecasts are already much more accurate (about 50% in the book's comparison). Updating then adds to it.

**Q2 (SA).** What is underreaction and what drives it?
**A:** Belief perseverance: failing to update enough. Commitment freezes belief, especially when a belief is deep in your "Jenga tower" of identity, expertise, and public statements. General DeWitt (1942) took the absence of sabotage as confirmation that sabotage was coming.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the dilution effect and what is its cost?
**A:** Irrelevant details sway judgment (tennis versus skiing, with no bearing on the question). It leads to overreaction to noise: highest-turnover households earned 11.4% versus the market's 17.9%.

**Q4 (SA).** Describe Tim Minto's updating pattern.
**A:** On a Syrian-refugee question he updated 34 times with an average move of only 3.5 points, ending with a Brier of 0.07. Frequent and small captures the value of new information without lurching.

**Q5 (T/F).** Good updating means always moving in small steps. **A:** False. Move in small steps by default, but jump hard when the basis of the forecast has collapsed. Update in proportion to how diagnostic the evidence is (likelihood ratio).

**Q6 (SA).** What is the role of Bayes' theorem?
**A:** A model for updating: the posterior depends on prior belief and how much more likely the evidence is if the hypothesis is true than if false. Supers rarely compute it but follow its logic.

## Chapter 8: Perpetual Beta

**Q1 (SA).** What is the strongest predictor of becoming a superforecaster?
**A:** Commitment to belief updating and self-improvement, "perpetual beta": growth mindset plus grit, about 3× as predictive as intelligence.

**Q2 (SA).** Contrast fixed and growth mindsets.
**A:** Fixed: ability is a trait; the person engages with "right/wrong" but not information that could improve the answer. Growth: ability is built by effort; failure is data. (Mary Simpson joined GJP after missing 2008: "I should be better at this.")

**Q3 (SA).** What is the cycle behind learning? Give the Keynes example.
**A:** Try, fail, analyze, adjust, try again. Keynes lost nearly everything in 1920 and 1929, scrutinized his assumptions, and developed something close to value investing.

**Q4 (SA).** Why do most forecasters fail to get useful feedback?
**A:** (1) Ambiguous language lets a vague forecast stretch to fit any result (the Forer effect: astrology boilerplate rated 4.2/5 as a personal profile). (2) Time lag plus hindsight bias: experts misremember their past forecasts (about 31 points too high on the Soviet collapse).

**Q5 (MC).** Why are weather forecasters and bridge players well calibrated?
a) higher IQ  b) better training in statistics  c) prompt, unambiguous feedback  d) bigger datasets
**A:** c. Calibration is domain-specific and follows feedback quality.

**Q6 (SA).** What did Polanyi's bicycle illustrate, and what training effect did GJP find?
**A:** Tacit knowledge needs practice; no manual substitutes. The training booklet gave roughly a 10% improvement and worked best combined with practice.

## Chapter 9: Superteams

**Q1 (SA).** What is groupthink (Janis)?
**A:** Cohesive groups maintain esprit de corps by developing shared illusions that interfere with critical thinking; agreement is taken as proof of correctness. Bay of Pigs is the example.

**Q2 (SA).** What did Kennedy change before the Cuban missile crisis?
**A:** Members were licensed as generalists to question anything; devil's advocates/watchdogs were named; hierarchy and protocol were suspended; outsiders were invited; Kennedy sometimes left the room and withheld his preference so alternatives got a hearing.

**Q3 (SA).** Why does the wisdom of crowds require independence?
**A:** Errors must be independent so they cancel. Loud voices, credentials, and premature consensus make errors correlate and pile up.

**Q4 (SA).** What is precision questioning? Give an example.
**A:** Respectful, specific probing instead of "you're silly": "What do you mean by 'pastime'?" or "What evidence, over what time frame?" At the Bay of Pigs, it would have stopped at "eighty miles of swamp and jungle?"

**Q5 (SA).** What is a premortem?
**A:** Imagine the plan has already failed and explain why. It makes doubts about the leader's plan safe to voice.

**Q6 (SA).** What were the team results?
**A:** Teams beat individuals by about 23%; superteams beat prediction markets by 15–30%.

**Q7 (SA).** What is constructive confrontation (Grove) and what role do "givers" play?
**A:** Disagree without being disagreeable. Givers share information and help others, lifting the whole team. Team open-mindedness is emergent from communication patterns, not the sum of individual traits.

## Chapter 10: The Leader's Dilemma

**Q1 (SA).** What is the dilemma?
**A:** Leaders need confidence and decisiveness; good forecasters need humility and openness to changing their minds.

**Q2 (SA).** How does mission command (Auftragstaktik) resolve it?
**A:** Deliberate with doubt, decide in time, then execute with resolve. Tell subordinates what to accomplish and why (goal and intent), never how, because decisions "can be shaped only on the spot." Adopted into US doctrine in 1982. Patton: "Tell them what to do, and they will surprise you with their ingenuity."

**Q3 (SA).** What did Moltke contribute?
**A:** "No plan survives contact with the enemy's main strength" (the original). Since you can't lay down binding rules, train officers to think, discuss, criticize, and with good reason, disobey.

**Q4 (SA).** Distinguish deliberation from implementation.
**A:** Time-box deliberation; an imperfect decision in time beats a perfect one too late. Once decided, commit ("half measures are out of place") until circumstances demand a new decision. The art is recognizing that moment.

**Q5 (SA).** Distinguish confidence from false certainty. Use Eisenhower.
**A:** Eisenhower drafted a note taking responsibility for D-Day failure before the landing, then projected calm assurance. Morale comes from composure, not claims of certainty.

**Q6 (T/F).** Intellectual humility means self-doubt. **A:** False. It is humility about the problem and compatible with confidence in yourself. Also separate facts from values, and assume competence in adversaries you despise.

## Chapter 11: Are They Really So Super?

**Q1 (SA).** What is Kahneman's critique?
**A:** System 2 vigilance is exhausting and the illusions can't be switched off, so even supers will eventually fall into them.

**Q2 (SA).** What is scope insensitivity? Give the forecasting evidence.
**A:** Answering "how bad does it feel?" instead of the question, ignoring scale (paying about the same to save 2,000 or 200,000 birds). Regular forecasters gave Assad's fall 40% at three months and 41% at six; supers gave 15% and 24% — they partly resisted. Training tip: mentally vary the cutoff date and thresholds.

**Q3 (SA).** What is Taleb's critique, and where is it right?
**A:** Only unforecastable black swans matter. He's right that tournaments of months-long questions can't test skill at ~0.1% events, and that fat tails exist in conflict and markets.

**Q4 (SA).** Distinguish strict from modest black swans.
**A:** Strictly inconceivable events are rare (9/11 was war-gamed in 1998; WWI was feared for a decade — "gray swans"). Modestly, highly improbable consequential events exist. Consequences of even true surprises unfold through forecastable steps.

**Q5 (SA).** Can you plan for surprise without forecasting?
**A:** No. Resilience investment is itself a probability judgment, so make it explicit.

**Q6 (SA).** How can System 2 corrections become habits?
**A:** Practice makes outside-view reflexes automatic, like a golf swing; software tools can help.

## Chapter 12: What's Next?

**Q1 (SA).** What is kto-kogo?
**A:** Lenin's "who, whom": argument and evidence are adornments on a power struggle. Forecasts often serve tribe and career. Examples: Nate Silver after his 2014 Senate forecast; an analyst fired for an accurate but unwelcome call.

**Q2 (SA).** Why does the book remain optimistic about change?
**A:** Evidence-based medicine happened despite resistance (Codman's End Result System, which got him expelled, was later adopted). Evidence-based policy, philanthropy, and sports analytics followed, and IARPA funded the tournament despite the wrong-side-of-maybe risk.

**Q3 (SA).** Why do institutions fear numbers?
**A:** Numbers expose a failed 70% call while fuzzy language protects you. Blame-game ping-pong (miss 9/11 → over-alarm; WMD false alarm → under-alarm) blocks long-term investment in accuracy.

**Q4 (SA).** How can big, unscorable questions be addressed?
**A:** Cluster many small, scorable questions that bear on the big one (Bayesian question clusters), and pair superquestioners (people who ask the right questions) with superforecasters.

**Q5 (SA).** What is adversarial collaboration?
**A:** Rivals agree in advance on questions, evidence, and bets that would change their minds, then test them together to depolarize debates.

**Q6 (T/F).** Brier scoring is a finished, perfect tool. **A:** False. It's a work in progress (it treats misses and false alarms equally); asymmetric costs can be built in if forecasters are told the rules. Numbers are tools, not totems; imperfect scores still beat none.

## Chapter 13 (Appendix): Ten Commandments

**Q1 (SA).** What was the evidence behind these guidelines?
**A:** They come from the book and from training experimentally shown to raise accuracy by about 10%.

**Q2 (SA).** Name the guidelines in order.
**A:** (1) Triage; (2) break problems into tractable sub-problems (Fermi-ize); (3) balance inside and outside views; (4) balance under- and overreaction to evidence; (5) look for clashing causal forces; (6) distinguish as many degrees of doubt as the problem permits, but no more; (7) balance under- and overconfidence, prudence and decisiveness; (8) look for the errors behind your mistakes without hindsight bias; (9) bring out the best in others and let others bring out the best in you; (10) master the error-balancing bicycle; (11) don't treat commandments as commandments.

**Q3 (SA).** What is triage in this context?
**A:** Work questions in the Goldilocks zone; skip clocklike questions (rules of thumb suffice) and cloudlike ones (even models can't beat the chimp).

**Q4 (SA).** What does "look for clashing causal forces" mean in practice?
**A:** Doves and hawks each list in advance the signs that would move them, then synthesize a hybrid. Synthesis is an art with no paint-by-numbers rules.

**Q5 (SA).** What is the theme running through all the guidelines?
**A:** Each balances opposing errors (inside/outside, under/overreaction, under/overconfidence). Skill comes from practicing the balance, not following a rule. Hence the last guideline.

## Cumulative Final Exam

1. **Write a scorable version of "Will the economy get worse?"**
   *A:* Add a time frame, a defined measure, and a probability, e.g., "Will US real GDP shrink for two consecutive quarters before 31 Dec 2027, per BEA data? 18%."
2. **A friend says you were wrong because a 75% forecast failed. Respond.** *A:* Wrong-side-of-maybe fallacy; judge calibration over many forecasts.
3. **List the book's eight-step forecasting method.** *A:* Triage; Fermi-ize; outside view; inside view; synthesize (others, markets, crowd within); granular number; update; postmortem.
4. **Why record your reasoning at forecast time?** *A:* Hindsight bias rewrites memory, and feedback is only useful if the original forecast was clear.
5. **Why can't a team just average members' forecasts after a discussion?** *A:* Discussion can destroy independence and create groupthink; protect independent judgment first, then aggregate, and extremize based on private information.
6. **Which uncertainty can a forecaster reduce, and which can't they?** *A:* Epistemic by research; aleatory not, so stay in the 35–65% band.
7. **Explain the leader's dilemma and its resolution.** *A:* See ch10: deliberate with doubt, execute with resolve, mission command.
8. **What are the book's main limits?** *A:* Accuracy fades beyond 3–5 years; fat tails and rare events can't be tested; WYSIATI and scope insensitivity persist; and forecasts often serve power (kto-kogo).
9. **Explain to a colleague, in three sentences, why superforecasters beat experts.** *A (model):* They use a repeatable method (decompose, base rate first, then specifics), give precise probabilities, and update in small steps. They treat beliefs as hypotheses and learn from scored feedback. They also aggregate diverse views, which cancels individual bias.
10. **Applied task:** Pick a real question, write your forecast with the method, set a date to score it, and record your reasoning.
