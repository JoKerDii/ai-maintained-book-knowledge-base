# Arguments and Evidence: *This Is How They Tell Me the World Ends* (Nicole Perlroth)

> **Source note:** This file reflects the skill's chapter notes (SKILL.md, glossary, patterns, cheatsheet and the 25 chapter files covering Prologue + Ch 1-24), **not the book's own wording**. Nothing here is added from outside those notes; where the notes are silent, this file is silent. Where the notes disagree with themselves, the conflict is flagged. Reading-time figures in Part 1 are a planning estimate for you, not facts from the notes.

**One-paragraph thesis.** The United States spawned and still bankrolls the global market for zero-day exploits, built the world's most powerful cyber arsenal, and then discovered it could neither keep those weapons secret nor defend its own home front, because everyone runs the same technology. Offense-over-defense hoarding boomeranged (EternalBlue to WannaCry, NotPetya, attacks on US towns, hospitals, the grid and nuclear plants). The danger is not one "Cyber Pearl Harbor" but a decade-long normalized plague: "death by a thousand hacks." Endgame: "It wasn't Ukraine. It was us."

# PART 2 - Chapter by chapter

## Prologue: Kyiv, Ukraine

**Central argument.** Ukraine is the world's cyberwar test kitchen: five years of Russian attacks ending in the 2015 and 2016 grid blackouts and NotPetya (June 27, 2017), the most destructive cyberattack in history, built on stolen NSA tools. The country with the greatest offensive cyber advantage is also the most vulnerable. The end game "wasn't Ukraine. It was us."

**Full stories.**
*Rehearsal in Ukraine.* Russia used Ukraine to rehearse every tool: 2014 election malware that would have declared a fringe candidate the winner, disinformation, media trapdoors, then the grid. Ukrainian forensic experts told Perlroth these attacks were "a cruel version of the scientific method": test a capability, demonstrate it to overlords, move on. When attacks on Ukraine slow, "that means they've moved on." Putin's rules for hackers were simple: no hacking inside Russia, do the Kremlin's favors when asked, and otherwise full autonomy, "patriotic" hackers as artists. The grid attack was routed through Singapore, the Netherlands and Romania and delivered in benign-looking pieces with randomized code, yet sophistication and absence of profit motive pointed to a state. Political hit jobs don't have business models.

*NotPetya.* On Ukraine's Constitution Day 2017 (an anniversary message), a family-run tax-software company's update mechanism, the "Patient Zero," was hijacked to deliver malware to every major Ukrainian agency and company. It mimicked Petya ransomware, but no payment could recover the data; it was a state weapon in a criminal's mask. At Chernobyl, 2,500 computers went dark in seven minutes, radiation monitors went offline, and staff were sent out to measure by hand: "Life before NotPetya and Life after NotPetya." The malware used the NSA's exploit, leaked by the Shadow Brokers, as propulsion, then hit Merck, Pfizer, Maersk, FedEx, a Cadbury factory in Tasmania, and Russia's own Rosneft and Evraz. "Snowden leaked the PowerPoint; the Shadow Brokers handed out the bullets."

*What Ukraine had that the US lacks.* Ukraine survived because it was not fully automated: no Internet of Things, paper ballots counted by hand. Every Ukrainian Perlroth met called migrating elections to computers "pure insanity." The US plugs in 127 devices a second and has built "the world's largest attack surface."

*Pulled punches.* Both the grid attacks (under six hours) and NotPetya stopped short of lethal outcomes (nuclear stations kept running): Russia demonstrating reach rather than exhausting it.

*The US threat ranking and 2016.* Pre-2016, the US ranked China as prolific (the "greatest transfer of wealth in history"), Iran and North Korea as willing (Sands casino, Sony, the Bangladesh bank heist), and Russia as the most sophisticated (Estonia, French TV posing as jihadists, the Saudi petrochemical safety-system attack, Brexit, 2016, the Olympics), with the US assuming a comfortable lead. In 2016 Russia probed back-end systems and voter data in all fifty states as a trial run, while Trump blamed "a four-hundred-pound hacker" and welcomed Putin's help in Helsinki. Disinformation follow-ons included anti-vaccine trolling aimed at Ukrainian mothers during a measles outbreak and later Covid conspiracies.

**Evidence.**
- NotPetya cost: over $10 billion and climbing by 2019; Merck and FedEx about $1 billion alone; 80% of Ukraine's computers wiped.
- Chernobyl: 2,500 computers dark in seven minutes.
- At the NSA, offense outnumbers defense roughly 100 to 1 (the notes do not say what is counted; Ch 10 gives a 2:1 budget ratio).
- Caveat: Merck-and-FedEx wording here differs from the cheatsheet (Merck + Mondelez over $1B); both agree the largest victims lost on the order of $1B each.

**Key terms.** Test-kitchen pattern; attribution by resources not traces; boomerang; ransomware as disguise; vulnerability paradox; Patient Zero; Cyber Pearl Harbor (the warning Perlroth heard for seven years).

**So what.** Treat a neighbor's cyberattacks as rehearsal footage for yours; use profit motive as the first attribution filter; treat automation and convenience as attack surface. Don't assume your offensive lead is permanent while your own weapons leak.

## Chapter 1: Closet of Secrets

**Central argument.** Working the Snowden documents in 2013 in a windowless Times storage closet, Perlroth learned the NSA was not cracking encryption so much as hacking around it, and that its capabilities rested on a vast catalog of zero-days, many sourced from a gray market the documents only hinted at.

**Full stories.**
*The closet.* British agents had forced the destruction of the Guardian's Snowden drives; partnering with the Times provided First Amendment cover. The rules: no fishing, no phones, no internet, no windows (fear of laser interception). Glenn Greenwald held a grudge because the Times in 2004 had held the warrantless-wiretapping story for a year; Snowden bypassed the paper and Greenwald withheld two key encryption memos for weeks. The notes call the Times "an insurance policy, not a partner."

*What the documents showed.* The NSA's algorithms-breaking was not the story ("held up quite nicely"). Instead: allies lobbied standards bodies to adopt a flawed random-number formula; the NSA paid RSA to make it the default; the CIA planted backdoors on chip factory floors; and the NSA hacked Google and Yahoo servers before data was encrypted. References to "commercial partners" and "security partners" implied a legal gray market of brokers and contractors; tools' provenance was either beyond a contractor's access or too controversial to write down. Tech companies said they complied with legal requests but denied granting backdoors, later complicated by Yahoo's over-compliance.

*Perlroth's path to the beat.* Hired in 2010 with no cybersecurity background because qualified candidates couldn't be understood; three years of Chinese espionage (the Times' own "summer intern" hacker on a 10-to-5 Beijing schedule), Iran's burning-flag attack on Saudi Aramco, Russian probes of utilities; growing paranoia about "anything with a plug." A $50 hotel-lock exploit already used by thieves and the front desk's reassurance made it personal. The Mariner 1 anecdote (a missing hyphen destroyed a $150M spacecraft) illustrates how small flaws wreck big systems.

**Evidence.** Stuxnet used seven Windows and Siemens zero-days; one Microsoft zero-day was used to steal source code. The notes do not give prices here.

**Key terms.** Zero-day ("God mode," a spare key); TAO; SIGINT Enabling Project; Mariner 1; "the most secretive, highly classified, and invisible market on earth."

**So what.** Ask "who else has this, and where did it come from?" rather than only "is the NSA spying?" Shared technology means a backdoor into an adversary is a backdoor into you. Don't fixate on the visible program (metadata) and miss the structural one (backdoors).

## Chapter 2: The Fucking Salmon

**Central argument.** The zero-day trade's operating norm is silence. A refusal to say whom you sell to is itself the answer, and offensive tools are dual-use: the builder's own infrastructure is the richest future target.

**Full stories.**
*The Miami dinner (2013).* At an invite-only industrial-control conference (pipelines, grids, nuclear plants), organized by a former NSA cryptographer, two Malta-based Italian hunters of industrial zero-days, Luigi Auriemma and Donato Ferrante, were asked the one question that matters: who do you sell to and whom won't you sell to? They deflected ("talk about my salmon"). Their rationalizations: vendors wrote the bugs; governments need exploits as encryption spreads ("going dark"). The omission: tools flow to repressive regimes and industrial systems, and eventually cause physical harm.

*Langner.* Ralph Langner, the first to attribute Stuxnet publicly to the US and Israel, condemned them as mercenaries. His mirror argument: Stuxnet used seven zero-days to reach air-gapped centrifuge controllers and wrecked roughly a fifth of Iran's centrifuges; the same techniques apply to power, water and chemical plants, and the largest concentration of vulnerable industrial systems is in the United States. He was now consulting utilities and chemical firms on an attack he considers inevitable.

*Culture notes.* "Infosec," not "cyber"; Spot the Fed at Def Con; mutual respect across sides for skill and contempt for incompetence; a male-dominated, jiujitsu-loving world where a reporter is treated as a child.

**Evidence.** Seven zero-days; about a fifth of centrifuges; seven years to answer the questions, by which time the NSA's tools had leaked and "the playing field was leveled."

**Key terms.** The fucking salmon; Langner's test; the question list (who buys/is refused; taxpayer money to foreign hackers; undermining commercial tech; stockpile use/leak; what happens if they get out; who profits and who resists).

**So what.** Treat a non-answer about buyers as the answer. For any offensive capability ask where the same technique's targets are most concentrated. Rationalizations that end at the sale are incomplete accounting.

## Chapter 3: The Cowboy

**Central argument.** The public face of the market began in 2002 when John Watters bought bankrupt iDefense for $10 and let young researchers pay hackers for bugs. It worked until Microsoft's security turnaround made exploits scarce and valuable, and mystery government contractors began paying $150,000 for bugs iDefense paid $400 for.

**Full stories.**
*The cowboy and the shell.* iDefense was a threat-alert vendor for banks and agencies; its bankruptcy hearing fell on 9/11 and the judge later chose restructuring. Watters, a Texas private-equity investor with no computer background, bought it for $10 after telling the British investors to call when they ran out of money. He wrote back-pay checks in the kitchen; it was "three years of work in one." Two young researchers, Endler and James, argued vendors lived off hackers' free labor via BugTraq and threatened them with lawsuits, so white hats had no lawful outlet and every incentive to go black. Paying gives clients early warning plus workarounds before patches exist and a unique product. Start small ($75), expect junk, and pay anyway to build trust.

*Vendor hostility.* Microsoft called disclosure "information anarchy"; the Def Con message was give us your bugs first or get sued; Oracle's security chief called bounties immoral, and Watters offered to put his mother on the phone. Symantec's $75M purchase of SecurityFocus (BugTraq) threatened iDefense's raw material. A working proof-of-concept forced vendors who dismissed a bug as minor to patch on the customer's timetable. With a unique product Watters doubled subscription prices ($18K to $38K), dropped non-payers, and won them back at multiples ($25K to $1.5M for one agency).

*The hackers.* Tamer Sahin (Turkey) earned $75, a month's rent, found fifty bugs; a thirteen-year-old in Kansas City bought his mother a second phone line; Cesar Cerrudo; and Greg McManus ("Zenith Parsec"), a New Zealand sheep farmer who took half the bounties, moved into Watters's apartment and painted the lab black. McManus's tutorial set out the motive stack: curiosity first, then rush, power, ideology, puzzle, belonging; money comes later. His kill chain: scan the perimeter, wait for someone to install a convenient app, probe it, mine support forums and update code, reverse-engineer, find remote code execution, harvest credentials from breach dumps or plaintext logs, chain pieces to move from phone system to network to admin machines. Patience and persistence define the "advanced persistent threat."

*Trustworthy Computing.* After Code Red, Melissa, ILOVEYOU, Nimda and federal threats to take business elsewhere, Gates's 2002 memo froze products, retrained about 10,000 developers, created a hacker hotline (with notes on each caller's temperament), and instituted Patch Tuesday. Result: Microsoft bugs became rarer and far more valuable, from pennies to six or seven figures for a remote Windows exploit. The mystery contractors' $150,000 offers followed; iDefense was priced out within three years ($400 to $4,000 to $50,000 per bug). Watters refused to withhold bugs from customers to sell to contractors, a business case distinct from the moral one. Another buyer, Digital Armaments (2005, Tokyo-registered), offered five-figure bounties for exclusive rights, promising to tell vendors "eventually."

*Exit.* $7M of Watters's money in; sold to Verisign for $40M in 2005. Nimda's "R.P. China" string left an attribution puzzle that made cyberterrorism a federal concern.

**Evidence.** Price series ($400, $4,000, $50,000; $150,000 offers); subscription multiples; Gates's memo effects. D5 (Pentagon purposes: deny, degrade, disrupt, deceive, destroy) explains why governments hoard.

**Key terms.** iDefense; BugTraq/Full Disclosure; D5; proof-of-concept as leverage; Trustworthy Computing; Patch Tuesday; kill chain; advanced persistent threat.

**So what.** Whoever pays most for a bug decides whether it is fixed. Don't sue finders; don't assume the market you opened stays yours; ask "who benefits from the hole staying open?"

## Chapter 4: The First Broker

**Central argument.** Long before public bounties, Beltway boutique contractors bought zero-days for US intelligence at six figures, on condition of silence. Access is king, secrecy is a precondition of value, and the cash-in-duffel-bags inefficiency kept prices rising.

**Full stories.**
*"Jimmy Sabien."* A pseudonymous ex-military network defender turned contractor with 80%+ of revenue from the Pentagon and intelligence; his deals pre-dated classification so he could talk, under a false name. His principles: disclosed bugs get named, scored, databased and patched, so government buyers pay for silence and never disclose. Cyberweapons are useless without a way in, so he sold "access": reconnaissance on a target's environment, then a reliable, clean beachhead. Agencies wanted the full kill chain (entry, beaconing to command-and-control, exfiltration, obfuscation) like a special-forces team, with stealth not shock and awe. The trifecta (reliability, invisibility, persistence) rarely appears; when it does, "ka-ching." Exploits near the hardware (a video memory card) survive wipes; the only cure is the dumpster. After intrusion, the first act is checking for other command-and-control beacons, scraping what they collect, sometimes patching the box to lock rivals out; high-value targets host several nations at once (an HP printer exploit used worldwide for years).

*Duplication.* Agencies bought the same exploit two, three, four times because nothing is shared; each wants "the win" and the budget that follows. Sabien once told four agencies to go to lunch together.

*The Grugq.* His Forbes interview, posing with a bag of cash, halved his business overnight: talking to media is a broker's fastest path to bankruptcy.

*Price structure, trust chain.* Mid-1990s: about $1M from an agency for ten exploits; half budgeted to buy bugs; about $50K for a Windows bug, double for an obscure adversary system, about $150K for deep persistent access. Suppliers: post-Soviet Eastern Europeans with skills and no jobs; the most talented in Israel (Unit 8200 veterans, one sixteen-year-old). Deals: hotel-room demos, re-creation before payment, no payment if unreliable, Israeli middlemen with $500K in cash, Western Union, no way to know whether a bug was resold to your enemies. Watters's verdict: dark markets are inefficient and prices only climb; open markets mature and put buyers in charge.

*Budgets.* Cyber was the one growth line in 1990s defense cuts; post-9/11 spending rose 50%+ in five years; contractors and prices doubled yearly; primes (Lockheed, Raytheon, BAE, Northrop, Boeing) poached boutiques; over a hundred contractors, "probably only a dozen that know what they're doing"; even the Missile Defense Agency buys zero-days. A 25-person team can't find everything: "anyone with two thousand dollars to buy a Dell is in the game." The CIA's 1995 working group found the agency unprepared for the web as an intelligence source. Policymakers' grasp was "a mile wide and an inch deep" (a former Strategic Command chief).

*The patriotism pivot and the warning.* When money failed with Watters, contractors invoked service to country. Sabien's phone showed Borenstein's line: the world most likely ends by accident, and computer professionals cause accidents. His parting words: "This will not end well."

**Evidence.** Prices above; 80% revenue; 50% spending growth; "a dozen" competent contractors of over a hundred.

**Key terms.** Access; kill chain; trifecta; firmware persistence; duplication; post-Cold War targeting (collect on everyone; arsenal for a rainy day).

**So what.** A zero-day's value is inversely proportional to how many know it exists. Use the kill-chain checklist to evaluate intrusion capability or defense. Classification and NDAs produce waste.

## Chapter 5: Zero-Day Charlie

**Central argument.** Charlie Miller sold a Linux zero-day to a US agency for $50,000, defied the NSA's attempts to keep him quiet, and published the first public account of exploit prices. Two years later "No More Free Bugs" pushed hackers away from vendors toward the underground, exactly what government buyers wanted.

**Full stories.**
*Miller's sale.* A math PhD hired by the NSA in 2001 as a "global network exploitation" analyst, he left in 2005 for family reasons and hunted bugs on paternity leave. A friend at Transversal Technologies with agency contacts brokered a sale for 10%. One agency offered $10,000; another accepted his $80,000 ask "too quickly" (he'd underpriced); the exploit failed on the target's Linux variant, so the price fell to $50,000 with a two-year silence license; he never learned the target.

*The publication fight.* Former NSA staff must clear anything they publish forever (prepublication review); Miller's appeal took nine months and won because the bug was found as a private citizen. Four NSA officials flew to St. Louis for a fifteen-minute "think of your country" visit with no cash and a plea to abandon the paper; refused. At Carnegie Mellon he showed a redacted $50,000 check and presented "The Legitimate Vulnerability Market"; vendors from Microsoft to Cisco were alarmed; the hacker community split between "unethical" and "yeoman's work." He expected publication to raise prices, embarrass vendors with foreign customers and prompt hackers to demand bounties.

*Price chaos, 2006-07.* Officials cited $250,000, brokers $125,000, hackers were offered $60-120K for Internet Explorer access; a $12,000 PowerPoint exploit deal went to zero when Microsoft patched. Conclusion: hackers were underpaid; only transparency would fix it.

*Why the underground is inefficient.* No price data; sellers can't find buyers without cold-calling; buyers can evaluate, feign disinterest and use the bug; weeks-long vetting during which a patch can zero the value; conscience risk as buyers diversify to foreign governments, fronts and criminals. Buyers can't advertise needs or guarantee exclusivity. Taxpayer money lets the market reach volume.

*No More Free Bugs (March 2009).* Google BCC'd Miller's boss to try to get him fired for reporting an Android exploit for free. Miller, Dino Dai Zovi and Alexander Sotirov declared that reporting to vendors for free, the most ethical option, produced the worst outcomes, and told hackers to stop. Government men at the back didn't clap: fewer patches, more sellers; one NSA veteran worried about whom foreign hackers would sell to instead.

*Miller's later career.* Celebrity Apple hacker (a Trojan stock-ticker app beating App Store vetting; first remote iPhone hack; a two-minute MacBook Air hack; "I'm that Apple 0day guy"), later the Jeep Cherokee remote-control exploit and Uber's driverless-car security; Apple's black-box security blacklisted the researcher who exposed vetting failures. In a hotel elevator running the same platform as the hacked Jeep: "I started taking the stairs."

**Evidence.** $50,000 sale; $10K/$80K offers; $250K/$125K/$60-120K price claims; $12,000 PowerPoint deal; nine-month review.

**Key terms.** Five options; prepublication review; No More Free Bugs; Transversal Technologies.

**So what.** Quick acceptance of your price means you asked too little. Use "which of the five options pays?" to predict where a discovered bug goes. Vendor hostility subsidizes the underground. Closing question: not every exploit goes to Uncle Sam.

## Chapter 6: Project Gunman

**Central argument.** The NSA's zero-day obsession predates the internet. In 1984 a Soviet implant hidden in the US embassy's IBM Selectric typewriters showed an adversary can capture data before encryption touches it, so encryption alone is never enough and anything that plugs into a wall is an attack surface.

**Full stories.**
*Deeley's hunch.* Walter G. Deeley, NSA deputy director for communications security, chain-smoking and profane, founder of the NSA's 24/7 operations center, lobbied Reagan to approve Project Gunman: six months, "VRK" (very restricted knowledge), to swap out every piece of electrical equipment at the Moscow embassy and examine the old gear at Fort Meade. He posted a $5,000 reward (about a quarter of an analyst's salary) for the first analyst to find the bug, an early bug bounty.

*The logistics chain.* NSA disassembled, X-rayed and tagged 250 replacement typewriters (only 50 obtainable at first), sealed them in tamperproof bags unavailable in the USSR, and escorted them by armed escort Fort Meade, Dover, Frankfurt, Moscow. The Soviets "coincidentally" disabled the elevator, so ten tons went up the stairs by hand. The ambassador was told by handwritten note to announce a routine upgrade. Each box was emptied, refilled with the old machine and resealed with sensors wired to a Marine guard post; old gear was escorted back to a trailer in the Fort Meade parking lot, where 25 analysts worked nights. They cleared the crypto gear (ten weeks, the "obvious" leak point) and faxes and teletypes (nothing), and on July 23, 1984 one analyst noticed an extra coil on a Selectric power switch, X-rayed it and saw the implant.

*The implant.* A magnetometer inside a metal bar converted keystroke mechanics into magnetic disturbances; an electronic unit logged them and radio-burst the data to a nearby Soviet post; remote-controllable so it could be switched off during inspections. The first traced to a 1976 shipment, so about eight years undetected; 16 typewriters were found bugged (13 Moscow, 3 Leningrad), across 5+ variants including battery-powered. Earlier, "The Thing"/"Golden Mouth" (1945-52) was a bug hidden in a carved Great Seal gifted to the ambassador. Missed clues: an antenna in the embassy chimney that nobody asked about; the Soviets' own ban on electric typewriters for classified material. Deeley's vindication: encryption was never the weak point; Faurer later admitted disdain for the adversary's technical ability was itself a vulnerability.

**Evidence.** 1984; 250/50 typewriters; 25 analysts; ten tons; 16 bugged units; $5,000; eight years.

**Key terms.** Pre-encryption capture; supply-chain tampering; VRK; the Thing.

**So what.** Ask where data lives in the clear; build chain of custody and tamper evidence into procurement for sensitive systems; read an adversary's defensive habits as intelligence about offense. When a leak survives all obvious explanations, widen the search to boring equipment. Gunman taught the NSA to hide implants as well as find them.

## Chapter 7: The Godfather

**Central argument.** Jim Gosler is the unheralded architect of American offensive cyber capability. His career traces the pivot from passive signals interception to "hacking the endpoints," driven by one realization: beyond a few thousand lines of code, no one can guarantee a system is free of implants.

**Full stories.**
*Gosler.* A Sandia scientist, NSA's first visiting scientist and founding director of the CIA's Clandestine Information Technology Office (CITO, 1996). His department went from $500K (DOE) in 1990 to $50M in intelligence money by 1995 while the rest of the IC was cut. He periodized history as Before Gunman (BG; "la-la land") and After Gunman (AG; breaking into "anything with a pulse"). His foundational text: Ken Thompson's 1984 Turing lecture "Reflections on Trusting Trust" (unless you wrote the code yourself you can't be sure it's not a Trojan). The Ware Report (1967) and Anderson Report (1972) had said technology cannot provide a secure system in an open environment, and trapdoors are inevitable wherever operating systems accept updates.

*Chaperon.* In 1985 Sandia split senior staff into subverters and evaluators. Gosler, subverting for the first time, planted a "cheap" implant (lines from the game Zork tricking the application into disclosing secret variables) and a deep implant later called a groundbreaking technical achievement. Evaluators spent months and failed on both; Gosler needed three eight-hour whiteboard sessions to explain the second, and management refused to use it for training for fear recruits would quit. In Chaperon 2 roles flipped: of about 100 engineers only Gosler found the new implant. Exported to the NSA in 1987, Robert Morris Sr.'s PhD squad also couldn't find it: "anything with more than a couple thousand lines of code looked suspicious." Morris's rule of thumb: total confidence in 10,000 lines or fewer, zero confidence above 100,000; Gosler's implant hid in under 3,000.

*Doctrine.* CITO recruited people: sysadmins, fiber techs, shipping couriers, even the janitor; humans hold combinations, passwords, manuals; online data lets operatives be vetted in minutes instead of months. Gosler dismissed recruiting at Black Hat/Def Con: the best nation-state hackers work quietly in labs and SOCs. NSA/CIA turf rivalry over "rice bowls" delayed adaptation until the CIA feared abolition. The vocabulary flip "SIGINT at rest vs. in transit" let the NSA hack endpoints (CIA turf) rather than intercept only atmospheric signals. Classic in-transit examples: Operation Regal (Berlin cable tunnel, 1950s), Ivy Bells (Soviet undersea cable tap, 1970s). Hayden's "golden age of signals intelligence" moved the fear from going blind to drowning.

*Crypto AG, SIGINT Enabling.* Swiss vendor Crypto AG's machines were spiked so US decoders could read Iran, Iraq and Libya traffic, "the NSA's own Gunman." The SIGINT Enabling Project (Snowden-leaked 2013) funded covertly influencing commercial product designs and claimed "complete enabling" of two leading VPN encryption chip makers.

**Evidence.** 10,000/100,000 lines; Gosler's under-3,000-line implant; Linux 176K to 15M lines, F-35 8M, Vista 50M; $500K to $50M; ~100 engineers, one found the implant.

**Key terms.** BG/AG; complexity threshold; Chaperon; Tiers I-VI (I-II buy click-and-shoot exploits; III-IV train hackers and hire contractors; V-VI spend years and billions and insert zero-days into the global supply chain, VI at push-button scale: Russia, China, US); Willie Sutton principle; CITO; Crypto AG.

**So what.** Verification does not scale with code size; red-team your own detection by planting implants and measuring whether evaluators find them; match defense to the adversary tier; humans in the supply chain are the softest and cheapest entry. Every capability the US built is a capability others can build.

## Chapter 8: The Omnivore

**Central argument.** After 9/11 TAO grew from a few hundred targeted implants into an industrialized, near-indiscriminate "electronic omnivore," creating a moral hazard: every backdoor in shared commercial technology is a backdoor into America's own infrastructure.

**Full stories.**
*Recruiting and culture.* TAO and its Remote Operations Center at Fort Meade (jeans-and-T-shirt hackers on 24/7 rotating shifts; existence long denied) recruited post-9/11 via "operational interviews": unmarked buses, red "uncleared" badges, polygraph, psych eval, about $40K starting salary. Iris scanners were considered and rejected for adding complexity, "more ways to get hacked."

*The assembly line.* One unit finds bugs and writes exploits; one hones implants and payloads; one handles hard-to-reach targets (hack the mistress or maid, leapfrog in); the Transgression Branch does fourth-party collection (piggybacking on other nations' hacks, including allies); others run infrastructure through front companies. The race to the bare metal: terrorist channels, operating systems, browsers and apps, kernel-level exploits.

*Genie and Shotgiant.* By 2013 Genie managed about 85,000 implants (4x in five years), targeting every major router, switch, firewall and encryption device; foreign sysadmins were high-priority for credentials. Operation Shotgiant (from 2007) penetrated Huawei's Shenzhen HQ, stole source code and planted backdoors, then used them to reach Huawei customers in Iran, Cuba, Sudan, Syria, North Korea and two major Chinese cellular networks, so accusations leveled at Huawei were likely true in reverse.

*Abuses and weak controls.* A dozen NSA employees were caught spying on exes (LOVEINT); demoted, stripped of clearances, none prosecuted. The FISA court approved 1,748 of 1,789 applications unchanged in 2012. Stellar Wind and Total Information Awareness produced leads so vague agents called them "Pizza Hut cases"; 9/11 was a failure to act on intelligence already held. Tools like Snacks (social-graph visualization) and Where's My Node? (cell-tower handoff alerting) automated analysis.

*The 2008 USB incident.* Russian-seeded thumb drives in a Middle East base parking lot breached classified US networks; the Pentagon superglued its USB ports.

*The moral abstraction.* The notes note the "death-count briefing" as the moment abstraction ends: exploit work is not "just math." Peter Neumann diagnosed the NSA's fatal flaw as arrogance that its flaws would not be found by others.

**Evidence.** 85,000 implants; 1,748/1,789; dozen LOVEINT cases; "no patents on vulnerabilities."

**Key terms.** Intravenous principle; race to the bare metal; TAO/ROC; Genie; Shotgiant; LOVEINT; moral hazard of shared technology.

**So what.** Every surveillance implant is an attack capability with a deferred payload. Kernel and firmware access survives patches, so prioritize those layers. Automation at scale removes the pause where someone asks "should we?"; build it back deliberately.

## Chapter 9: The Rubicon

**Central argument.** Stuxnet, the Olympic Games operation against Natanz, was the first cyberweapon to cross from espionage into physical destruction. Its escape onto the open internet proved that a weapon built from zero-days cannot be kept in the box and will boomerang back.

**Full stories.**
*The decision.* Between failed diplomacy and airstrikes (World War III), Keith Alexander offered Bush the "third option": a cyberattack mimicking natural accidents. Olympic Games was a five-way effort (NSA, Israel's Unit 8200, CIA, Mossad, national energy labs); the operation is set to declassify in 2039.

*The kill chain.* A human (identity unknown) carried an infected USB across the air gap into a Natanz PC. A .LNK zero-day fires when Windows renders the drive's icons, no click. The worm spreads over Natanz's file-sharing network via a printer-spooler exploit and an unpatched RCE bug, installs drivers signed with stolen certs from two Taiwanese firms sharing one office park (likely an inside job), finds machines running Siemens Step 7, logs in with the default "admin"/"password," embeds in the Step 7 database, waits for a technician to connect, and reaches PLCs. It fires only on clusters of exactly 164 centrifuges at 800-1100 Hz, a limit designed with NSA legal review to avoid collateral damage (PLCs run everything from roller coasters to cotton candy).

*The Mission Impossible replay.* After a 13-day observation it loops normal readings to Step 7 monitors while spinning rotors to 1400 Hz for 15 minutes and down to 2 Hz for 50 minutes on a 27-day cycle. Because centrifuges already failed at about 10% a year, Iranian engineers blamed themselves, turned on each other, fired technicians and guarded machines. As Sabien put it, "We're computer professionals. We cause accidents." About 2,000 of 8,700 centrifuges were destroyed by early 2010.

*The escape.* In June 2010 the worm left Natanz. The leading theory blames an Israeli-added spreading mechanism ("Sonofabitch... they went too far" - Biden); Obama chose to accelerate rather than shut down. Kaspersky, Symantec, Microsoft and Ralph Langner reverse-engineered it; Langner's TED talk named the US as lead cyber power ("Fortunately... otherwise our problems would be even bigger"). He had warned for years that PLCs would be sabotaged and nobody acted. Hayden called it "a whiff of August 1945."

**Evidence.** Seven zero-days (4 Microsoft + 3 Siemens); 500 KB, fifty times larger than anything seen, 100x the code that sent Apollo 11 to the moon, a "Costco bargain" against a $2B B-2; 164; 13 days; 27-day cycle; 2,000/8,700. Caveat: one spread mechanism was documented in Hakin9, so not a true zero-day.

**Key terms.** Third option; sabotage-as-accident; PLC; air gap; Olympic Games.

**So what.** Precision does not equal containment; design propagation limits separately from targeting limits. The air gap is a speed bump. Default passwords and unpatched known bugs decide nation-state operations. Using a novel weapon legitimizes it and teaches adversaries a shortcut.


## Chapter 10: The Factory

**Central argument.** As Stuxnet boomeranged home and the NSA doubled down on offense over defense, a private zero-day industry crystallized around the Beltway, epitomized by Vulnerability Research Labs (VRL), turning raw bugs into reliable turnkey cyberweapons and inadvertently seeding an unregulated global arms race.

**Full stories.**
*VRL.* Five elite TAO hackers (the "Maryland Five") left in 2008 seeking autonomy, not money, and built VRL in Reston, kept secret 12 years, acquired by Computer Sciences Corp. in 2010. A monk-like, Navy-SEAL culture (a colleague donated a kidney), with a Sun Tzu motto. The value chain as the notes reconstruct it: VRL conceives a gadget or cyberweapon; tasks contracted foreign hackers (Argentina, Malaysia, Italy, Australia, France, Singapore) or takes a "walk-in," "not caring" who else the hacker sells to; weaponizes it and runs it through fuzz farms (tens of thousands of VMs) until it is 100% reliable and silent; delivers a push-button tool through a "special contract vehicle" that hides buyer and exploit origin; the agency uses it only in a critical precision operation (e.g., against al-Zawahiri or a missile system) because burning it risks discovery. Perlroth's reporting hit walls at every step: contracts booked as "$2.9M for computer peripheral equipment," exactly as Panetta warned.

*NOBUS.* The NSA's rationale: patch low-hanging fruit any adversary could find; keep exploits only the US can supposedly weaponize (Hayden's "four acres of supercomputers" test). The premise collapsed from about 2012 as Russia and China honed the same router-hacking skills.

*The industrial machine.* Turbine ("Owning the Net") was an NSA robot for "industrial-scale exploitation," by 2013 designed to autonomously manage millions of implants for spying and "active attack," "to relieve the user from needing to know/care about the details"; the NSA could man only about 1/8 of its implants by hand. The Der Spiegel TAO catalog (2013, 50 pages) included Monkeycalendar (geolocation via invisible SMS), Picasso (location + hot-mic), Surlyspawn (keystrokes off air-gapped machines, "the digital Gunman"), Dropoutjeep (offline iPhone monitoring), Cottonmouth I/Nightstand (USB radio transceiver, suspected Stuxnet delivery); its sophistication made some suspect a second mole beyond Snowden.

*Brain drain and brain bills.* The government paid contractors more than employees for the same work; talent and prices rose. Developers threw tools "over the fence" and never learned how they were used ("Was that me?").

**Evidence.** Cyber Command created June 2009, budget tripled $2.7B to $7B by 2012, staff 900 to 4,000 to 14,000 (2020). 2013 NSA breaking-and-entering budget $652M, twice network defense. New $25.1M zero-day procurement line, enough for about 625 zero-days a year. RAND: average zero-day secret about 7 years, but about 25% discovered within 18 months (earlier studies about 10 months). Conflict: Ch 24 says RAND's average is about a year; the notes do not reconcile (see Part 3f).

**Key terms.** NOBUS; weaponization gap; turnkey vs raw; clean fail/fuzz farm; Turbine; Cyber Command; Maryland Five; special contract vehicles; burn economics ("virtually never deployed 'in anger'").

**So what.** The valuable work is reliable weaponization, not bug discovery. Contractors let agencies buy in markets they can't legally touch, laundering provenance and removing accountability. Paying more for offense than for defense drives a brain drain and inflates the market. Secrecy protected the agency from accountability, not Americans: classification kept out journalists, not adversaries.

## Chapter 11: The Kurd

**Central argument.** The zero-day trade escaped American control. Through Sinan Eren and the failure to regulate exports (Wassenaar), US-trained talent and tooling flowed to repressive governments, culminating in ex-NSA hackers in Abu Dhabi targeting dissidents, journalists and eventually the US First Lady.

**Full stories.**
*Immunity and Eren.* Immunity Inc. (Miami, 2002) was founded by ex-NSA hacker Dave Aitel; its Canvas is an automated exploitation framework, and The Shellcoder's Handbook angered the NSA (a dartboard with his face). Aitel's refusal to name customers is "the fucking salmon"; "He's very American... this was business." Sinan Eren, a Kurdish hacker from Istanbul who saw hacktivism as resistance, came on an H-1B to San Jose and became Immunity's second employee. He built a homemade upper-right-quadrant ethic: cross-reference Amnesty International reports and sell only to governments scoring high on democratic norms and press rights. He quit in 2009 after a Turkish general demanded personal training, and later built a mobile anti-surveillance app, protecting citizens from his former customers. He worried even about "friendly" customers (France/Algeria, Spain/Basques). Gera Richarte (Ch 17) helped plan his Kurdish resistance.

*Wassenaar.* The 1996 nonbinding multilateral regime added surveillance tech to its control list (2012-13) after Perlroth/Citizen Lab reporting; the US alone never adopted the changes after EFF, Google and the $1.7B pen-testing industry objected. Arguments: restriction would handicap cross-border research, expose US firms to selective prosecution, and regulate math/speech. Only encryption export controls apply; embargoed countries (North Korea, Iran, Sudan, Syria, Cuba) are off-limits but "favorable" states (including Turkey) are fair game. The industry's slogan: "We don't sell weapons, we sell information."

*Project Raven.* CyberPoint, under the Clarke-initiated (2008) DREAD contract, diversified beyond US clients, luring ex-NSA hackers at 2-4x salary to Abu Dhabi. David Evenden arrived in 2014 and was handed two folders: "purple" (memorized defensive cover story for reporters) and "black" (the real offensive job). The mission crept: track ISIS; prove Qatar funds the Muslim Brotherhood; hack Qatar; hack FIFA officials; template spearphish for UAE dissident journalist Rori Donaghy (a "never to be sent" booby-trapped email that was sent; fingerprints on about 400 victims including jailed Emiratis); and in late 2015, reading every email between Michelle Obama's staff and Sheikha Moza's team as the First Lady planned a Doha trip, the "cold, hard slap." Evenden demanded the State Department authorization letter; it was real but dated 2011. Promised destruction of Americans' data never happened. When contracts moved to the Emirati LLC Dark Matter (no State cover, direct Emirati control; later under FBI investigation), he refused, was frozen out, returned home, tipped the FBI and went public with a PSA for NSA operators. Colleagues joined Dark Matter knowing they'd target Americans ("If I do this a couple more years, I'll be fine"). The notes say "in the War on Terror and the offensive cyber trade, you could rationalize just about anything."

**Evidence.** 1996 Wassenaar; 2012-13 additions; $1.7B industry; 2-4x pay; about 400 victims; lifetime bar on revealing tradecraft routinely claimed to be waived.

**Key terms.** Wassenaar; "we sell information"; upper-right-quadrant ethic; purple/black briefings; DREAD; Dark Matter.

**So what.** Self-regulation does not hold; the US built demand and declined to regulate supply. Offensive contracts drift. "Two folders" and a hidden job description are reliable signs you're being used for something you'd refuse if told. Talent and tradecraft are the real exports.

## Chapter 12: Dirty Business

**Central argument.** Adriel Desautels was the zero-day broker with a conscience: sold only to US entities and turned down 85% of offers. His downfall shows that in an opaque, unregulated market personal ethics ("Bushido") cannot survive money and middlemen.

**Full stories.**
*The ethical broker.* Boston-based Netragard (aliases Cyanide, then "Simon Smith"); pen-test motto "We Protect You From People Like Us," with real break-ins, freight elevators, bribed cleaners, forged badges (the "get out of jail free card" clause). His origin sale: a $16,000 joke price for an MP3 zero-day (play the song, own the machine), paid without hesitation, which told him the market was real. He stepped into the light while rivals cultivated Darth Vader mystique; trust from three-letter agencies and Beltway contractors was his asset. He vetted sellers by "micro-expressions and tells," accepting about 15% of offers; exclusivity cost 3x pay plus NDAs backed by implicit threats ("if it pops up elsewhere... you're going to get gutted"). His broker-grade spec: zero target interaction, about 98.9% reliability, clean fail.

*The new mercenaries.* The Grugq, MJM (scrubbed from the internet), Luigi and Donato, Thomas Lim (Singapore, COSEINC) and Chaouki Bekrar (Vupen, the "Wolf of Vuln Street," Darth Vader avatar) felt no responsibility and honored no code.

*The collapse.* His exploit developers threatened to stop selling unless he dropped exclusivity; buyers accepted "exclusive for 2-3 months, then nonexclusive." A trusted US buyer asked to resell to a "friendly" European client in Italy; Desautels assumed friendly, brokered an Adobe Flash zero-day and emailed Hacking Team that he was "comfortable working with you directly" (ignoring three years of front-page reporting, including Perlroth's, on Hacking Team). On July 5, 2015 Phineas Fisher hacked the Milan spyware firm and dumped 420 GB of contracts, invoices, source code and five years of email. Hacking Team's "Remote Control System" had been sold to the FBI, DEA, CIA (via an Annapolis front), Sudan ($1M), Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Russia, Morocco, Bahrain, Ethiopia, the 'Stans, with near-deals with Belarus and a Bangladeshi death squad, despite CEO David Vincenzetti's denials and a supposed human-rights veto board. Citizen Lab had traced the spyware to servers in dictatorships. The firm dropped Sudan and Russia in 2014, after hundreds of thousands were dead / Crimea annexed. Desautels found his own email and his Flash bug baked into spyware sold to Sudan, the UAE, the Saudis and Ethiopia: "I'd never been so repulsed in my life." He shut down his zero-day business, noting a vendor "could not sufficiently vet the ethics and intentions of new buyers." His one consolation: exposure meant the Flash bug would be patched.

*Coverage as advertisement.* Perlroth's exposés, meant to shame the trade, showed have-not governments what they were missing: "no intelligence agency on the planet was going to miss out."

**Evidence.** 85% rejected / ~15% accepted; $16,000; 98.9%; ladder: routers/USB low five figures; PDF/Safari/Firefox/Word/Excel; Windows/Exchange remote $100K-$250K; urgent $500K-$1M; broker cut 3% (quick match) to 60% (sourcing, vetting, testing); 420 GB; Sudan $1M.

**Key terms.** Reputation as currency; ideal-state exploit; sniff test; exclusivity as rental; Bushido ("More like Bullshit"); Hacking Team; Phineas Fisher.

**So what.** One middleman defeats "U.S.-only." Do due diligence on the buyer and end use; denials, veto boards and "untraceable" branding are marketing. Sunlight doesn't always disinfect: the market is a ratchet. "Nobody in this trade ever seemed to take a stance until it was too late."

## Chapter 13: Guns for Hire

**Central argument.** Israel's NSO Group and Pegasus democratized nation-state phone surveillance: for a seven-figure fee any government could invisibly own a smartphone, the encryption-proof endpoint. Vendor "ethics committees" are theater; once the weapon ships, abuse is inevitable.

**Full stories.**
*NSO.* Founded 2008 by Unit 8200 alumni Shalev Hulio and Omri Lavie, originally selling remote phone troubleshooting; it pivoted to surveillance after the FBI's 2011 "going dark" testimony (general counsel Valerie Caproni). The FBI pushed for backdoor mandates; national security officials knew they'd become "a target for all." Francisco Partners bought a controlling stake for about $120M in 2014. Pegasus handles calls, texts, email, GPS, WhatsApp/Skype, "room tap" (mic + camera) and screenshots, a "ghost" that "leaves no traces." Its secret sauce was zero-click "over the air stealth installation," the "unicorn" that terrified Hacking Team (Vincenzetti: "zero tolerance on a feature we allegedly blatantly miss"). It is battery-conscious: detects when it is draining the battery, shuts down, and waits for WiFi. NSO had never been denied an export license. Mahaffey's AK-47 line: "When you're selling AK-47s, you can't control how they'll be used once they leave the loading docks."

*Mansoor.* Ahmed Mansoor (Egret), an Emirati poet/activist among the "UAE Five," was hit by Hacking Team, Gamma Group, and CyberPoint (which also bugged his wife, Purple Egret, and a baby monitor). In August 2016 he got texts promising details of torture of Emiratis, with a link. Suspicious, he forwarded them to Citizen Lab's Bill Marczak instead of clicking. Marczak infected his own iPhone and found a chain of three Apple zero-days (a Safari bug worth six-to-seven figures) delivering a payload from a UAE domain, its files naming "Pegasus" and "NSO" hundreds of times, the first Pegasus infection caught in the wild. Lookout helped; Apple shipped an emergency patch to about 1 billion users. Mansoor was sentenced in 2018 to 10 years, largely in solitary, on hunger strike. A single dissident who declined to click exposed a weapon sold to dozens of governments.

*Mexico.* Pegasus targeted soda-tax proponents (nutritionists, doctors), anticorruption activists, lawyers for the 43 disappeared students and the Atenco assault victims, and journalist Carmen Aristegui (and her 16-year-old son), critics of President Peña Nieto. Lures escalated from news links to "your daughter was in an accident" to "your wife is having an affair," redirecting to a funeral home while Pegasus loaded. Mexican law required judicial sign-off, but illegal surveillance was the norm. The most common tip-off of misuse was a call from the reporter herself.

**Evidence.** Pricing: $500K install + $650K for 10 phones; +100 targets $800K; scaling down to +10 for $150K; about double Hacking Team. Reach: 67 servers, 400+ targets, operators in 45+ countries.

**Key terms.** Zero-click; endpoint hacking; going dark; room tap; AK-47 defense; democratization of surveillance.

**So what.** Endpoint compromise defeats encryption with no backdoor; "don't click" is no longer enough guidance. Treat ethics committees, export approvals and "no-trace" claims as marketing. Independent watchdogs (Citizen Lab, Lookout) and a target who doesn't click are the real detection mechanism. Backdoor mandates fail (Skype in Luxembourg).

## Chapter 14: Aurora

**Central argument.** Operation Aurora, China's 2009 breach of Google, made a private company fight a nation-state, name China publicly, and rebuild itself as a quasi-intelligence agency, starting "The Resistance": the tech industry as the front line of cyber defense.

**Full stories.**
*The blip.* A new tripwire fired; an intern assumed another intern; by 4 p.m. the "blip" was racing through employee machines faster than anything Google had seen. Zurich (Morgan Marquis-Boire) picked up the trail while Mountain View slept and confirmed it was no insider. Google called Mandiant (Kevin Mandia, "the Wolf," told not to wear suits and wore them anyway), ran "Upchuck Hour" (hand over all firewall/web/email/chat logs), and traced infection to Beijing staff who clicked a "Go Kill Yourself" chat link, which led to an IE zero-day on a Taiwan server and encrypted malware. 250 engineers, sleeping on campus, mapped the path to the PLA (Legion Yankee) and found the goal: source code plus dissidents' Gmail. The Five Poisons (CCP priority targets): prodemocracy activists, Tibetans, Uighurs, pro-independence Taiwanese, Falun Gong; China reserved its best zero-days to menace its own people.

*Why source code.* Passwords change but a backdoor in Gmail's source guarantees permanent access. Verifying about 2 billion lines clean is Gosler's Sandia problem at planetary scale: "the only certainty was uncertainty."

*Responders.* Heather Adkins ("hunt down evil"), Eric Grosse, Marquis-Boire, Sergey Brin (a Soviet émigré who took it personally; "Never again"), Mandia. Google confiscated touched machines overnight ("Security incident. Took your machine."), reset all passwords, shut Google.cn and redirected to uncensored Hong Kong, tipped the State Department, and on Jan 12, 2010 published, the first US company to call out China. Alperovitch/Comey's "two kinds of companies": those who know they've been hacked by China and those who don't know yet. Attack routing through universities (Jiaotong), Tencent, 163.com and Sina left deniability; the NSA saw a "probable intelligence requirement feed from China's Ministry of State Security." Legion Yankee resurfaced at RSA (2011) and Lockheed Martin; Aurora also hit defense, tech and finance firms.

*The asymmetry.* China steals trade secrets to hand state-owned firms; the NSA has no company to hand DuPont's rivals' formulas to: "the greatest transfer of wealth in history" (Alexander).

*The regression.* Within a year execs pushed reentry; later Dragonfly (secret censored search), a Beijing AI lab, a Saudi women-tracking app, Pentagon Project Maven (resignations), disinformation ad profits, radicalizing YouTube algorithms. Brin hoped Google's stand would open China; Xi's Great Cannon and Great Firewall showed the opposite. Google poached NSA/allied hackers ($100K no-questions signing bonuses) and made a painful culture shift (security vs "employee happiness"); "Nobody has ever said, 'I love long passwords.'"

**Evidence.** About 2 billion lines; 250 engineers; Jan 12, 2010; $100K signing bonuses. Caveat: the notes call the intelligence-agency response a "fool's errand" most companies decline.

**Key terms.** Fog of Peace; Legion Yankee; Five Poisons; name-and-shame; "shepherds of their safety" (Adkins).

**So what.** Triage against innocent-explanation bias; treat source code as the crown jewel; breaking silence is a deterrent rarely used; corporate principle erodes against market size.

## Chapter 15: Bounty Hunters

**Central argument.** Bug bounties pioneered post-Aurora by Google, then Facebook, Microsoft and HackerOne became the defensive counter-market: pay the world's hackers to disclose and fix bugs rather than weaponize and sell them. Bounties can never match government prices, so it is a war of incentives, not dollars. Aurora was Silicon Valley's Project Gunman.

**Full stories.**
*Google, Facebook.* Google's "leet" bounties: min $500, max $1,337, then $31,337, up to $60K for Chrome, eventually $1.5M for a full Android remote jailbreak by 2020; funded new lives for hackers in Algeria, Egypt, India, Romania, Poland; some donated to charity. Facebook/Zuckerberg made "hacker" a positive identity, sent bounties with no upper limit ($1.5M to 330 researchers by 2013) after the Sandberg "sensitive" email; MySpace had sued bug-reporters, Dorsey said "those guys like to whine a lot." Telling the world about the attack "ended up being the best recruiting strategy" (Adkins).

*Vupen/Zerodium.* Chaouki Bekrar refused Pwn2Own disclosure ("wouldn't share with Google for $1M"), sold to NSA/BSI/NATO, and after the EU revoked its export license rebranded as Zerodium in D.C. with a price list: $80K Chrome, $100K Android, $500K iPhone, $1M-$2.5M for zero-click iMessage/WhatsApp and remote jailbreaks. Soghoian called him a "modern-day merchant of death... the bullets for cyberwar." A Chrome bug earns about 3x more on the government market.

*Moussouris at Microsoft.* Microsoft had a dead disclosure link for years ("calling 911 and getting a full voicemail"), then charmed hackers with beer and karaoke; its Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure policy handled 200K reports a year. Reports dried up in 2011, hackers hoarding or selling to contractors. Katie Moussouris could not get budget, so she spent two years gathering data showing the drop-off was worst for Internet Explorer, a prime government target. She studied game theory to avoid perverse incentives ("the more money on offense, the more people abandon defense") and chose to pay $500 to $100K for bugs in beta IE/Windows that governments don't yet want. Ballmer's team approved a one-month pilot in June 2013, timed as Snowden's Prism leak eroded trust; the first bounty went to a Google engineer; two months of critical bugs arrived in one month; a permanent program was approved that November and paid about $250K in year one (about one engineer's salary).

*HackerOne.* Founded 2014 by Alex Rice (Facebook), Moussouris (Microsoft) and the three Dutchmen (Terheggen, Abma, Prins, who hacked 100 Valley firms in about 15 minutes each); $9M from Benchmark ("Every company is going to do this... to not try this is brain dead" - Gurley). Clients: Yahoo, Square, Twitter, GM, Toyota, Verizon, Lufthansa and the Pentagon.

*Hack the Pentagon.* Ash Carter's 2016 program (RSA groans): 1,400+ hackers, DDS ("Get shit done"); a Synack hacker breached classified networks in under 4 hours; TADS F-15 zero-days; later $34M funded. The OPM breach (2015, ~1M+ federal employees' personal, financial, medical data and fingerprints, by Chinese hackers) spurred the government to act.

*Stuxnet's cousins.* Duqu and Flame; Flame spread via Microsoft's Windows Update mechanism (900M machines), the "Holy Grail" that turned customer trust into a weapon.

**Evidence.** Prices and figures above; passwords: with billions dumped and rainbow tables cracking hashes for about $1, 2FA is "the single best" defense ("Dear reader, use long passwords"). Inglis: "462-452, all offense and no defense." Cyber Shatner: "in any market, there is a fool."

**Key terms.** Compensation/recognition/intellectual happiness; beta-scope bounty; HackerOne; Pwn2Own; Zerodium; Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure.

**So what.** You can't outbid governments; win on recognition, openness and challenge; scope to pre-release software; use breaches for hiring; 2FA plus long passwords neutralize the most common attack. Even the Pentagon spends vastly more keeping holes open than closing its own.

## Chapter 16: Going Dark

**Central argument.** The Snowden "Muscular" leak, the NSA hacking Google's and Yahoo's data centers to grab data before encryption, with a triumphant hand-drawn smiley face, turned Silicon Valley against its own government and triggered the crypto wars: universal device encryption, Project Zero, and the FBI's failed attempt to force a backdoor.

**Full stories.**
*Muscular.* An NSA-GCHQ man-in-the-middle on Google and Yahoo internal links; in one day it grabbed 444,743 Yahoo address books plus Hotmail, Facebook, Gmail data. Google's front-end servers held data unencrypted internally until Snowden. The smiley face "converted an ambiguous diagram into a declaration of war." Prism, by contrast, was downstream (court orders) and its slides misleadingly implied direct server access; companies had never heard the name. Google's fix: encrypt data between its own data centers (Perfect Forward Secrecy) and lay its own sensored subsea cable. Grosse (Silicon Valley's Gandalf): "Signals intercept is totally off the table."

*Project Zero.* Launched Aug 2014 at Lake Tahoe by Chris Evans with Ben Hawkes, Tavis Ormandy, Geohot, Ian Beer, and Lokihardt (Jung Hoon Lee), it attacked the whole ecosystem (Flash, Java, Intel chips, iOS, Windows), published bugs after a 90-day deadline, and found 1,600+ critical bugs. Beer neutered iOS exploits used against Uighurs. "Where there was one bug, there were more." Trade-off: peak exploitation right after publication, but silence doesn't improve long-term security. Jailbreaks need chains; breaking one link sets spies back months or years.

*The crypto wars.* Zimmermann's PGP (1990s) led to Clinton's failed Clipper Chip (1996), then Zfone (2011, "This can't be good"), then iPhone 6 full-device encryption (Sept 2014; Apple holds no keys; 10 wrong tries wipes the phone). Tim Cook, flooded with German (Stasi-memory) letters, brought them to Obama's 2013 meeting and positioned Apple as a civil-rights org selling devices, not data.

*San Bernardino.* Farook and Malik killed 14 and destroyed their own devices but left Farook's county-owned, un-backed-up iPhone. The FBI (Comey's "Going Dark tour") chose it as the perfect precedent (dead suspect, no Fourth Amendment claim, employer consents) and asked Apple to write "Government OS" to bypass the 10-tries wipe. Cook's 1,100-word letter refused ("the equivalent of cancer"), citing the fresh OPM breach as proof the government can't safeguard a key. Public opinion tilted to Apple (even a victim's mother). A week before trial the DOJ dropped the case after unnamed American hackers (not Cellebrite) sold a zero-day for $1.3M (more than Comey's remaining salary), and the FBI wouldn't tell Apple the flaw: the first time the government admitted paying top dollar for a vulnerability. The market, not the court, resolved it. Comey admitted litigation "stimulated a marketplace."

**Evidence.** 444,743; 1,600+ bugs; 90 days; 1,100 words; $1.3M; 10 tries; 14 killed.

**Key terms.** Upstream/downstream; Muscular; GFE; Prism; Perfect Forward Secrecy; Project Zero; Clipper Chip; Government OS.

**So what.** Covert interception damages trust more than lawful compelled access; encrypt the seams between your own systems; encryption doesn't stop an endpoint zero-day; a "good guys only" backdoor is a target for all and a precedent for every government; publicity inflates the exploit trade.

## Chapter 17: Cyber Gauchos

**Central argument.** Argentina's hacker scene shows how the market globalized beyond US control: scarcity bred elite developers, and once Stuxnet and Snowden published the blueprint, foreign brokers could simply fly to Buenos Aires. The Western "sell only to good governments" calculus collapses in the Global South.

**Full stories.**
*The scene.* "Atado con alambre" ("held together with wire") is the MacGyver mantra of a country under an "embargo on cool shit," forcing reverse engineering to the metal. Cesar Cerrudo, iDefense-era teenage star from Paraná, demoed switching D.C. (and NYC, SF) traffic lights from a laptop. Core Security's Gera Richarte and Ivan Arce, the godfathers, built the Impact/Implant automated attack tool (NASA an early customer) and trained Juliano Rizzo and the Gaucho; they opened a New York office on Sept 6, 2001, and 9/11 five days later erased $1M in contracts as Argentina's economy imploded.

*Ekoparty.* Latin America's largest hacking con, with Zerodium as "Platinum Sponsor"; "throw a stone, you'll hit someone selling exploits" (Fede Kirschbaum). Perlroth watched Rizzo demo a six-figure zero-day onstage; foreign brokers swarmed him afterward, not for the burned exploit but for his next one and a standing relationship "for a rainy day." Arce: "A guy from the NSA and a guy from Iran show up with big bags of cash. Do you perform an ethical analysis? Or do you weigh the bags?"

*The Gaucho.* Alfredo Ortega, a Patagonian firmware specialist, broke Argentina's voting machines in under 20 minutes (a police raid followed), built an X-ray device to cross air gaps, and confirmed a nation-state supply-chain firmware implant in an appliance maker's products. He refuses to sell, not from morals ("I don't think spying is bad") but survival: "like being a physicist in the 1930s with the atomic bomb. You'd get killed." Perlroth's hotel safe was opened and her loaner laptop repositioned, a warning shot she answered by trashing the machine. On closing night she watched a Gulf-state buyer and a thirty-something Argentine hacker close a deal in a Palermo club as David Byrne played "here comes the twister."

*Generational split and flipped map.* Older hackers lived through the Dirty War (30,000 desaparecidos) and distrust governments; millennials, with instant gratification and untaxed cash, sell freely ("no more loyalty"). Kissinger green-lit the junta (1976), so to Argentines the US is the country that "bombed another into oblivion" and Iran an ally.

*Geer's plan.* Dan Geer's idea: the US outbids everyone 10x, uses the bug, then patches it. Perlroth, standing in Buenos Aires: too late.

**Evidence.** 30,000 desaparecidos; ICPC wins shifted to Russia, Poland, China, Iran; post-Snowden NSA "epidemic" of departures; $1M contracts lost.

**Key terms.** Atado con alambre; depth erosion (US engineers "skim the surface"); weigh the bags; hackers as "the new nuclear physicists" (but deterrence fails: no fissile material, low barrier, fast escalation).

**So what.** Constraint forces mastery; capability is globally distributed and cannot be recalled; the US can't conscript or outbid its way back; brokers invest in relationships.

## Chapter 18: Perfect Storm

**Central argument.** Stuxnet's boomerang arrived: every US cyber adversary came for America in the decade after, each learning from the last. The US was devastating on offense but nearly defenseless at home, with lobbyists blocking even voluntary infrastructure standards.

**Full stories.** (The notes' escalation ladder.)
1. 2012: the US/Israel wipe Iran's oil-network data.
2. Aug 15, 2012: Iran plagiarizes that code into Shamoon and wipes 30,000 Saudi Aramco computers on the "Night of Power," leaving a burning-American-flag image; it tried and failed to cross from business to production systems ("a weapon that could create as much havoc as 9/11 or Pearl Harbor" - Panetta). Iran's budget rose from $76M to $1B+ post-Stuxnet, for roughly the cost of three F-35s.
3. Weeks later: Iranian bank DDoS (BofA, JPMorgan, Citi, NYSE, ~4 dozen banks; 70+ Gbps vs banks' 40 Gbps), the longest-running cyberattack in internet history; DHS was "toothless," victims bore the cost.
4. 2012 infrastructure bill: McConnell, "if we were attacked, we would lose"; Chamber of Commerce lobbyists gutted mandatory standards to voluntary, and even those were filibustered.
5. Aug 2013: the 3 a.m. Bowman Dam call. Officials nearly retaliated against Iran for a supposed attack on a 245-foot Oregon dam; it was a 20-foot New York flood-control dam with sluice gates disconnected for maintenance. "The first assessment is almost always wrong."
6. China: Perlroth, Barboza, Sanger and Mandiant traced thousands of attacks (Coca-Cola, RSA, Lockheed) to PLA Unit 61398 in a 12-story Shanghai tower; DOJ indicted five soldiers (symbolic). Ali Abbasi, a young Iranian hacker funded by China's 863 program, hacked a grid controller onstage in 5 seconds.
7. Feb 2014 Sands: Iran wiped Adelson's casinos ("Don't let your tongue cut your throat"). Dec 2014 Sony: North Korea ("Guardians of Peace") wiped 70% of Sony's computers over The Interview and dumped emails, a "playbook" for the later election hack.
8. 2015 deals: the Iran nuclear deal ended destructive attacks; the Xi-Obama commercial-IP agreement cut Chinese industrial attacks about 90% for about 18 months, until Trump's trade war and stealthier attacks resumed.

**Evidence.** Numbers above. Legion Yankee later (RSA/Lockheed).

**Key terms.** Plagiarized-weapon boomerang; asymmetric retaliation; glass houses (the US couldn't ban hacking government agencies like OPM because the NSA does it, so it drew the line at commercial IP); Shamoon; Bowman Dam.

**So what.** Deploying a cyberweapon is free R&D for your adversary; cyber is the great equalizer; negotiate only norms you will keep; verify attribution before retaliating; offense without hardened defense leaves private companies holding the bag; deals pause attacks but restraint evaporates when the deal does.

## Chapter 19: The Grid

**Central argument.** Russia's Sandworm (GRU Unit 74455) spent years embedding in critical infrastructure, and on December 23, 2015 took 230,000 Ukrainians offline in the first confirmed cyberattack to black out a grid: a mutually-assured-destruction signal that Russia could do the same to a more connected, more dependent and more in-denial United States.

**Full stories.**
*Energetic Bear.* CrowdStrike's name for the earlier (2010+) Russian grid campaign: Russian-language artifacts, Moscow work hours, watering-hole and man-in-the-middle attacks on 1,000+ firms in 84 countries. Russia Trojanized industrial-control software updates to reach dams, nuclear plants, pipelines and the grid, "not there to collect intelligence on the price of gas" (Hultquist).

*Sandworm.* iSight (John Hultquist) named it for Dune references in the code; BlackEnergy began as Cr4sh's $40 DDoS tool and evolved into espionage/sabotage malware; KillDisk wipes and cleans up. Targets included GE Cimplicity (.cim/.bcl), Siemens and Advantech software. BlackEnergy arrived one harmless-looking module at a time across months. A timely phishing lure (a fake list of "Kremlin sympathizers" or a Ukrainian court decision) delivered a Microsoft zero-day; in April Starlight Media's lawyer clicked an Excel file, becoming "Patient Zero," and by October 200 machines were infected. Attackers rehearsed different techniques on media companies (Starlight, TRK), using KillDisk to wipe evidence: "the scientific method in action." Hultquist told the Pentagon a month before: "they're going to turn off the lights."

*December 23, 2015.* Sandworm hijacked SCADA at Prykarpattyaoblenergo and two other utilities; an invisible cursor opened 30 substations' breakers, changed the operator's password, killed emergency phone lines and backup power; 230,000 people were dark for about 6 hours. The point was the message: "We can torch you." Russia embedded in the US grid as it moved "little green men" into Crimea. Kaspersky grid-hacking contests showed how trivially Russian substations fall; Russia had pushed, and been rebuffed on, a cyberweapons treaty after Stuxnet.

*US weakness.* In four months of early 2013 DHS's top four cyber officials resigned; NSF scholarship recipients chose NSA over DHS; analysts leaked to the NYT as a last resort; 198 critical-infrastructure incidents in 2012 (+52%). The grid has local distributors regulated state-by-state, no federal security standards and legacy unpatched software built "for access, not security." A 2010 bipartisan letter (Schlesinger, Perry, Woolsey, Deutch, Hadley, McFarlane) warned of outages "for at least months to two years"; McCain led the lobbyist-backed opposition that killed the bill. Two decades of FUD marketing, a "blood sport," dulled warnings. Left of boom: Watters's iSight doctrine.

**Evidence.** 230,000; 30 substations; ~6 hours; 200 machines; 198 incidents; 1,000+ firms/84 countries; $40.

**Key terms.** Preparing the battlefield; staged-assembly implant; MAD for the internet; left of boom; FUD.

**So what.** "Die/turnoff" targeting means attack preparation; staged malware defeats signature detection; adversaries rehearse on soft proxies; starving the defensive agency leaves the home front exposed.

## Chapter 20: The Russians Are Coming

**Central argument.** Two threads converge: America's threadbare cyber defense (a printout emergency plan and a VEP that always tilted toward hoarding zero-days like EternalBlue) and Russia's 2016 election interference, an information-warfare and hacking campaign that exploited American divisions and media appetite.

**Full stories.**
*The printout defense and VEP.* The White House's cyber emergency plan was literally a printout ("Hour 0... Notify... may take days or weeks"). The VEP weighs keeping vs disclosing: widespread technology favors disclosure, adversary-only use favors withholding, subject to periodic review. With vast offense budgets it "always weighs more heavily on hoarding" ("there's blood left on the table" - Daniel). EternalBlue (nicknamed "EternalBluescreen" for crashing targets until perfected; "not dirty" with minimal logging; "we knew it could be a weapon of mass destruction") was withheld seven years and never seriously considered for disclosure; Daniel came to regret it. "You can no longer cut a hole in something without poking a hole in security for everyone."

*Heartbleed.* "Given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow" failed: OpenSSL secured millions of systems but was maintained by one engineer on $2,000 a year.

*The IRA.* Prigozhin's St. Petersburg troll farm (Translator Project): in 2014 Krylova and Bogacheva scouted nine "purple states"; fake communities (Heart of Texas vs United Muslims of America) staged real-world standoffs; Black Lives Matter/"Woke Blacks" pages suppressed votes; reach 126M Facebook users, 288M Twitter impressions.

*The hacks.* Cozy Bear (SVR) and Fancy Bear (GRU) were both inside the DNC. Fancy Bear phished John Podesta via a fake Google password alert; the aide's "This is a legitimate email" (a typo for illegitimate) yielded 60,000 emails. The material was laundered through Guccifer 2.0 (metadata in Cyrillic, "Iron Felix" username, Google-Translate Romanian), DCLeaks (registered months earlier) and WikiLeaks, timed for maximum damage (pre-convention DNC/Sanders emails; pre-election Podesta/Wall Street speeches) and amplified by Gawker, Smoking Gun and mainstream outlets. Russia scanned all 50 states' voter rolls, breached Arizona and Illinois, and hacked VR Systems (e-pollbooks). Experts judged the goal was not necessarily a Trump win but chaos: bruise Clinton and doubt the election's legitimacy; disenfranchising voters in blue counties of purple states beats hacking machines.

*The response.* McConnell refused a bipartisan attribution statement; Trump amplified WikiLeaks ("400-pound hacker"); Obama's December sanctions and 35 expelled "diplomats" were "barely a spanking." NYT editors buried the DNC story as "cyberattacks had become the overplayed soundtrack of our lives."

**Evidence.** 7 years; $2,000/year; 60,000 emails; 126M/288M; 35 diplomats.

**Key terms.** VEP; kompromat; IRA; Cozy/Fancy Bear; Guccifer 2.0; DCLeaks; Heartbleed.

**So what.** Formal disclosure processes still tilt to hoarding when offense is better funded; shared technology makes every withheld exploit a liability to your own side; open source ubiquity is not security; information warfare amplifies real divisions and the media's appetite is attack surface; partisan paralysis and symbolic sanctions aren't deterrence.

## Chapter 21: The Shadow Brokers

**Central argument.** The nightmare the VEP was supposed to prevent came true: an anonymous group stole and dumped the NSA's own zero-day arsenal, including wormable EternalBlue. The debate over whether adversaries might independently discover the NSA's bugs became moot; someone simply handed the crown-jewel cyberweapons to the world for free.

**Full stories.**
*The auction.* In Aug 2016 the Twitter account @shadowbrokerss auctioned "Equation Group" (Kaspersky's name for TAO) tools, released 300 MB samples (Epicbanana, Egregiousblunder, etc.), then a "better than Stuxnet" file, demanding up to 1M Bitcoin, laced with anti-"elites" screeds. Jake Williams, ex-TAO (2008-2013), recognized the tools' authenticity; the Cisco and Fortinet zero-days on firewalls securing American networks forced emergency patching; ex-TAO analysts scrambled to protect clients suddenly exposed to their own government's tools.

*The timeline.* Aug 2016 auction; Halloween "Trick or Treat?" (a map of NSA decoy servers, taunting "DirtyGrandpa" Biden, #hackelection2016); direct sales; "going dark"; when the US struck Syria and Biden threatened retaliation for the DNC, the group escalated by outing Williams with true NSA code words (OddJob, CCI, Q Group), proving insider depth; Williams felt his government didn't "have his back." April 2017: password reveal and a final dump of about 20 exploits including EternalBlue and DoublePulsar. EternalBlue was "point-and-shoot," barely detectable; the NSA quietly tipped Microsoft a month before the dump (MS17-010), attribution blank. Infections hit 400,000 machines within two weeks.

*Chasing the wrong people.* Hal Martin, an NSA contractor with 50 TB of stolen data, was a hoarder not the leaker. Vault7 (a CIA tool leak, attributed to Joshua Schulte, mistrial on the leak charge) was separate. The Kaspersky angle: Israeli intelligence, having hacked Kaspersky, showed the US that Kaspersky antivirus was pulling Top Secret files; an NSA employee's home PC running Kaspersky may have been the vector: "spies hacking spies hacking spies."

*Attribution.* The mock-Russian English read like a native English speaker faking Russian; Snowden: "somebody is sending a message" and retaliating for the DNC "could get messy fast."

**Evidence.** 300 MB; 1M Bitcoin; ~20 exploits; 400,000 in two weeks; Cisco zero-days worth five figures each on the market, but hundreds of millions in damage after the dump.

**Key terms.** Shadow Brokers; Equation Group; EternalBlue; DoublePulsar; Vault7; capabilities vs descriptions; false flag.

**So what.** A stockpile is a single point of catastrophic failure; losing working code is far worse than losing descriptions ("the keys to the kingdom"); NOBUS was a fiction (the tools hit bugs anyone could find); wormable exploits arm every future attacker; last-second patching helps little.

## Chapter 22: The Attacks

**Central argument.** Within weeks in 2017, EternalBlue powered two of history's most destructive cyberattacks, North Korea's WannaCry and Russia's NotPetya. The US government deflected responsibility for losing its own weapons, prompting Microsoft's Brad Smith to demand a Digital Geneva Convention.

**Full stories.**
*WannaCry (May 12, 2017).* North Korea's Lazarus group hit 200,000 organizations in 150 countries in 24 hours, including about 50 UK hospitals, Renault, Telefónica and FedEx, via EternalBlue. It was botched: no working decryption (netting under $200K) and a built-in kill switch that 22-year-old Marcus Hutchins triggered for about $11 ("no good deed goes unpunished": he was later arrested by US feds). Bossert refused to own the loss: the tools were ours but we're not responsible for how others use them. Inglis on North Korea: "low cost of entry, asymmetrical, anonymous, holds infrastructure at risk, a source of income... one of the most successful cyber programs on the planet" despite being unsophisticated.

*NotPetya (June 2017).* Six weeks before launch, the GRU breached Linkos Group, maker of M.E.Doc, Ukraine's mandatory tax software ("Ukraine's TurboTax"), and Trojanized a routine update, believing it would confine the blast to Ukraine. On Constitution Day the payload fired: fake ransomware with irreversible encryption. EternalBlue and EternalRomance spread it laterally, MimiKatz harvested credentials. Any multinational with even one remote Ukrainian employee was infected: Merck, Maersk, FedEx/TNT, Mondelez, DLA Piper, Reckitt Benckiser, Cadbury Tasmania, Chernobyl's radiation monitors, and Russia's own Rosneft. $10B+ in damage; insurers refused to pay Merck and Mondelez, classifying it as an act of war. The White House named North Korea quickly but Trump shielded Putin: Bossert's NotPetya op-ed blaming Russia was killed to avoid angering Putin.

*Patch reality.* MS17-010 (March 2017) existed, but critical infrastructure runs legacy XP, patches need approval and rare maintenance windows, and automated patching is a "no-no."

*Brad Smith.* His UN Geneva speech (Nov 9, 2017) invoked the 1949 Geneva Conventions: no peacetime attacks on civilians, hospitals, the grid or elections; no IP theft; mutual response. "Stockpiling of vulnerabilities by governments is such a problem... a wake-up call." The irony: as he spoke, Pentagon hackers were planting logic bombs in the Russian grid.

**Evidence.** 200,000/150/24 hrs; <$200K; $11; $10B+; 400,000 (Ch 21).

**Key terms.** WannaCry; NotPetya; M.E.Doc; Linkos Group; MimiKatz; EternalRomance; Digital Geneva Convention; war exemption.

**So what.** A leaked exploit is a permanent upgrade to every adversary; cyber is cheap for the weak; irreversible "ransomware" is a wiper; supply-chain attacks have no borders; patch exists is not patched; accountability dodges and selective attribution undermine deterrence.

## Chapter 23: The Backyard

**Central argument.** The NSA's leaked exploits came home to American towns, cities, hospitals and nuclear plants. With restraining deals abandoned and the "Russia hoax" war on, every adversary escalated, leaving the country "one step from taking everything down."

**Full stories.**
*Eternal.* EternalBlue-powered ransomware crippled Allentown, a San Antonio jail and Baltimore ($18M cleanup). The NSA deflected via wordsmithing (Rob Joyce) and a "91% disclosed" statistic, meaningless without denominators. Rogers posed the Toyota question; Microsoft's Burt: "That analogy presupposes exploits have any benefit to society... It's already a bomb." "Eternal is the perfect name": no longer a zero-day, ubiquitous because towns run legacy software. Rogers: "our lawyers would never have let us get away with that."

*China.* Legion Amber stole the NSA's exploits off its own systems about a year before the Shadow Brokers leaked them, using them against Five Eyes and to steal nuclear-propulsion technology. A Uighur watering-hole attack used 14 iOS zero-days hidden in plain sight for two years (found by Project Zero), plus parallel Android/Tibetan campaigns, capabilities worth $2M+ used broadly. "The Chinese use their best tools against their own people first because that's who they're most afraid of. Then they turn those tools on us" (Lewis).

*Iran and the Gulf.* After the nuclear-deal withdrawal Iran made 2,700 hits on Trump's 2020 campaign and a Proud Boys voter-intimidation spoof; the US's own Nitro Zeus had planted logic bombs in Iran's grid; escalation after the Suleimani strike. Crowdfense (Saudi/Emirati front) offered $3M for iPhone exploits; Twitter's Saudi-spy engineers; the Bezos WhatsApp hack (same exploit sold to Crowdfense); Khashoggi's murder went unpunished.

*Russia.* Russia sat inside Wolf Creek (Kansas) and Cooper (Nebraska) nuclear plants, mapping for a future attack; the Petro Rabigh (Saudi) attack switched off safety locks, the last step before an explosion: "All that's missing is some political motivation" (Chien). Pax mafiosa: criminals like TrickBot and Evil Corp (whose leader "was FSB") enjoy untouchable status in exchange for national service.

*2020.* "Defend forward" (Nakasone): Cyber Command planted malware in the Russian grid, disrupted TrickBot and the IRA; Microsoft took legal action on TrickBot; CISA (Krebs, "rumor control") zipped up states; Iran was attributed fast. Election Day was "just another Tuesday on the internet." But the perception hack, a "rigged election" narrative, came from within: "Moscow Mitch" blocked election-security bills, Ratcliffe twisted intelligence, Trump undermined confidence. Disinformation was amplification: "Americans, and our president, do it for them"; "our candidate is chaos." Perlroth's verdict: not a Cyber Pearl Harbor but "death by a thousand hacks."

**Evidence.** $18M; 14 zero-days; two years; 2,700; $3M; $2M+; 91% statistic.

**Key terms.** Legion Amber; Nitro Zeus; Crowdfense; pax mafiosa; defend forward; perception hack; watering hole.

**So what.** NOBUS is dead twice (stolen and bought); paying ransoms funds the threat; coordinated action can blunt interference; the gravest interference came from within.

## Chapter 24: Epilogue

**Central argument.** The internet was built at a biker bar in 1976 with zero thought to security, and that original sin has metastasized into a decade-long plague. The prescription is the hacker mindset: work up the stack, from code to architecture to humans to elections to policy.

**Full stories.**
*Origin.* SRI's 1976 first internetworked message from a bread truck at a biker bar ("The Zott's"); Dave Retz: "Everything can be intercepted... everything is vulnerable." SRI's radios had already bled into SFO's air-traffic radar in 1974. "We were just trying to get the thing working."

*The up-the-stack plan.* (1) Code: stop shipping glaring bugs; Microsoft's 2002 Trustworthy Computing turned it from punchline to security leader (a Windows zero-day now costs about $1M); "Move slowly and fix your shit" against "move fast and break things"; security from inception; defense in depth. Open source: 80-90% of modern software; Heartbleed's OpenSSL had one underfunded engineer; fix with a Linux Foundation/Harvard census, bounties, a "cybersecurity bill of materials," developer certification ("driver's license for programmers"), MFA for maintainers. (2) Architecture: "Build it like it's broken" (Casey Ellis); compartmentalize crown jewels (Apple sandboxing); CHERI (DARPA/SRI/Cambridge) adds "contamination chambers," with Arm, Microsoft and Google exploring it despite performance fears. (3) Humans: 98% of attacks start with phishing; password spraying is "insanely effective"; MFA "right now"; patch promptly (peak exploitation is the day a patch drops); cyber and media literacy in schools. (4) Elections: never online (no online voting platform has survived expert hacking, "lunacy"); protect registration databases though the data is public; paper trails. (5) Policy: reinstate the national cybersecurity coordinator (eliminated 2018); mandate basic infrastructure standards (no expired software, pen tests, no default passwords, MFA, air-gapping); Norway/Japan models (most-digitized = safest, via national strategies). VEP reform: civilian agencies (HHS, Transportation), oversight moved from NSA to DHS with IG/PCLOB audits, expiration dates, take credit for disclosures (GCHQ's BlueKeep), close the NDA loophole for purchased zero-days, pass the stalled PATCH Act. EternalBlue by every VEP criterion should have been disclosed years earlier: "fishing with dynamite." (6) Market: as biggest buyer the US could require exclusive rights + patching from any broker and refuse abuser-selling vendors; set red lines (hospitals, elections, airplanes, nuclear facilities) even without a Digital Geneva Convention.

*Obstacle.* The post-Snowden trust deficit: states (especially red ones) and companies resist federal help and threat-sharing. Ignorance is the greatest vulnerability; governments rely on public confusion to keep hoarding. "The cost of doing nothing now outweighs the burden of doing something."

**Evidence.** 98%; 80-90%; $1M; average zero-day life "about a year (RAND)" (conflicts with Ch 10's 7 years; see 3f); EternalBlue held 5+ years.

**Key terms.** Left of boom; "Move slowly and fix your shit"; Build it like it's broken; CHERI; PATCH Act; zero-day expiration dates; red lines.

**So what.** Design assuming compromise; MFA and fast patching beat exotic zero-days; rebalance national policy from hoarding to defense; this book is Perlroth's own left-of-boom effort.

# PART 3 - Key learnings

## 3a. Core concepts and frameworks

| Framework | Definition | Use it when | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Zero-day** | A flaw the vendor doesn't know about, hence unpatched ("God mode") | Assessing any exposure to unpatched software | Treating zero-days as the main attack route; 98% of attacks are phishing/credentials |
| **Access is king; weaponization is the moat** | Finding a bug is easy; a reliable, silent, clean-fail exploit is what is valuable | Pricing or ranking offensive capability | Valuing raw bugs like finished tools |
| **Secrecy as precondition of value** | A bug's worth collapses when public | Understanding why the trade resists transparency | Expecting self-reporting or transparency from sellers |
| **Offense-defense price gap** | Governments pay far more (Chrome about 3x) | Designing defensive incentives | Trying to match offense dollar for dollar |
| **NOBUS** | "Nobody But Us" rationale for hoarding | Evaluating any "only we can use it" claim | Assuming exclusivity lasts; adversaries find or buy equivalents |
| **Intravenous principle** | Any implant can swap payload from spying to destruction | Judging "surveillance-only" tools | Treating espionage implants as harmless |
| **Using a weapon teaches the target** | Deployment is free adversary R&D | Cost-benefit of offensive use | Ignoring the copy-and-return effect (Shamoon) |
| **Precision is not containment** | Targeted payloads can ride promiscuous spreaders | Designing or assessing cyber operations | Equating legal target limits with propagation limits |
| **Hack the endpoint** | Capture data before/after encryption on the device | Evaluating any security architecture | Believing encryption alone suffices |
| **VEP** | Weigh prevalence x reliance x severity to hold or disclose | Stockpile debates | Letting offense-funded process hoard indefinitely |
| **Fog of Peace** | Benign alerts hide the real attack | Alert triage | Assuming "probably an intern" |
| **Name and shame** | Public attribution breaks the silence norm | Responding to nation-state intrusion | Staying silent to protect stock price |
| **Compete on non-money motives** | Recognition, intellectual happiness, beta scope | Bounty design | Bidding against offense for the same bug |
| **Pre-positioning vs espionage** | "Die/turnoff" commands signal attack prep | Reading intrusions in control systems | Treating "no data stolen" as "no harm" |
| **Perception hack** | Small attacks that erode confidence in legitimacy | Election defense | Counting only altered votes |
| **Patch exists is not patched** | Legacy and maintenance windows leave exposure | Vulnerability management | Assuming disclosure equals protection |
| **Build it like it's broken** | Assume compromise; compartmentalize crown jewels | System design | Relying on a perimeter moat |
| **Glass houses** | States ban what they don't do | Norm negotiation | Demanding norms you violate |

## 3b. The big ideas of the book (12)

1. **The US created and feeds the zero-day market.** Government buyers paid for silence and outbid vendors from the start (Ch 3-5). *Appears:* Ch 3, 4, 5, 10, 12, 17.
2. **Secrecy is both the value and the accountability gap.** A bug is valuable only while hidden, so NDAs, omertà and "the salmon" block oversight. *Appears:* Ch 2, 4, 12.
3. **Encryption is beaten at the endpoint, not broken.** From Gunman typewriters through Muscular to Pegasus, attackers capture data before or after encryption. *Appears:* Ch 1, 6, 13, 16.
4. **Shared technology makes every hoarded exploit a hole in your own defenses.** There are no patents on vulnerabilities. *Appears:* Ch 8, 10, 20, 21, 23.
5. **Every espionage implant is a latent weapon.** The same access can deliver destruction (intravenous principle; Stuxnet; Sandworm). *Appears:* Ch 8, 9, 19.
6. **Weapons escape and teach.** Stuxnet's escape and Shamoon show deployment is free adversary R&D. *Appears:* Ch 9, 17, 18.
7. **NOBUS is dead.** Adversaries independently find, steal (Legion Amber, Shadow Brokers) or buy (Gulf) your tools. *Appears:* Ch 10, 21, 23.
8. **Markets cannot self-regulate on ethics.** Eren, Desautels and NSO ethics committees all failed against money and middlemen. *Appears:* Ch 11, 12, 13.
9. **Defense must compete on incentives, not dollars.** Bounties, Project Zero and name-and-shame are the tech industry's answer. *Appears:* Ch 14, 15, 16.
10. **The home front is the weak point.** Offense was lionized and defense (DHS) starved; infrastructure standards were killed by lobbyists. *Appears:* Ch 0, 18, 19, 23.
11. **Information warfare amplifies and delegitimizes.** The IRA exploited real divisions, and by 2020 the greatest threat to confidence was internal. *Appears:* Ch 20, 23.
12. **The human layer decides most attacks.** 98% start with phishing/credentials; MFA, long passwords and fast patching have the highest ROI. *Appears:* Ch 15, 24.
13. **Attribution is spoofable and first assessments are usually wrong.** Verify before retaliating (Bowman; Guccifer; Shadow Brokers). *Appears:* Ch 18, 20, 21.
14. **The danger is a plague, not a single blow.** "Death by a thousand hacks," not Cyber Pearl Harbor; build assuming compromise. *Appears:* Ch 0, 23, 24.

## 3d. Anti-patterns to avoid
1. Hoarding widely used zero-days indefinitely (EternalBlue, "fishing with dynamite").
2. Believing NOBUS; assuming exclusivity.
3. Suing or ignoring the people who find your flaws.
4. Self-certified ethics in place of law (Eren, Desautels, ethics committees).
5. Taking a vendor's word on large-system security (beyond a few thousand lines).
6. Dismissing anomalies as benign (Fog of Peace).
7. Retaliating on a first attribution (Bowman Dam).
8. Offense without hardened defense; starving the defensive agency.
9. Firewall-moat-only security; leaving internal links unencrypted.
10. Backdoors "for the good guys only."
11. Online voting; failing to protect public registration data.
12. Paying ransoms.
13. Deflecting accountability for lost weapons; selective attribution.
14. Automating away the moral pause (Genie, Turbine).
15. Move fast and break things.
16. Ignoring a neighbor's blackouts.
17. Letting corporate principle erode as a market opens.
18. Dismissing a rival's treaty offer as a ploy.

## 3f. Open questions and limits the notes flag
- **Scope and currency:** journalistic narrative reported through about 2020; not an exploit-development manual or operational-security advice; some figures are estimates or contested (Perlroth flags this repeatedly); verify current tools, prices and actors.
- **US-centric vantage:** the moral calculus looks different elsewhere (Ch 17).
- **Conflicting numbers in the notes:**
  - RAND zero-day lifespan: about 7 years average with about 25% found in 18 months, earlier studies about 10 months (Ch 10, cheatsheet) versus "about a year" (Ch 24, patterns). This file reports both and does not resolve them.
  - EternalBlue's hold time: 7 years (Ch 20, 21), "5+ years" (Ch 24), "5-7 years" (cheatsheet).
  - NotPetya's biggest victims: Merck and FedEx about $1B (Prologue) versus Merck + Mondelez over $1B (cheatsheet); consistent only in order of magnitude.
  - Offense vs defense: roughly 100 to 1 (Prologue, unspecified measure) versus 2 to 1 on budget (Ch 10).
  - Stuxnet: "seven zero-days" is the headline figure, yet one spread mechanism was documented publicly (Hakin9), so the count is looser than it sounds. Also Ch 9's caption "2,000 of 8,700 by early 2010" versus Ch 2's "roughly a fifth."
  - Windows exploit price: $100K-$250K (Ch 12) versus about $1M (Ch 24); different eras.
- **Unresolved items:** the identity of the Natanz USB carrier; whether the Shadow Brokers were an insider or a Russian breach (investigators were unsure); the exact origin of the Stuxnet escape (a leading theory, not proof); the Nimda "R.P. China" puzzle; whether Google-style defense can scale ("a fool's errand" most firms decline).
- **Structural questions the book leaves open:** how to build norms (Digital Geneva Convention vs red lines) while the US itself plants logic bombs (Ch 22-23); whether NSA-chaired VEP reform can happen without the stalled PATCH Act; how to restore post-Snowden trust between states, companies and federal agencies.
