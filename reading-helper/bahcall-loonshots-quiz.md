# Loonshots Quiz Book
*Loonshots: How to Nurture the Crazy Ideas That Win Wars, Cure Diseases, and Transform Industries* by Safi Bahcall

**How to use:** Cover the answers, answer from memory (out loud or in writing), then check. Anything you miss is a section to reread. Each chapter has short-answer (SA), true/false (T/F), and multiple-choice (MC) questions. A cumulative final exam is at the end. Built from the `bahcall-loonshots` skill notes, not the book's own wording.

## Chapter 1 (Introduction): Structure, Not Culture

**Q1 (SA).** What is the difference between a loonshot and a moonshot?
**A:** A moonshot is an ambitious, expensive, widely celebrated goal (a destination). A loonshot is a neglected project, widely dismissed, whose champion is written off as unhinged. Nurturing loonshots is how you reach moonshots.

**Q2 (SA).** The book's central claim is "structure, not culture." What does it mean?
**A:** Innovation is an emergent property of group structure. Teams that suddenly stop innovating (Nokia, Merck, Disney after *The Lion King*) did not change their people or culture; they crossed a phase transition triggered by a control parameter such as group size.

**Q3 (MC).** In the organizational "tug-of-war" that drives a phase transition, the two competing forces are:
a) creativity vs. discipline  b) stake in project outcomes vs. the perks of rank  c) artists vs. soldiers  d) cost vs. quality
**A:** b. Small groups: stake dominates. As size grows, rank perks rise and stake falls; when they cross, the system snaps and starts rejecting loonshots.

**Q4 (SA).** What did Phil Anderson's motto "more is different" mean here?
**A:** The whole is not just more than, but different from, the sum of its parts. You cannot explain ice by studying one molecule, or a team by studying one person.

**Q5 (T/F).** The goal is for an innovative organization to be entirely in the loonshot phase. **A:** False. No group can be fully in both phases at once, except at the edge of a transition. The goal is to hold the edge where both phases coexist, like water at 32°F.

**Q6 (SA).** Why does the book call "culture advice" like yelling at ice, and what is the alternative image?
**A:** One molecule yelling at its neighbors cannot unfreeze a block of ice. Structure is like salt on the sidewalk: a small additive that shifts the freezing point.

**Q7 (SA).** What is "post-hoc culture extraction" and why is it flawed?
**A:** Picking winners by stock price and reverse-engineering "lessons" from them (the Amgen example, where the real lesson was "pick up the check and hire good lawyers"). Victors rewrite a twisted path into a straight line, like reading the socks of a lottery winner.

## Chapter 2: How Loonshots Won a War

**Q1 (SA).** State Bush-Vail Rule 1 and its two parts (plus the third part added in Ch 4).
**A:** Separate the phases: (a) separate your artists and soldiers; (b) tailor the tools to the phase (wide spans, loose controls, creative metrics for loonshot groups; narrow spans, tight controls, quantitative metrics for franchise groups); (c) watch your blind side by seeding both P- and S-types (introduced in Ch 4).

**Q2 (SA).** State Bush-Vail Rule 2 and its parts.
**A:** Create dynamic equilibrium: love your artists and soldiers equally, and manage the transfer, not the technology. The weak link is never the supply of ideas but the transfer to the field and the feedback back.

**Q3 (MC).** In the four-quadrant map, "separated but no healthy transfer" is called:
a) stagnation  b) chaos  c) the Trap  d) the Bush-Vail target
**A:** c. Stagnation = no separation; chaos = everyone told to innovate; the target = separated and exchanging.

**Q4 (SA).** What happened with early airborne radar, and what did it show about field feedback?
**A:** Pilots ignored it. The problem was not the technology but a switch-fiddling interface. The fix (the PPI display) came only because Bush forced pilots to explain why to the scientists.

**Q5 (T/F).** Vannevar Bush's key contribution was a technical breakthrough in radar. **A:** False. Bush "made no technical contribution whatever"; his job was the touch and balance between the two groups. As a leader, be a gardener, not a Moses.

**Q6 (SA).** What did Bush's structural fix in 1940 consist of, and what result did microwave radar produce?
**A:** A civilian agency reporting directly to the President, quarantined in anonymous MIT buildings (separation), with Bush personally pushing the technology to battlefield leaders (transfer). Within ~30 months microwave radar cut Allied shipping losses by 95% and forced the U-boat withdrawal.

**Q7 (SA).** Why did Jobs 1.0 calling the Apple II team "bozos" matter?
**A:** It illustrates belittling one side. Artists favor artists and soldiers favor soldiers; a leader must genuinely respect both or one side leaves (Wozniak quit and the Mac launch failed).

## Chapter 3: The Surprising Fragility of the Loonshot

**Q1 (SA).** What are the Three Deaths?
**A:** Sir James Black: "It's not a good drug unless it's been killed at least three times." Nearly every breakthrough is ridiculed, defunded, or abandoned at least three times before it succeeds.

**Q2 (SA).** What is a False Fail, and what are the three triage questions?
**A:** A negative result caused by a flaw in the test, not the idea. Ask: (1) Is the negative outcome due to the idea or the experiment? (2) What would you have to believe for it to be a flaw in the test? (3) How could you evaluate that cheaply?

**Q3 (MC).** Which is NOT cited as a False Fail example?
a) statins in rats  b) the dog "cancer" signal  c) Friendster's crashes  d) Polavision's failure
**A:** d. Polavision was a genuine commercial failure (Ch 5). The others were test or execution flaws (rats carry mostly HDL; ultra-high doses; crashes rather than a fad).

**Q4 (SA).** What does LSC stand for and why "with curiosity"?
**A:** Listen to the Suck with Curiosity: investigate rejection rather than defend against it. Acknowledgment is not enough; you must probe beneath the surface for the mechanism. Anger is stubbornness; curiosity is persistence.

**Q5 (SA).** Why separate the inventor and the champion?
**A:** They are different skill sets. Most inventors cannot or will not promote their own work. Champions are bilingual (artist-speak and soldier-speak), like Deak Parsons, who dragged radar out of neglect "with the persistence of a door-to-door salesman."

**Q6 (SA).** Walk through the three deaths of Akira Endo's statin.
**A:** (1) "Diet-heart" had been declared dead and almost nobody attended his talk. (2) No effect in rats; he tested on hens (LDL like humans) and cholesterol fell by nearly half. (3) A dog safety study "showed cancer"; Sankyo killed the program after Endo had left and Merck terminated too, until Brown and Goldstein proved a false positive and pressured a restart. Mevacor was approved in 1987.

**Q7 (T/F).** Beecham gave up on statins after the rat result and so forfeited a share of enormous value. **A:** True (a share of $300 billion in the notes): the classic cost of killing on the first animal-model failure.

## Chapter 4: The Two Types of Loonshots: Trippe vs. Crandall

**Q1 (SA).** Define P-type and S-type loonshots with their "tells."
**A:** P-type: product/technology; "There's no way that could ever work." S-type: strategy/business model/new application of an existing product; "There's no way that could ever make money."

**Q2 (MC).** Compared with P-types, S-types are:
a) faster to kill you  b) easier to copy  c) dimmer, slower to kill you, and harder to copy  d) rarer in great ideas
**A:** c.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the "dangerous virtuous cycle"?
**A:** P-type loonshots feed a franchise, which funds more P-types; momentum builds tunnel vision (bigger, faster, more) until the wheel turns one too many times.

**Q4 (SA).** What S-type loonshots did Crandall and the startups use against Pan Am?
**A:** Two-tier pay (A-scale/B-scale), the Sabre reservation system with yield management, and hub-and-spoke, secondary airports, and 20-minute turnarounds.

**Q5 (SA).** What is an "S-type moment"?
**A:** A sudden shock (airline deregulation in 1978, Android given away free) that acts like a solar eclipse, letting the faint light of S-type loonshots show and annihilating the unprepared.

**Q6 (SA).** How did IBM win the P-type battle and lose the strategy war?
**A:** It won the PC but missed that customers cared about the software standard and the chip, not the box, handing about $1.5 trillion of value to Microsoft and Intel.

**Q7 (T/F).** Juan Trippe's skill at P-type deals (the jet-age poker game) was unrelated to Pan Am's fall. **A:** False. The same P-type instinct that won the jet age bought jumbo jets after deregulation while Crandall's unglamorous strategy won. Pan Am lost money in 18 of its last 22 years and ceased operations in 1991.

## Chapter 5: Edwin Land and the Moses Trap

**Q1 (SA).** Define the Moses Trap and give its three stages.
**A:** An all-powerful leader anoints the holy loonshot by decree, for love of loonshots rather than strength of strategy. Stages: (1) the dangerous virtuous cycle builds momentum; (2) franchise blinders harden (only loonshots that spin the existing wheel are seen); (3) Moses grows all-powerful and anoints by decree.

**Q2 (SA).** What was Polavision and why did it fail?
**A:** Land's 1977 instant-movie system: technically miraculous, commercially dead within a year ($2,500 camera, $30 per 3-minute cassette, not erasable). $200M write-off; Land was forced out.

**Q3 (MC).** Land's failure with digital was primarily:
a) not understanding the technology  b) a failure to see its S-type economics  c) lack of lab talent  d) lack of funding
**A:** b. He pushed CCD spy satellites (KH-11) in 1971 yet dismissed digital for Polaroid because "there's no film; there's no way that can make money."

**Q4 (T/F).** A leader who is both CEO and head of research is a structural strength. **A:** False. It is a structural warning sign: judge-and-jury leadership.

**Q5 (SA).** What does "The bottom line's in heaven" represent?
**A:** Land's answer to an analyst; the Moses creed of launching for scientific and aesthetic appeal over commercial significance.

**Q6 (SA).** How was Land's first synthetic polarizer connected to the False Fail idea?
**A:** At 19 he found herapathite crystals in an obsolete-words dictionary entry; the graveyard of unexplained experiments is a good place to find a False Fail.

**Q7 (SA).** Why does a walled-off brilliant lab not save the company?
**A:** It sits in the bottom-right quadrant: it postpones decline but does not prevent it. Polaroid's digital camera arrived in 1996; bankruptcy followed in 2001.

## Chapter 6: Escaping the Moses Trap

**Q1 (SA).** Describe the three levels of Rule 3 (system mindset).
**A:** Level 0: don't analyze failures. Level 1 (outcome mindset): ask why the project failed. Level 2 (system mindset): ask how the decision was made (who, what data, framing, incentives) and change the process.

**Q2 (T/F).** Rule 3 says to analyze failures only. **A:** False. Analyze wins too: good decisions can have bad outcomes and vice versa; failing to probe wins reinforces a bad process.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the PARC Trap and how does it differ from the Moses Trap?
**A:** PARC: phase separation works brilliantly but loonshots stay parked and never leave (transfer under-forced; "Color is not part of the office of the future"; Kodak burying its 1975 digital camera). Moses over-forces the transfer by decree.

**Q4 (MC).** What did Jobs learn from Ed Catmull at Pixar?
a) to micromanage creatives  b) to mind the system rather than manage the project  c) to avoid S-types  d) to build more hardware
**A:** b.

**Q5 (SA).** Why do creatives need peer feedback, and what are examples?
**A:** Artists accept feedback from thoughtful peers, not suits. Examples: Pixar's director braintrust and Genentech's scientists responding to scientists. Jobs was kept out of early film feedback.

**Q6 (SA).** What S-type moves did Apple 2.0 use?
**A:** The iTunes store and the closed ecosystem. Jobs paid $100M+ to kill Mac clones while near bankruptcy.

**Q7 (SA).** What did Xerox Texas illustrate about bonus structures?
**A:** Bonuses forced the franchise to sandbag the loonshot: it couldn't make typewriter numbers and launch the Alto.

## Chapter 7: Interlude + Phase Transitions I

**Q1 (SA).** What are the two things to know about phase transitions?
**A:** (1) At the heart of each is a tug-of-war between two competing forces. (2) They are triggered when small shifts in a system property (density, temperature) change the balance between those forces.

**Q2 (SA).** What does "simple, but not simplistic" mean?
**A:** Keep just enough detail to extract general insight with confidence in its micro origins. Too little explains nothing; too much (137 case studies) also explains nothing.

**Q3 (SA).** What is the "Yellowstone effect" and its organizational analogue?
**A:** Suppressing every small fire lets fuel build past the threshold until one catastrophic fire is inevitable (1988 burned 36% of the park). Organizationally, suppressing every small failure invites one catastrophic one.

**Q4 (MC).** A power-law tail (twice as many 10-acre fires as 20-acre) signals:
a) a stable system  b) a system near a transition  c) random noise  d) an efficient market
**A:** b.

**Q5 (SA).** What does percolation theory describe?
**A:** Whether a connected path exists through random pores, trees, or people. Below a critical density fires die out; above it they engulf everything. It also applies to epidemics, networks, and rock fracture.

**Q6 (T/F).** Small-world networks are fragile to random failure but robust to attacks on hubs. **A:** False, reversed. They are robust to random failure but fragile to attacks on the most influential nodes.

**Q7 (SA).** In the traffic phase diagram, what are the two forces, the two control parameters, and three ways to restore smooth flow?
**A:** Forces: driver wants cruising speed vs. brakes to avoid the bumper ahead. Parameters: car separation and average speed. Fixes: lower speed limits in heavy traffic, meter on-ramps, ban truck overtaking.

## Chapter 8: Phase Transitions II: The Magic Number 150

**Q1 (SA).** What is the Invisible Axe?
**A:** The collective behavior above the threshold in which everyone individually favors innovation yet the group rationally rejects loonshots. Politics beats projects.

**Q2 (SA).** Write the magic number equation and define each variable.
**A:** M = E · S² · F / G. E = equity fraction; S = management span; F = organizational fitness (project–skill fit ÷ return-on-politics); G = salary step-up per promotion.

**Q3 (SA).** Compute M for E = 50%, S = 6, F = 1, G = 12%.
**A:** 0.5 × 36 × 1 / 0.12 = 150.

**Q4 (T/F).** Dunbar's number is correct in observation and in theory. **A:** False. Right observation (Brigham Young's companies averaged 150; Gore builds a new plant at 150 parking spaces), wrong theory (extrapolating from monkey brain size).

**Q5 (MC).** Politics appears first in:
a) the executive suite  b) the lowest professional-manager layer  c) the research lab  d) the board
**A:** b.

**Q6 (SA).** Why does the angled transition line matter?
**A:** A group past 150 can restore loonshot focus by adjusting structure instead of shrinking. The number is not universal, like solids melting at different temperatures.

**Q7 (SA).** In the Pfizer thought experiment, why is dismissing the loonshot rational at 100,000 people?
**A:** A 1-in-10 loonshot adds about 1% to revenue after seven years with 99,999 colleagues claiming credit or blame, while belittling it and proposing a modest franchise step could bring a 30% raise next year. In a 50-person firm with equity, the loonshot wins.

## Chapter 9: The Fourth Rule: Raise the Magic Number

**Q1 (SA).** List the six levers of Rule 4.
**A:** (1) Reduce return-on-politics; (2) use soft equity; (3) increase project–skill fit; (4) fix the middle; (5) bring a gun to a knife fight (chief incentives officer); (6) fine-tune the spans.

**Q2 (SA).** How do DARPA and McKinsey reduce return-on-politics?
**A:** DARPA: no career ladder; program managers serve 2–4 year terms with expiring badges. McKinsey: important promotions are evaluated by a senior partner from a distant office or practice who interviews up to two dozen colleagues and clients over about three months.

**Q3 (MC).** Which span suits a loonshot group?
a) 5 or fewer  b) 6  c) 15+  d) always the same as franchise groups
**A:** c. Wide spans give loose controls and peer problem-solving (Coughran at Google, 180 reports). Narrow spans (≤5) suit franchise groups.

**Q4 (SA).** Explain the Dead Sea Scrolls test.
**A:** Before launching a reward, ask what a rational actor would do to maximize the metric. Paying per fragment led shepherds to tear scrolls into scraps. Ford Pinto's "Goals Gone Wild" is another example.

**Q5 (SA).** Why is the "middle" dangerous and how do you fix it?
**A:** Far from board oversight, evaluations are complex, and bonuses are one promotion away. Steep grant curves (100% bonuses at top, <10% in the middle) turn it into *Survivor*. Flatten them, celebrate results not rank, and cut rank perks.

**Q6 (T/F).** Wage dispersion is associated with higher productivity. **A:** False. Research finds it lowers productivity and cooperation and raises turnover; a big G is bad.

**Q7 (SA).** How did MIT win the Red Balloon Challenge?
**A:** A recursive payout ($2,000 to the spotter, $1,000 to the recruiter, $500 to that person's recruiter, and so on) gave everyone a stake. 4,400 people recruited in 36 hours; all ten balloons found in 8h 52m. Georgia Tech, using charity, recruited 1,400.

## Chapter 10: Why the World Speaks English

**Q1 (SA).** What are the three conditions of a loonshot nursery?
**A:** Phase separation, dynamic equilibrium, and critical mass.

**Q2 (SA).** How many 10%-odds loonshots give a good chance of at least one hit?
**A:** Ten give 65%; two dozen give 92%.

**Q3 (SA).** State the Needham Question.
**A:** Why did the Scientific Revolution happen in Europe when China led in paper, printing, compass, gunpowder, canals, literacy, and fleet size?

**Q4 (MC).** Song China had:
a) all three conditions  b) critical mass but neither separation nor exchange  c) only separation  d) none
**A:** b. The emperor personally directed astronomy and could end it by decree.

**Q5 (SA).** Contrast Tycho and Shen Kuo.
**A:** Equal talent, equal loss of royal patronage. Tycho hopped to Prague (and hired Kepler); Shen had nowhere to go, since private astronomy was illegal, and spent his last decade under house arrest.

**Q6 (T/F).** Inside a Major, a killed project stays dead; in a nursery it can survive on the next lily pad. **A:** True (immunotherapy survived in small biotechs after every big pharma passed).

**Q7 (SA).** What does the Haiti vs. Dominican Republic example show?
**A:** That adoption questions differ from first-appearance questions. Same island rules out culture, climate, and geography, so institutions explain the difference.

## Chapter 11 (Afterword): Loonshots vs. Disruption

**Q1 (SA).** How do "loonshot" and "disruptive" differ?
**A:** Loonshot is a real-time label about what leaders believe; disruptive (Christensen) is a hindsight label about effect on a market. They are independent axes, "like height and hair color."

**Q2 (T/F).** The transistor and PageRank were both disruptive from the start. **A:** False. Both were sustaining: the transistor was a better switch from the largest company in the country ($20 vs. $1 for a tube, sold first to high-end military buyers); PageRank gave incrementally better results than 17 other search engines.

**Q3 (SA).** What does "leaf in the tornado" mean?
**A:** Early-stage projects in fast-moving markets end up somewhere no one predicted, so classifying them by eventual market effect in advance is impossible.

**Q4 (MC).** In a pharma CEO's survey, how many recent launches landed within 2× of the forecast?
a) 20 of 20  b) 10 of 20  c) 5 of 20  d) 0 of 20
**A:** d.

**Q5 (SA).** Give the two market-guess examples.
**A:** Interferon, the viral "magic bullet," became a $6B multiple-sclerosis franchise; Amgen's EPO, a tiny kidney-disease niche, became a $100B company.

**Q6 (SA).** Why is IKEA a good test of the two lenses?
**A:** The disruption lens narrates (true, but useless to Kamprad in 1953). The loonshot lens shows each move challenged a belief (warehouse in the countryside, customers assembling furniture, sourcing in Poland), all S-types forced by a blockade from competitors.

# Cumulative Final Exam (10 Questions)

**F1 (SA).** Why does the book say innovation is a matter of structure and not culture?
**A:** Teams flip phases when a control parameter (chiefly size) crosses a threshold, driven by the tug-of-war between stake and rank. Small changes in structure can melt steel; one molecule yelling cannot.

**F2 (SA).** List the four Bush-Vail rules.
**A:** (1) Separate the phases; (2) create dynamic equilibrium; (3) spread a system mindset; (4) raise the magic number.

**F3 (MC).** Which pair correctly maps trap to cause?
a) Moses: under-forced transfer; PARC: over-forced  b) Moses: over-forced transfer by decree; PARC: under-forced, ideas parked  c) both lack separation  d) both are chaos
**A:** b.

**F4 (SA).** What is the difference between a P-type and S-type loonshot, and how did each relate to Pan Am and Polaroid?
**A:** P: "won't work"; S: "won't make money." Pan Am died of S-types (Crandall's pay, reservations, hubs); Polaroid's blind side was digital's S-type economics inside a P-type (no film, no money).

**F5 (T/F).** A negative test result means the idea is flawed. **A:** False. It may be a False Fail (statins in rats, Friendster crashes, the Comet jets). Run the triage before killing.

**F6 (SA).** Compute M for S = 10, E = 50%, F = 1, G = 33%, and say what the result implies.
**A:** 0.5 × 100 / 0.33 ≈ 150. Wider modern spans with aggressive step-ups land at the same number.

**F7 (SA).** Name three levers for raising the magic number with an example of each.
**A:** Reduce return-on-politics (DARPA's expiring badges, McKinsey's distant evaluators); soft equity (DARPA visibility and autonomy, open innovation like Red Balloon); fine-tune spans (Coughran with 180 reports vs. franchise groups at ≤5). Others: project–skill fit, fix the middle, chief incentives officer.

**F8 (SA).** What are the three nursery conditions and how does the framework explain Europe vs. China?
**A:** Phase separation, dynamic equilibrium, critical mass. China had critical mass but no separation or exchange; Europe's fragmented states allowed rejected ideas and scientists (Copernicus, Tycho) to find another patron.

**F9 (SA).** How should you steer in real time, and why not by forecasts?
**A:** By the belief an idea challenges (P: won't work; S: won't pay). Forecasts for loonshots are reliably wrong (0 of 20 within 2×), so fund portfolios and test cheaply.

**F10 (SA).** What do Level 2 analysis and SRT each ask of you?
**A:** Level 2 (system mindset): how did we decide and what in the process should change, including after wins. SRT (spirit, relationships, time) are the three "glass balls" a loonshot champion must not drop, per the skill's patterns file.
