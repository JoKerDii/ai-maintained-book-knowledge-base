# Loonshots Quiz Book

*Based on "Loonshots" by Safi Bahcall. **Source note:** this file reflects the skill's chapter notes (the `bahcall-loonshots` skill: SKILL.md, glossary, patterns, cheatsheet and the eleven chapter files), NOT the book's own wording. Anything the notes are silent on is deliberately left out.*

## How to use this quiz

1. Work one chapter section at a time. Cover the answers (they sit under each question as "**A:**") and write or say your answer first.
2. Question types are mixed: short answer (SA), true/false (T/F), multiple choice (MC), explain-in-your-own-words (EXPLAIN) and scenario/application (SCENARIO). Scenario questions are where the frameworks earn their keep: name the concept, then say why it applies.
3. Score yourself honestly: full credit only if you give the idea and the reason. Anything you miss, reread that chapter's section in the companion file `bahcall-loonshots-arguments-and-evidence.md`.
4. Re-do the chapter sections after 1 day and 1 week. Finish with the 20-question cumulative final exam at the end.
5. The book has 11 chapters (introduction, nine chapters, afterword), so each chapter gets its own section of 8-10 questions.

# Chapter 1 - Introduction: Structure, Not Culture

**Q1 (SA).** Which three teams does the chapter use as examples of groups that suddenly stopped innovating, and what is the proposed explanation?
**A:** Nokia, Merck, and Disney after *The Lion King*. The explanation is that their people and culture did not change; they crossed a phase transition. Innovation is an emergent property of group structure, so the remedy is to change structure, not culture.

**Q2 (SA).** Define "loonshot" and "moonshot" and say how they relate.
**A:** A moonshot is an ambitious, expensive, widely celebrated goal, a destination. A loonshot is a neglected project, widely dismissed, whose champion is written off as unhinged. Nurturing loonshots is how you reach moonshots.

**Q3 (T/F).** "A project that leadership already loves needs a loonshot nursery to protect it."
**A:** False. If leadership already loves it, it is a moonshot and needs no special protection. A project being laughed at is the one that needs a nursery.

**Q4 (MC).** The two "phases of organization" are most like which physical analogy? (a) hot and cold, (b) liquid and solid, (c) fast and slow, (d) light and dark.
**A:** (b). Being good at loonshots (original films) and being good at franchises (sequels) are distinct phases of group behavior, like liquid and solid. A group cannot be in both phases at once, except at the edge of a transition.

**Q5 (SA).** What is the "organizational tug-of-war" that drives the phase transition, and how does group size affect it?
**A:** Stake in project outcomes versus the perks of rank. In small groups stake dominates. As size grows, rank perks rise and stake falls; when they cross, the system snaps and starts rejecting loonshots. (In water the analogous forces are binding energy and entropy.)

**Q6 (EXPLAIN).** In your own words, what does Phil Anderson's phrase "more is different" mean and why does it matter for innovation?
**A:** The whole is not just more than the sum of its parts but different from it. You cannot explain ice by studying one molecule, or a team by studying one person. So asking whether individuals are "innovative" misses the point; the behavior belongs to the group's structure.

**Q7 (SA).** What is a control parameter? Give two examples from the notes.
**A:** The dial whose small change triggers a phase transition. Examples: temperature, density, team size, equity fraction.

**Q8 (EXPLAIN).** The chapter says "culture" advice is like yelling at ice to loosen up, while structure is like salt on the sidewalk. Explain the contrast.
**A:** One molecule shouting at its neighbors cannot unfreeze a block of ice, just as a leader's sermon cannot change group behavior. Salt is a small additive that shifts the freezing point, as a small structural change shifts the point at which a group flips phase. "Small changes in structure can melt steel; one molecule yelling at its neighbors cannot."

**Q9 (SCENARIO).** A CEO sees that an acquired startup team, once bold, has become cautious and political after three years of growth. The board wants to hire a culture consultant. Which concept from the chapter applies, and what would you ask instead?
**A:** Phase transition / structure over culture. Ask "which phase is this structure in, and what control parameter (size, equity fraction, etc.) put it there?" rather than "are these people innovative?". The same people behave as risk-takers in a startup and project-killers in a big company.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A journalist proposes to study the most successful drug company by stock price and extract its "culture of innovation". What error does the chapter warn about, and what example does it use?
**A:** Post-hoc culture extraction, or revisionist history: picking winners by outcome and reverse-engineering lessons, like reading the socks of a lottery winner. The notes' Amgen example: the real lesson was "pick up the check and hire good lawyers." Ask instead for the Three Deaths behind any success.

**Q11 (SA).** What was Miller's piranha and what does it teach?
**A:** Ibrutinib, an idea ridiculed by every large pharma, whose champion was fired by his own board, later bought for $21 billion. Use it as the template question for any dismissed idea: "What would have to be true for the critics to be wrong?"

**Q12 (EXPLAIN).** What question motivates the whole book, according to Bahcall's biotech story?
**A:** Not "how do we get more ideas?" but "why do good teams, with excellent people and the best intentions, kill great ideas, and what structural change stops it?" The motivation: a physicist-CEO at a first patient's bedside with no more sarcoma chemotherapy options than 40 years earlier, and at his father's leukemia bedside with no new drugs. Dozens of candidates sat buried in labs, rejected by groups able to develop them.

# Chapter 2 - How Loonshots Won a War: Life on the Edge

**Q1 (SA).** Who are the two heroes of the chapter and what organizations did they run?
**A:** Vannevar Bush (OSRD, the wartime science agency) and Theodore Vail (Bell Labs). Both rescued stagnant franchises by building a structure, not by championing any one idea.

**Q2 (SA).** State Bush-Vail Rule 1 and its two sub-parts (plus the one added in Ch 4).
**A:** Separate the phases: (a) separate your artists and soldiers; (b) tailor the tools to the phase. Chapter 4 adds (c) watch your blind side.

**Q3 (MC).** Which fits a *loonshot* group under "tailor the tools to the phase"? (a) narrow spans, tight controls, rigid quantitative metrics; (b) wide spans, loose controls, flexible/creative metrics; (c) narrow spans, loose controls; (d) wide spans, tight controls.
**A:** (b). Narrow/tight/quantitative is the franchise recipe. The notes' cautionary example: 3M under a Six Sigma CEO, where innovation plunged.

**Q4 (SA).** State Rule 2 and its two sub-parts.
**A:** Create dynamic equilibrium: (a) love your artists and soldiers equally; (b) manage the transfer, not the technology.

**Q5 (T/F).** "In Bush's setup, the weak link was the supply of new ideas."
**A:** False. The weak link is never the supply of ideas but the transfer to the field and the feedback back from it. Bush "made no technical contribution whatever"; his job was the touch and balance between groups.

**Q6 (EXPLAIN).** Why is "draw a box on the org chart and rent a building" necessary but not sufficient?
**A:** It achieves separation (Rule 1) only. Without exchange the group becomes a shiny research lab that ships nothing (a long list of failed companies) or lands in one of the traps. Both rules are required.

**Q7 (SA).** Walk through the radar story: what went wrong before Bush and what structural fix did he apply, with outcome?
**A:** Two Navy radio engineers proposed radar in 1922 after a passing ship doubled their signal; the Navy ignored it, rejected a $5,000 request in 1930 because results "might take two or three years", took five years to assign one full-time person, and was still field-testing on the morning of Pearl Harbor. In 1940 Bush created a civilian agency reporting to the President, quarantined in anonymous MIT buildings (separation), and personally flew to battlefield HQ and arranged for the Secretary of War to ride an experimental radar plane so generals would look (transfer). Within ~30 months microwave radar cut Allied shipping losses by 95% and forced the U-boat withdrawal. Same Navy, same engineers, different structure.

**Q8 (SA).** What do the Jobs 1.0 "bozos" episode and the PPI display each illustrate?
**A:** "Bozos" (Apple II team) illustrates failure of Rule 2a: belittling one side leads to exits (Wozniak quits, Mac launch fails). The PPI display illustrates the field feedback loop: early airborne radar was ignored by pilots because of a switch-fiddling UI problem, not a core technology flaw; the fix came only because Bush forced pilots to explain why to scientists.

**Q9 (EXPLAIN).** What does "gardener, not a Moses" mean?
**A:** The leader tends the transfer and the balance between phases, intervening with a gentle hand only when the link breaks, rather than anointing ideas by decree. (Moses is the over-forcing failure, covered in Ch 5.)

**Q10 (SA).** Why could Bush "bump heads"?
**A:** Explicit top cover: FDR said "you go ahead and bump, and I will back you up." Takeaway: get top cover before you start bumping heads.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** A lab at a manufacturer produces patents and prototypes but nothing reaches customers, and engineers call the sales division "damn bureaucrats". Which rule is failing and what do you do?
**A:** Rule 2, dynamic equilibrium: the weak link is transfer and feedback, and the nicknames show one side is unloved (compare "regular Navy," "damn professors," "bozos"). Manage the transfer rather than the technology, show equal love, and secure top cover for bumping heads.

**Q12 (SCENARIO).** A CEO announces "everyone must innovate; the CEO is our Chief Innovation Officer", and imposes creativity targets on the whole firm, including the call center. Which quadrant is this and what is the fix?
**A:** Top-left, chaos. Sometimes you need the phone operator to answer the phone. The fix is separation: let soldiers be soldiers and build a distinct nursery for loonshots, with tools tailored to each phase.

**Q13 (MC).** In the four-quadrant map, which quadrant is "separated and exchanging"? (a) bottom-left, (b) top-left, (c) bottom-right, (d) top-right.
**A:** (d), the Bush-Vail target. Bottom-left is stagnation (no nursery), top-left chaos, bottom-right the Trap (separated, no transfer).

# Chapter 3 - The Surprising Fragility of the Loonshot

**Q1 (SA).** What is the "Three Deaths" rule and who said it?
**A:** "It's not a good drug unless it's been killed at least three times" (Sir James Black). Nearly every breakthrough is ridiculed, defunded or abandoned, often more than three times.

**Q2 (SA).** Give the timelines the notes cite for the Three Deaths.
**A:** Endo (statins) 16 years; Black's beta-blockers 7; Folkman 32.

**Q3 (SA).** Define a False Fail and give its three-question check.
**A:** A negative result caused by a flaw in the test, not the idea. Ask: (1) is the negative due to the idea or the experiment? (2) what would you have to believe for it to be a flaw in the test? (3) how could you evaluate that hypothesis cheaply?

**Q4 (SA).** Name four examples of False Fails from the chapter.
**A:** Statins in rats (rats carry mostly HDL, so LDL-lowering drugs show nothing); the dog "cancer" signal (an artifact of ultra-high doses); Friendster (users left because of crashes, not because social networks were fads); the Comet jet explosions (metal fatigue from a window design, not jets per se).

**Q5 (EXPLAIN).** What does LSC stand for, and why is "with curiosity" essential?
**A:** Listen to the Suck with Curiosity. When attacked, override the urge to defend and investigate why the investor declined, the partner walked, or the customer chose a rival. Active-listening-style acknowledgment is not enough; you must probe beneath the surface for the mechanism.

**Q6 (T/F).** "Anger in response to rejection is a sign of persistence."
**A:** False. Anger is stubbornness; investigation is persistence. The chapter's line: "I find it's when I question the least that I need to worry the most."

**Q7 (EXPLAIN).** Why must the inventor and the champion be treated as two different jobs?
**A:** They require different skills; most inventors cannot or will not promote their work. The champion is "bilingual", fluent in artist-speak and soldier-speak (Deak Parsons, the Navy liaison who dragged radar out of neglect "with the persistence of a door-to-door salesman"). Authority must be elevated even though soldiers see no product and artists find promotion distasteful. If one person holds both roles (Endo, Folkman), plan for departure: Sankyo's program collapsed after Endo retired.

**Q8 (SA).** Trace Endo's three deaths.
**A:** Death 1: "diet-heart" had been declared dead and almost nobody attended his mevastatin talk. Death 2: no effect in rats; he carried a resignation letter, pleaded for time, and after a bar conversation with a colleague about to eat his research hens, tested on chickens without formal approval; hens have LDL like humans and cholesterol fell by nearly half. Death 3: after a first patient's cholesterol dropped 30% and a 12-hospital study launched, a dog safety study "showed cancer" and Sankyo killed the program after Endo left; Merck (given his confidential data) terminated too until Brown and Goldstein proved the dog result a false positive and pressured a restart. Mevacor was approved in 1987; statins now prevent about 500,000 US heart attacks and strokes a year.

**Q9 (SA).** What did Beecham lose by quitting on the rat result, and what does that illustrate?
**A:** It forfeited a share of $300 billion. It illustrates the anti-pattern of killing on the first animal-model failure.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A startup's social app loses users after launch and the board wants to call social networking a fad. What does the chapter suggest?
**A:** Run False-Fail triage. Friendster's users left because of crashes; Thiel and Howery extracted a contrarian answer with confidence from retention data. Check cheaply whether the test (the user drop) reflects the idea or an execution flaw.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** A researcher's drug candidate is rejected by an investor. Her colleagues console her. What does the chapter recommend?
**A:** Go to the rejecter and ask why (LSC) rather than turning to friends for reassurance, which the chapter lists as an anti-pattern.

**Q12 (SA).** What are "Spouse Activation Factor" and "counting the arrows"?
**A:** SAF is Folkman's term for the support that kept him in research. "You can tell a leader by counting the number of arrows in his ass" is Folkman's line about leaders' scars.

# Chapter 4 - The Two Types of Loonshots: Trippe vs. Crandall

**Q1 (SA).** Define P-type and S-type loonshots and the "tell" phrase for each.
**A:** P-type: a new product or technology no one thinks will work ("There's no way that could ever work"). S-type: a new strategy, business model or application of an existing product, with no new technology ("There's no way that could ever make money").

**Q2 (T/F).** "Deaths from S-types are quick and dramatic; deaths from P-types are slow."
**A:** False, reversed. P-type deaths are quick and dramatic (streaming kills rentals); S-type deaths are slow and hard to diagnose even in hindsight (Walmart took three decades).

**Q3 (SA).** What is "watch your blind side"?
**A:** Rule 1c: make sure the nursery seeds the type of loonshot you are least comfortable with. Audit your pipeline by type; if one column is empty, fund the other deliberately.

**Q4 (SA).** Give an example of a great idea needing both types.
**A:** Google: a P-type ranking algorithm plus S-type advertising strategies.

**Q5 (EXPLAIN).** What is the Dangerous Virtuous Cycle?
**A:** P-type loonshots feed a franchise, which funds more P-types; momentum builds tunnel vision (bigger, faster, more) until the wheel turns one time too many. It is dangerous precisely because it works.

**Q6 (SA).** Describe Trippe's jet-age poker game.
**A:** Boeing wouldn't redesign the 707 for nonstop transatlantic range; Douglas wouldn't build a jet; Pratt & Whitney wouldn't release its experimental high-compression engine. Trippe opened talks with Rolls-Royce to spook Pratt, then ordered 120 engines directly (about $40M, four times Pan Am's annual income) with no planes to put them in. Holding the engines, he got Douglas to commit to the DC-8 to his design, ordered 20 inferior 707s from Boeing, and timed both announcements for the same *Wall Street Journal* morning. Boeing's president "felt like an earthquake victim" and conceded.

**Q7 (SA).** What happened to Pan Am?
**A:** After deregulation, Trippe's instinct bought jumbo jets (the 747 order, then eight more, then a $100M terminal) while Crandall's unglamorous reservation system and pay scales won the war. Pan Am lost money in 18 of its last 22 years and ceased operations in 1991.

**Q8 (SA).** Name Crandall's S-type moves.
**A:** Two-tier pay (A-scale/B-scale) to escape legacy labor contracts; Sabre reservation system given free to travel agents, giving placement advantage and, unexpectedly, the first big-data pricing engine (yield management, "thirty years before Big Data"). The startups added hub-and-spoke, secondary airports and 20-minute turnarounds.

**Q9 (MC).** What S-type did IBM miss? (a) the box, (b) the software standard and chip, (c) the printer, (d) the monitor.
**A:** (b). IBM won the P-type PC battle but missed that customers cared about the software standard and chip, handing $1.5 trillion of value to Microsoft and Intel.

**Q10 (EXPLAIN).** What are S-type moments, and what image does the chapter use?
**A:** Sudden shocks (airline deregulation in 1978, Android given away free) that act like a solar eclipse, letting the faint light of S-type loonshots show and annihilating the unprepared.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** A platform company suddenly makes its operating system free. How should a rival respond using this chapter?
**A:** Treat it as an S-type moment: ask "which S-types just became visible?" and inventory strategy loonshots immediately, rather than only racing another product.

**Q12 (SCENARIO).** A hardware firm with a 20-year string of product hits plans to bet more on a bigger version of its flagship after sales stall. What pattern, and what alternative?
**A:** Betting the franchise on one more P-type after the music stops (Pan Am's 747 order then more). Check for the turn too many, and seed S-types: ask what new income streams the next technology enables.

# Chapter 5 - Edwin Land and the Moses Trap

**Q1 (SA).** Define the Moses Trap and give its three stages.
**A:** When an all-powerful leader anoints the holy loonshot by decree, acting for love of loonshots rather than strength of strategy. Stages: (1) the dangerous virtuous cycle builds momentum; (2) the franchise blinders harden; (3) Moses grows all-powerful and anoints by decree, becoming "principal cheerleader".

**Q2 (MC).** Edwin Land's nursery satisfied which rule and failed which? (a) 1 and 2, (b) 1 not 2, (c) 2 not 1, (d) neither.
**A:** (b). A Nobel-caliber nursery (separation) but he stayed judge and jury of every idea (no real dynamic equilibrium). He even banned his own head of engineering from the ninth-floor lab.

**Q3 (SA).** What was Land's rule for choosing problems, and its danger?
**A:** "Manifestly important and nearly impossible". Powerful for P-types, dangerous when it becomes the only filter.

**Q4 (SA).** What happened with Polavision?
**A:** Instant movies in 1977: technically miraculous but commercially dead within a year ($2,500 camera, $30 per 3-minute cassette, not erasable). Land led it personally for ten years and half a billion dollars, launched it with mimes and dancers, and said "the bottom line's in heaven". $200M write-off; Land was forced out. Its epitaph: "much more scientific and aesthetic appeal than commercial significance."

**Q5 (EXPLAIN).** Land understood digital technology better than almost anyone (KH-11). Why did Polaroid still miss it?
**A:** He argued in April 1971 for digital CCD spy satellites, before Sony or Kodak started, and won against nearly the entire military leadership. But for Polaroid he dismissed it because "there's no film; there's no way that can make money." The failure was strategic (S-type), not technical: he never looked for the new income streams digital could enable.

**Q6 (SA).** Give Polaroid's franchise blinders in revenue terms.
**A:** Only loonshots that spun the instant-film wheel mattered, because cameras earned little and film cartridges earned a lot. Sales went from $1.5M to $1.4B across sepia prints (1947), black-and-white, auto-exposure, instant color (1963) and SX-70 (1972).

**Q7 (SA).** What was herapathite and why is it in a chapter on Moses?
**A:** At 19, Land found herapathite crystals in an obsolete-words dictionary entry and built the first synthetic polarizer. It is a textbook False Fail rescued from the "graveyard of unexplained experiments". It also began Polaroid (polarization, whose two-filter trick every LCD pixel still uses).

**Q8 (T/F).** "Polaroid entered digital cameras within a few years of Land foreseeing digital."
**A:** False. Polaroid's digital camera arrived in 1996 and bankruptcy followed in 2001 (the notes list this under the anti-pattern of waiting a decade to enter a category you foresaw).

**Q9 (SA).** Quote Land at the Polavision warehouse.
**A:** "I wanted you to see what hubris looks like", said in the warehouse of unsold Polavision machines.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A founder is both CEO and Director of Research, and no project ships without his personal blessing. Which concept applies and what do you advise?
**A:** The Moses Trap's stage 3; the dual title is a structural warning sign, not a strength. Install a transfer process where field intelligence flows back and is weighed on merit, and separate the roles.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** A company's R&D budget has gone to ever-better versions of the product that funds it for years, and the CEO asks the board to approve a launch because "the bottom line will follow". What do you check?
**A:** Stage 2 franchise blinders and the "one turn too many". Ask what new income streams the new thing enables and whether a structure exists that would surface commercial reality (Polavision's $2,500 camera and $30 cassette).

# Chapter 6 - Escaping the Moses Trap

**Q1 (SA).** State Rule 3 and the three levels of Kasparov's mindsets.
**A:** Spread a system mindset. Level 0: don't analyze failures. Level 1 (outcome mindset): ask why the project failed and resolve to work harder on that. Level 2 (system mindset): analyze the decision process (who, what data, how framed, what incentives) and change the process.

**Q2 (T/F).** "You should analyze losses but not wins."
**A:** False. Analyze wins too: good decisions can have bad outcomes (a 100:1 lottery with three tickets sold) and bad decisions good ones (the keeper slipped). Not probing wins reinforces a bad process.

**Q3 (SA).** What are the "Ugly Babies" and the "Beast"?
**A:** Catmull's metaphor: originality is fragile and ugly at first (ugly babies); the franchise Beast is a glutton but a necessary motivator. Keep them coexisting by balancing forces.

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** Why do creatives need peer feedback and how do Pixar and Genentech show it?
**A:** Artists accept feedback from thoughtful peers, not from suits. Pixar has a director "braintrust"; Genentech's scientists respond to scientists. The leader's presence is "gravity that crushes candor": Jobs was kept out of early film feedback and prefaced late comments with "I'm not a filmmaker; you can ignore everything I say."

**Q5 (SA).** Define the PARC Trap and give two illustrations.
**A:** Phase separation succeeds brilliantly but loonshots stay parked and never leave the lab; transfer is under-forced. "Color is not part of the office of the future"; Kodak burying its 1975 digital camera for a decade; Xerox Texas couldn't make typewriter numbers and launch the Alto (bonuses forced the franchise to sandbag the loonshot).

**Q6 (MC).** Where do Moses and PARC sit on the transfer dial? (a) both over-forced, (b) Moses over-forced, PARC under-forced, (c) Moses under-forced, PARC over-forced, (d) both under-forced.
**A:** (b). Aim for the gardener's touch between.

**Q7 (SA).** What went wrong at NeXT in system terms?
**A:** Outcome: the Cube was $10,000, optical drives slow, Businessland sold under 400 units. System: one person was financier, visionary and factory designer; the launch was modeled on Land's Polavision theater; press crowned Jobs the P-type genius, an incentive to be Moses; excluded information included staff and PC makers proposing a software-only strategy and Sun's data on $10,000-machine buyers. "We made a decision to risk our company" on optical drives showed love of loonshots over strength of strategy.

**Q8 (SA).** How did Apple 2.0 apply the rules?
**A:** Ive's studio sealed off (separation), Cook as heir (loving soldiers), iTunes store and closed ecosystem as S-types, and Jobs staying out of the nursery's candor. Jobs paid $100M+ to kill Mac clones while near bankruptcy; IBM's OS/2 failure had been a False Fail. Jobs: "The best innovation is sometimes the company, the way you organize."

**Q9 (EXPLAIN).** What does it mean that relinquishing control is not relinquishing attention to detail?
**A:** Art Levinson grilled junior technicians on data: ferocious precision is a creative-phase tool, tailored to the phase, not micromanaging of the idea.

**Q10 (SA).** What is a "synthesizer" in the chapter?
**A:** Newton and Jobs each assembled others' pieces (Hooke/Raskin) into a system, and each tried to bury the precursor.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** After a product flop a team lead says "the storyline was too predictable; we'll write better scripts." What mindset is this and what should he ask?
**A:** Outcome mindset (Level 1). Ask why the organization made its choices and how the decision process should change; consider a neutral outside mediator.

**Q12 (SCENARIO).** A company's research lab loves its work, but bonus plans for the production division penalize any time spent launching new products. Which trap, and what fix?
**A:** PARC Trap, with the incentive fault of Xerox Texas. Fix franchise incentives that force sandbagging and appoint champions.

# Chapter 7 - Interlude + Phase Transitions I

**Q1 (SA).** What are the "two things to know" about phase transitions?
**A:** (1) At the heart of each is a tug-of-war between two competing forces. (2) They are triggered when small shifts in a system property (density, temperature) change the balance between those forces.

**Q2 (EXPLAIN).** What is "simple, but not simplistic"?
**A:** A model that keeps just enough detail to extract macro insights with confidence in their micro origins ("describes the forest but is built from the trees"). Too little detail explains nothing; all detail (137 case studies) also explains nothing. It echoes Hemingway's Theory of Omission.

**Q3 (SA).** What is percolation theory and who started it?
**A:** The mathematics of whether a connected path exists through random pores, trees or people (Broadbent and Hammersley, 1957). Below a critical density fires die out; above it they engulf everything. It applies to gas masks, rock fracture, oil reservoirs, epidemics.

**Q4 (SA).** What is the Yellowstone effect and its numbers?
**A:** Suppressing every small fire lets fuel density build past the threshold; 1988 burned 36% of the park. Controlled and prescribed burns back the forest away from the line. The organizational version: no small failures, then one catastrophic one.

**Q5 (SA).** Walk through the traffic phase diagram.
**A:** Forces: wanting cruising speed vs. braking to avoid the car ahead. Axes: average car separation (vertical), average speed (horizontal). Stopping distance is about 6 car lengths at 30 mph and 30 at 80 mph, so the line slopes up-right. A brake tap on an empty road dies out; in dense traffic it doubles in delay per car (2, 4, 8, 16 s) into a jam. Fixes: lower speed limits in heavy traffic, meter on-ramps to cap density, ban truck overtaking.

**Q6 (T/F).** "Phantom jams were confirmed at the Nagoya Dome."
**A:** True: above a critical car density, jams appear with no cause.

**Q7 (SA).** What does a power-law tail signal and what is the terror-attack data point?
**A:** A system near a transition: event frequency falls in inverse proportion to size (twice as many 10-acre fires as 20-acre). Terror-attack casualties show an exponent of about 2.5 across nine conflicts.

**Q8 (SA).** What did Neil Johnson's study show?
**A:** He tracked 108,086 followers across 196 pro-ISIS pages; clusters grow, merge, split and dissolve like fire clusters, and their exponential growth predicts eruptions weeks ahead, without analyzing any individual.

**Q9 (EXPLAIN).** Why does the notes call small-world networks both robust and fragile?
**A:** Mostly local ties with occasional distant ones (Watts and Strogatz): robust to random failure, fragile to attacks on the most influential nodes.

**Q10 (EXPLAIN).** What is the lesson of Greenspan's "notably rare exceptions" and of the invisible hand?
**A:** The invisible hand is emergent, not fundamental (Smith's model was Boyle's emergent physics, not Newton's laws). Treating efficient markets as laws leaves bubbles as "anomalies"; the storms are what you most need to understand.

**Q11 (EXPLAIN).** Why does iron melt at 2,800 F, not 32 F, and what is the management moral?
**A:** Its binding energy is about 100x stronger. Multiple control parameters mean leverage: if you can't change size, change another dial (salt on the sidewalk).

**Q12 (SCENARIO).** A manager wants to predict which of her teams will fall into infighting by interviewing every individual. What does the chapter suggest?
**A:** Prefer analyzing clusters over individuals; collective behavior is not explained by individuals in isolation. Ask what the two forces are and what dial shifts their balance.

**Q13 (SCENARIO).** A regulator suppresses every minor bank failure. Which concept predicts what happens?
**A:** The Yellowstone effect: fuel builds until a catastrophic burn. Prescribed small burns back the system away from the line.

# Chapter 8 - Phase Transitions II: The Magic Number 150

**Q1 (SA).** Write the magic number equation and name each variable.
**A:** M = E x S^2 x F / G. E: equity fraction (hard and soft). S: management span. F: organizational fitness = project-skill fit / return-on-politics. G: salary step-up per promotion.

**Q2 (SA).** Compute M for E = 50%, S = 6, F = 1, G = 12%. Then for S = 10, G = 33%.
**A:** 0.5 x 36 x 1 / 0.12 = 150. And 0.5 x 100 / 0.33 is about 150 again.

**Q3 (EXPLAIN).** What is the Invisible Axe?
**A:** The collective behavior that emerges above the threshold: everyone individually favors innovation, yet the group rationally rejects loonshots. The cause is structure, not bad people.

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** Explain the "last-hour choice".
**A:** At 4 p.m. each employee chooses between work that raises project value and lobbying or networking for promotion. Structure determines which is rational.

**Q5 (SA).** Where is Dunbar's number right and where wrong?
**A:** Right observation (Brigham Young's companies averaged 150; Gore builds a new plant at 150 parking spaces), wrong theory (extrapolating monkey brain size). The magic number follows from four structural dials, so it can be raised.

**Q6 (T/F).** "The magic number is a universal constant."
**A:** False. Teams transform at different sizes, like solids melt at different temperatures. And the transition line is angled, so a group can restore the loonshot phase by changing structure rather than shrinking.

**Q7 (SA).** Define project-skill fit and return-on-politics.
**A:** Project-skill fit: how much an extra 10% of effort improves expected project value; low when under-matched or over-matched (bored). Return-on-politics: how much an extra 10% of lobbying improves promotion odds.

**Q8 (SA).** Which variable is squared and why does that matter?
**A:** S, span. It is the highest-leverage dial in the numerator. Wide spans mean fewer layers, rarer promotions and bigger per-person stake.

**Q9 (EXPLAIN).** Walk through the Pfizer thought experiment.
**A:** A middle manager at a 100,000-person, $50B company likes an early drug. Option 1, champion it: seven years of committee slogs, about 10% odds, a hit adds about $500M (1%), 99,999 colleagues claim credit and blame falls on you if it fails. Option 2, belittle it wittily and propose a modest franchise step: agrees with the boss, advertises judgment, plausible path to the boss's job next year (+30% salary, double influence). Rational answer: Option 2. At 50 people with meaningful equity and $0 revenue, a hit changes revenue infinitely and makes everyone a millionaire and hero, so Option 1 is rational. The crossover is M.

**Q10 (SA).** Where does politics appear first?
**A:** At the lowest professional-manager layer (lower-middle management), whose span of projects is smallest relative to the whole.

**Q11 (MC).** Which is hard equity? (a) peer recognition, (b) purpose, (c) options, (d) skill growth.
**A:** (c). Hard equity: options, restricted stock, at-risk bonuses. Soft equity is non-financial: recognition, purpose, skill growth.

**Q12 (SCENARIO).** A 400-person division is stuck in politics. The CEO proposes splitting into units of 100 and cutting each unit's equity. Applying M, what's wrong?
**A:** Shrinking is not the only route, and cutting E lowers M. Use the equation's dials (raise E including soft equity, widen S, raise F, lower G). The angled transition line means any size can restore the loonshot phase.

**Q13 (SCENARIO).** A company's promotions come with 33% raises and spans of 4. Which dials are hurting?
**A:** High G and low S (squared), both lowering M. Politics pays and promotions are frequent.

# Chapter 9 - The Fourth Rule: Raise the Magic Number

**Q1 (SA).** State Rule 4's six levers and the variable each acts on.
**A:** (1) Reduce return-on-politics (F). (2) Use soft equity (E). (3) Increase project-skill fit (F). (4) Fix the middle (raise E, lower G). (5) Bring a gun to a knife fight, a chief incentives officer. (6) Fine-tune the spans (S, in the right place).

**Q2 (SA).** How do DARPA and McKinsey reduce return-on-politics?
**A:** DARPA: no career ladder; program managers serve 2-4 year terms with badges printed with expiration dates; a roughly 200-person nursery inside a two-million-person organization. McKinsey: important promotions are evaluated by a senior partner from a distant office or practice who interviews up to two dozen colleagues and clients over about 3 months.

**Q3 (SA).** What are examples of open innovation?
**A:** Coors' cold-activated can, Kraft's melt-resistant chocolate, and the Red Balloon Challenge. They bring fresh ideas and peer recognition, and external peers are impartial judges.

**Q4 (SA).** Give the undermatch and overmatch examples.
**A:** Undermatch: Bahcall's toothpaste project. Overmatch: a bored Frank Lloyd Wright on a coffee machine ("idle hands are the devil's workshop"). Training raises fit twice: better output and desire to practice new skills instead of politicking.

**Q5 (EXPLAIN).** Why is the "dangerous middle" dangerous and what's the "Survivor" problem?
**A:** It is far from CEO/board oversight, evaluations are complex and bonuses are one step away. Steep equity-grant curves (100% bonuses at the top, under 10% in the middle) turn it into *Survivor*. Fix by celebrating results, not rank, cutting rank perks (parking, executive cafeterias, Hawaii "workshops"), and hunting perverse incentives.

**Q6 (SA).** Give the two perverse-incentive examples.
**A:** Dead Sea Scrolls paid per fragment, so shepherds tore scrolls into scraps; Ford Pinto's "under $2,000, under 2,000 lb" stretch goal left no time for safety ("Goals Gone Wild").

**Q7 (SA).** What is a free-rider bonus and what does research say about wage dispersion?
**A:** Paying junior staff on company earnings they cannot move. Wage dispersion lowers productivity and cooperation and raises turnover, so big G is bad.

**Q8 (SA).** What spans does the chapter recommend, with examples?
**A:** 15+ in loonshot groups (loose controls, more experiments, peer-to-peer problem solving): Bill Coughran at Google with 180 direct reports; Bob Taylor at PARC with 40-50. Narrow, 5 or fewer, in franchise groups (tight controls, fewer failures). "What's the right span?" is like "what's the right temperature for tea?"

**Q9 (EXPLAIN).** Tell the Red Balloon Challenge story and lesson.
**A:** A rotating Air Force test pilot pitched a joke: ten red weather balloons in ten undisclosed parks, $40,000 to the first team to find all ten; funded like any program manager. Georgia Tech, with a three-week head start, promised winnings to charity and recruited 1,400. MIT, which heard four days before, used a recursive payout ($2,000 to the spotter, $1,000 to the recruiter, $500 to theirs, and so on), recruited 4,400 people in 36 hours and found all ten in 8h 52m. A reward giving every link a stake beats altruism, and a structure with no ladder let a joke become a landmark experiment.

**Q10 (SA).** What does the chief incentives officer do and for whom?
**A:** A strategist in cognitive biases, hard and soft equity and perverse-incentive detection, not a rubber-stamp comp specialist. Small firms can hire part-time, like a fractional CFO.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** A firm launches a bonus per product launch. What test do you run?
**A:** The Dead Sea Scrolls test: what would a rational maximizer do (launch more, regardless of recalls)? The notes say to check recalls, failed trials, padded hours or unprofitable customers for launch, trial-start, hours and sales rewards.

**Q12 (SCENARIO).** A company applies one uniform span of 7 to R&D and manufacturing. Which anti-pattern and what do you change?
**A:** Uniform span policy across artists and soldiers. Widen R&D to 15+ with loose controls; narrow manufacturing to 5 or fewer with tight controls.

# Chapter 10 - Why the World Speaks English

**Q1 (SA).** What are the three conditions for a loonshot nursery?
**A:** Phase separation, dynamic equilibrium, critical mass. They apply inside a company, industry or civilization.

**Q2 (SA).** What are the critical mass numbers?
**A:** At 1-in-10 odds per loonshot, ten shots give a 65% chance of at least one hit; two dozen give 92%.

**Q3 (SA).** Describe industry-level phase separation in film and drugs.
**A:** Film (post-1948 *US v. Paramount*): Majors acquire and distribute franchises, hundreds of small production shops nurture loonshots, partnerships connect them. Drugs (post-genetic engineering): pharma Majors vs. hundreds of biotechs linked by deals (Roche-Genentech > $30B/yr).

**Q4 (EXPLAIN).** What is "loonshot life support" and the lily-pad image?
**A:** In a nursery, a killed project floats around until a new investor raises a hand (immunotherapy survived in small biotechs after every big pharma passed); inside a Major a killed project stays dead. Loonshots are frogs hopping lily pads; there must always be another pad.

**Q5 (SA).** State the Needham Question.
**A:** China led in paper, printing, compass, gunpowder, canals, literacy and fleet size, yet the Scientific Revolution happened in Europe. Why?

**Q6 (SA).** Tell the Kepler's eight minutes story.
**A:** An error of eight arc-minutes (under 0.05%) in Mars's position that he refused to ignore, abandoning 2,000 years of circular-orbit assumptions, a template for truth judged by measurement rather than authority.

**Q7 (SA).** Compare Tycho and Shen Kuo.
**A:** Equal talent, equal loss of royal patronage. Tycho hopped to Prague (and hired Kepler); Shen had nowhere to go (private astronomy was illegal) and spent his last decade under house arrest "with only my writing brush and ink slab to talk to." The Song emperor declared Shen's system "good enough" after seven years and fired his assistant.

**Q8 (MC).** Score Song China on the three conditions. (a) 3/3, (b) 2/3, (c) 1/3, (d) 0/3.
**A:** (c). Critical mass yes; no phase separation (the emperor directed astronomy and could end it by decree); no dynamic equilibrium. China met one of three and failed two (the chapter's worked example says "Two of three fails for China"; read that as fails on two of the three conditions).

**Q9 (EXPLAIN).** Why does the chapter separate first-appearance from adoption?
**A:** Needham's question is why science first appeared in Europe; separately, why some countries adopted it faster. Haiti vs. the Dominican Republic share an island, ruling out culture, climate and geography; institutions explain the difference.

**Q10 (SA).** What is the Royal Society chain from the notes?
**A:** Royal Society of London (1660), the first successful nursery inside one nation; Boyle's air pump, Papin's piston (buried in a cookbook on digesting bones), Newcomen's steam engine (1712), then the *Nemesis* off the Chinese coast.

**Q11 (T/F).** "Minors can never become Majors."
**A:** False, except in baseball by antitrust exemption: Disney, Amgen, England.

**Q12 (SCENARIO).** A city wants a biotech hub and plans a single large flagship firm with a big research budget. Using the chapter, what's missing?
**A:** Phase separation across firms, an exchange web and critical mass: Boston has 200+ biotechs plus VCs, vendors and acquirers in a self-feeding cycle, while a handful of firms (Detroit) do not ignite. Aim for roughly two dozen shots at 10% odds.

**Q13 (SCENARIO).** A national leader declares "we have nothing to learn from strange or ingenious objects." What is this a tell of?
**A:** The franchise-phase tell for nations (the Qianlong emperor), together with supreme-ruler veto (emperor as Moses).

# Chapter 11 - Afterword: Loonshots vs. Disruption

**Q1 (SA).** Distinguish a loonshot from a disruptive innovation.
**A:** Loonshot: an idea most leaders think won't work or won't matter, a real-time label about beliefs. Disruptive (Christensen): a low-end inferior entrant that improves until it takes the incumbent's best customers, a hindsight label about market effect.

**Q2 (T/F).** "P/S-type and disruptive/sustaining are two ends of the same axis."
**A:** False. They are independent axes, like height and hair color.

**Q3 (EXPLAIN).** What is the "leaf in the tornado"?
**A:** Early-stage projects in fast-moving markets end up somewhere no one predicted, so classifying them by eventual market effect in advance is impossible.

**Q4 (SA).** Why are the transistor and PageRank examples?
**A:** Both were sustaining. The transistor: a better switch from the largest company in the country, $20 vs $1 for a tube, sold first to high-end military buyers, later disrupted everything. PageRank: incrementally better results than the other 17 search engines.

**Q5 (SA).** Origins of Walmart and IKEA?
**A:** Walmart: Sam Walton wanted a big-city department store; his wife refused to live anywhere over 10,000 people and Bentonville offered four quail seasons. IKEA: showroom, flat-pack, self-service warehouse, own designers and Polish suppliers were desperate responses to being banned from trade fairs and cut off from designers and suppliers.

**Q6 (SA).** What do interferon and Amgen's EPO show?
**A:** Markets guess wrong in both directions: the "magic bullet" for viruses became a $6B multiple-sclerosis franchise; a tiny kidney-disease niche became a $100B company.

**Q7 (SA).** What was the "zero of 20" finding?
**A:** A pharma CEO asked how many recent launches landed within 2x of the original forecast. None. Fund portfolios, not point estimates.

**Q8 (EXPLAIN).** Why test loonshots according to the afterword?
**A:** Organizations hold beliefs about products and business models; loonshots are contrarian bets against them. "Would you rather learn you were wrong in your own pilot or in a competitor's press release?"

**Q9 (SA).** What does the low-cost-airline example teach?
**A:** Hundreds of "disruptive" low-cost airlines failed while an incumbent (American) won with sustaining S-types; the disruption label had no steering value.

**Q10 (SCENARIO).** A committee rejects a proposal because it's "merely sustaining" and the market size can't be credibly estimated. What's wrong?
**A:** Both are anti-patterns: ask which belief the idea challenges (P: won't work; S: won't pay) and fund a cheap test. Requiring a credible market estimate would kill the interferons.

**Q11 (SCENARIO).** Run IKEA through both lenses for a manager.
**A:** Disruption lens (hindsight): true but useless to Kamprad in 1953. Loonshot lens: each move challenged a belief: customers won't buy furniture from a warehouse in the countryside (S-type), won't assemble furniture (S-type, born from an employee removing table legs to fit a Volvo), can't source quality outside Sweden (S-type, Poland at half price). The loonshot lens says what to do next: find the belief your rivals hold and test it cheaply.

# Cumulative Final Exam (20 questions)

**1 (SA).** State all four Bush-Vail Rules in one sentence each.
**A:** (1) Separate the phases (artists/soldiers, tailor tools, watch your blind side). (2) Create dynamic equilibrium (love both equally, manage the transfer not the technology, bilingual champions). (3) Spread a system mindset (analyze the decision, wins as well as losses). (4) Raise the magic number (six levers).

**2 (EXPLAIN).** Explain why "innovation is an emergent property of structure" is the premise of the whole book, citing the physical analogy and one organizational example.
**A:** Phases like liquid/solid; a control parameter (size) crossing a threshold flips the same people from risk-takers to project-killers (Nokia, Merck, Disney; Ch 1, 7, 8). Structure shifts the transition the way salt shifts freezing.

**3 (SA).** Which four-quadrant position corresponds to each: Land at Polaroid, Xerox PARC, "everyone must innovate", a firm with no nursery?
**A:** Land: bottom-right Moses Trap. PARC: bottom-right PARC Trap. "Everyone innovate": top-left chaos. No nursery: bottom-left stagnation.

**4 (MC).** M = 150 for E = 50%, S = 6, F = 1 and which G? (a) 6%, (b) 12%, (c) 33%, (d) 50%.
**A:** (b) 12%.

**5 (SA).** What are the numbers for critical mass and the forecast failure rate, and what do they imply together?
**A:** Ten shots at 10% give 65%; two dozen give 92%. Zero of 20 launches landed within 2x of forecast. Together: you can't forecast individual loonshots, so build a portfolio of enough shots.

**6 (T/F).** Sankyo and Merck both terminated statins on an unpublished dog study.
**A:** True (Ch 3); Brown and Goldstein forced a restart.

**7 (SYNTHESIS).** Compare Trippe (Ch 4) and Land (Ch 5). What do they share structurally?

**8 (SYNTHESIS).** How does the False Fail concept (Ch 3) connect to the PARC Trap (Ch 6) and Kodak's 1975 digital camera?

**9 (SA).** What is the cheatsheet rule for deciding a project's fate?
**A:** The person deciding must not be the person who loves it. If the CEO is also head of research, you are Moses.

**10 (SYNTHESIS).** Link Kasparov's levels (Ch 6) to Duke/Kahneman outcome bias as cited in the notes.

**11 (SCENARIO).** A 1,000-person lab has gone political. List three levers and the variable each affects.

**12 (EXPLAIN).** Why is DARPA described as "the OSRD reborn"?
**A:** Small nursery inside a huge organization (about 200 people in a two-million-person one), top-level protection, autonomous program managers, no ladder, fixed terms; it applies the dials of M.

**13 (SA).** What are the Three Deaths timelines and what's the lesson of "zero deaths"?
**A:** Endo 16 years, Black 7, Folkman 32. A project that has died zero times has not been tested.

**14 (MC).** Which is NOT one of the three nursery conditions? (a) critical mass, (b) phase separation, (c) strong culture, (d) dynamic equilibrium.
**A:** (c).

**15 (SYNTHESIS).** How does Song China's failure (Ch 10) mirror the Moses Trap (Ch 5) and the PARC Trap (Ch 6)?

**16 (EXPLAIN).** Why is S in the equation squared and how do the notes use that in Ch 9?

**17 (SCENARIO).** A newly deregulated industry faces a free-platform entrant. Use Ch 4 and Ch 11.
**A:** Expect an S-type wave; inventory strategy loonshots; steer by beliefs challenged rather than by predicted disruption; fund a portfolio with cheap tests.

**A:** Great teams kill great ideas because of structure, not people. Separate loonshot and franchise groups, keep them exchanging on merit, analyze decisions not outcomes, and raise the group size at which politics wins by adjusting equity, span, fitness and salary step-ups, so that ideas survive their Three Deaths and nurseries reach critical mass.
