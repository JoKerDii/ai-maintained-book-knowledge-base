# Range — Patterns & Techniques

## Diagnose the Learning Environment Before Choosing a Strategy
**When to use**: Before deciding whether to specialize, trust an expert, or trust your own intuition in any domain.
**How**: Score the domain on Hogarth's four criteria — (1) Do patterns repeat? (2) Are the rules stable and known? (3) Is feedback fast? (4) Is feedback accurate and unambiguous? Four yeses = **kind**; any no, especially on feedback, = **wicked**. In kind environments, repetition builds real intuition — specialize, drill, trust the expert. In wicked ones, experience can build *confident* wrongness — favor breadth, outside views, and explicit belief updating.
**Trade-offs**: Most real domains are mixed and shift over time. Re-score when the environment changes; expertise built in a formerly kind environment becomes a liability (cognitive entrenchment) when the rules move.

## Run a Sampling Period
**When to use**: Early in any career, skill, or field — and again after any major change in circumstances or interests.
**How**: Deliberately try several adjacent activities at low commitment. Judge each on two axes: what you're good at, and what you actually want to keep doing. Keep the period lightly structured; the goal is *information about yourself*, not accumulated hours. Narrow only when a clear match emerges, and expect the eventual specialist phase to be shorter and steeper than a head start would have been.
**Trade-offs**: You lose early technical head start and will trail the precocious for a while. In genuinely kind domains with fixed early ceilings (elite gymnastics, chess prodigies), that cost is real. Everywhere else, fadeout means the head start wasn't worth much.

## Optimize for Match Quality, Not Persistence
**When to use**: Any decision to stay in or leave a job, field, degree, or project.
**How**: Treat the choice as a **multi-armed bandit** — weigh the *informational value* of switching against the value of continuing. Ask "is this the best fit I know of, given what I have learned about myself since I chose it?" not "am I a quitter?" Enumerate quit conditions in advance, while calm, so the decision isn't made under sunk-cost pressure. Distinguish **informed switching** (you learned something that changed the calculus) from avoidance (you hit an expected difficulty).
**Trade-offs**: Switching resets accumulated domain capital and looks like flightiness on a résumé. The cost falls over time as you learn more about yourself, so switch earlier rather than agonizing longer.

## Make Practice Deliberately Harder (Desirable Difficulties)
**When to use**: Any time you want durable learning and transfer rather than a good feeling during the session.
**How**: Stack four moves. **Generation** — attempt the answer before seeing it, even wrongly; being confidently wrong then corrected sticks best (hypercorrection). **Spacing** — leave enough gap that recall is effortful. **Testing** — retrieve rather than reread. **Interleaving** — mix problem types so you must first identify *which* strategy applies, instead of blocking one type at a time.
**Trade-offs**: Performance during practice drops and learners feel they're learning less — so learners, teachers, and managers all reliably prefer the worse method. Measure on delayed tests and novel problems, never on in-session fluency.

## Take the Outside View
**When to use**: Estimating cost, duration, or success odds for a project — especially one that feels distinctive.
**How**: Ignore your case's specifics first. Strip it to its structural bones, build a **reference class** of superficially unrelated situations that share that deep structure, and take the base rate. Only then adjust for genuine particulars. Use *multiple, distant* analogies rather than one near one — a single surface analogy is worse than none.
**Trade-offs**: Feels dismissive of hard-won detail, and insiders will object that their case is different. That objection is the inside view talking.

## Run an Outsider Search
**When to use**: A problem has resisted the specialists who own it, or progress has stalled inside a single domain.
**How**: Write the problem abstractly enough that a non-specialist can grasp it — strip jargon and stated assumptions about the solution's form. Broadcast it outside the field (the InnoCentive model). Expect winners from the *margins* of the field, whose home domain differs from the problem's. Look for **undiscovered public knowledge**: answers already published in a literature nobody reads alongside yours.
**Trade-offs**: Requires disclosing the problem, which cuts against confidentiality reflexes and insider status. Most outside submissions are useless; the value is in the tail.

## Lateral Thinking with Withered Technology
**When to use**: You cannot win a frontier-technology arms race, or you want reach and reliability over specification.
**How**: Pick technology that is cheap, mature, and thoroughly understood. Invest the creativity in the *application*, not the components. Ask "what could this well-worn thing do that nobody has tried?" Be a producer who spans domains, not an engineer optimizing one.
**Trade-offs**: Reads as unambitious, and specialists will mock the specs. The reverse failure is real too — abandoning the doctrine for novel technology you don't fully understand.

## Build Incongruence Into a Team or Organization
**When to use**: Designing a culture where conformity failures would be catastrophic, or diagnosing why dissent isn't surfacing.
**How**: Deliberately cross-pressure the culture — pair process accountability with outcome accountability, individualist norms with collective ones. Create channels that bypass hierarchy (**Monday Notes**-style: chain of command is not the chain of communication). Actively **prospect for doubts** rather than waiting for objections. Never require dissent to be quantified before it counts.
**Trade-offs**: Uncomfortable, slower, and less "aligned." Strongly congruent cultures are easier to describe and feel better from inside — which is exactly the trap.

## Practice Sensemaking Under Pressure
**When to use**: The situation has stopped matching your training and you feel the pull to execute the familiar routine harder.
**How**: Name that you are in an unfamiliar situation before choosing an action. Ask what *kind* of situation this is, not what the procedure says. Identify your overlearned tools — the credential, the model, the standard process — and ask explicitly which to drop. Hold hunches lightly and say them out loud even without data.
**Trade-offs**: Dropping familiar tools feels like abandoning competence and identity, and is hardest exactly when it matters most.

## Protect Deliberate-Amateur Time
**When to use**: Sustaining creative output over a career; running a research or product group.
**How**: Reserve recurring unstructured time for work outside your specialty and outside your obligations (Friday night / Saturday morning experiments; dabble time). Deliberately work at the edge of your competence where you can still ask naive questions. Maintain a **network of enterprise** — several loosely connected projects — and porous boundaries with other fields, importing methods from one to another.
**Trade-offs**: Unfundable by proposal (you must say what you'll find before you look), invisible on short-term metrics, and mostly unproductive — breakthroughs are high variance, so judge the portfolio, not the session.
