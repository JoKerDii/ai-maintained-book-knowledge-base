## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The whole thesis**: The 10,000-hours/early-specialization model is real but *narrow* — it holds in **kind learning environments** and fails in the **wicked** ones that describe most of modern work and life. In wicked domains, breadth, late specialization, and analogical thinking outperform accumulated domain hours.

### The master distinction — Kind vs. Wicked (Robin Hogarth) [Ch 1]
- **Kind learning environment**: patterns repeat, rules are clear and stable, feedback is fast, accurate, and unambiguous. Chess, golf, firefighting, anesthesiology.
- **Wicked learning environment**: rules are unclear or incomplete, patterns don't reliably repeat, feedback is delayed, inaccurate, or absent — and can actively teach the wrong lesson.
- **Use it as a gate**: *In kind domains, specialize and trust intuition. In wicked ones, distrust the confident veteran and reach for breadth, outside views, and belief updating.* This is the book's single most load-bearing move — apply it before any other framework.
- **The Kahneman–Klein accord**: expert intuition is trustworthy exactly to the degree the environment is kind. Both were right about different worlds.
- **Failure mode**: **cognitive entrenchment** — the deeper the specialization, the more crippling when the environment shifts from kind to wicked.

### Sampling and match quality [Intro, Ch 3, Ch 6, Ch 7]
- **The Tiger path vs. the Roger path**: Woods was engineered from infancy; Federer sampled many sports and specialized late. Elite performers far more often resemble Roger — the media just prefers Tiger's story.
- **Sampling period**: the early phase of trying many activities, learning your own abilities and proclivities, then narrowing. *It is integral to elite development, not a delay to be excised.*
- **Match quality** — the fit between a person's abilities/interests and their work. This is the thing to optimize. **Prefer maximizing match quality over minimizing time-to-specialize**, because head-start advantages **fade out** and a good match compounds.
- **Multi-armed bandit**: sampling has *informational value* that offsets its short-term cost. **Use informed switching when you've learned something that changes the calculus** — this is not a failure of grit.
- **Restriction of range**: grit research was run on already-selected populations (West Point), so it can't tell you when quitting is correct.
- **End of history illusion**: you will change more than you predict → **don't plan long-term from a self you'll outgrow. Test-and-learn, don't plan-and-implement.**

### Learning that transfers [Ch 4]
**Desirable difficulties** (Bjork) — obstacles that depress in-session performance and raise durable learning:
- **Generation effect**: struggle to produce an answer *before* seeing it. Being confidently wrong then corrected sticks best (**hypercorrection effect**).
- **Interleaving**: mix problem types so you must first identify *which* strategy applies — as opposed to **blocked practice**, which feels great and transfers poorly.
- **Spacing** and **testing** over massed rereading.
- **Rule**: judge learning on delayed tests and novel problems, never on fluency during the session. Everyone — learners, teachers, managers — reliably prefers the method that teaches less.

### Thinking outside experience [Ch 5]
- **Deep analogical thinking**: solve novel problems by mapping **deep structure**, not **surface analogies**. **Use multiple, distant analogies — one near analogy is worse than none.**
- **The outside view** (vs. the **inside view**): estimate by building a **reference class** of structurally similar cases and taking the base rate; adjust for specifics only afterward. Your case's vivid details are what corrupt the estimate.
- **Fermi-izing** [Ch 2]: decompose an unanswerable question into estimable parts — part of the **conceptual Swiss Army knife** of broad transferable reasoning tools.

### Innovation from the outside [Ch 8, Ch 9, Ch 12]
- **Outside-in solvers beat local search**: when specialists are stuck, broadcast the problem outward; winners come from the *margins* of the field. Watch for **undiscovered public knowledge** — the answer already published in a literature nobody reads alongside yours.
- **Lateral thinking with withered technology** (Gunpei Yokoi): take cheap, mature, thoroughly understood technology and apply it in a radically new way. **Prefer this over frontier arms races** when reach and reliability beat specs.
- **Breadth profiles**: **birds and frogs** (Dyson), **T-shaped / π-shaped** over **I-shaped**, **serial innovators**, **network of enterprise** (many loosely connected projects).
- **The deliberate amateur**: deliberately work at the edge of your competence, where naivety produces questions insiders won't ask. Protect **Friday night / Saturday morning experiments**.

### Judgment, forecasting, and organizations [Ch 10, Ch 11]
- **Foxes vs. Hedgehogs** (Berlin/Tetlock): hedgehogs know one big thing and get *less* accurate as they get more famous; **foxes with dragonfly eyes** aggregate many perspectives and win. **Belief-updating frequency predicts accuracy better than intelligence or subject expertise.**
- **Active open-mindedness**: treat your beliefs as hypotheses to test, not possessions to defend.
- **Dropping your familiar tools** (Weick): under pressure experts grip overlearned routines even when those routines are killing them. **Switch from decision making to sensemaking** — ask what situation you're actually in before asking what the procedure says.
- **Incongruence beats congruence**: deliberately cross-pressure a culture (process *and* outcome accountability; chain of command ≠ chain of communication). Perfect alignment suppresses the dissent that prevents catastrophe. Never require a doubt to be quantified before it counts.

### The stance [Conclusion]
**Don't feel behind.** Breakthroughs are high variance; compare your portfolio and process, not your timeline against someone else's. **Plan experiments, not plans.**
