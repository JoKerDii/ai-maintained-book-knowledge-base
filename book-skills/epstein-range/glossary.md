# Range — Glossary

**Action at a distance** — A force operating without contact; the deep-structure idea Kepler reached only by analogy from light and magnets. (Ch 5)

**Active open-mindedness** — Jonathan Baron's disposition of treating your own beliefs as hypotheses to be tested rather than treasures to be defended; the strongest correlate of forecasting accuracy. (Ch 10)

**Altered gospel** — The popularized, truncated version of grit research that drops its caveats and turns "don't quit" into universal advice. (Ch 6)

**Ambiguous Sorting Task** — A test where problems can be grouped by surface features or by deep structure; people trained across domains sort by deep structure. (Ch 5)

**Bansho** — Japanese teaching practice of arraying multiple student-generated solutions on the board for comparison, forcing making-connections work. (Ch 4)

**Birds and frogs** — Freeman Dyson's division: frogs delve into the mud of specific problems; birds survey broadly and connect. Both are needed; ecosystems fail when only frogs are funded. (Ch 9)

**Blocked practice** — Drilling one skill type repeatedly before moving on. Feels effective, produces fast gains and poor retention. (Ch 4)

**Career streams** — Viewing a career as a path of experiences and learning rather than a ladder of titles. (Ch 1)

**Centaurs** — Human-computer teams; in freestyle chess, amateurs with good process beat both grandmasters and standalone engines. (Ch 1)

**Chunking** — Perceiving meaningful recurring patterns as single units; the mechanism of expertise in kind domains, and useless when patterns don't recur. (Ch 1)

**Cognitive entrenchment** — Ryan Dane's term for expertise that becomes inflexible: the deeper the specialization, the harder it is to adapt when the environment changes. (Ch 1)

**Conceptual Swiss Army knife** — Broad, transferable reasoning tools (Fermi-izing, economics, statistics) usable on unfamiliar problems. (Ch 2)

**Congruence / Incongruence** — Congruence is cultural "fit" among an institution's values, goals, and norms; Epstein argues *incongruence* (deliberate internal cross-pressure) protects organizations from conformity failures. (Ch 11)

**Consistency of interests** — The Grit Scale subcomponent measuring how long you stick with the same goals; high scores can indicate a good match or an unexamined one. (Ch 6)

**Context principle** — Dark Horse Project finding that motivation and performance are situation-specific, not fixed traits. (Ch 7)

**Dark Horse Project** — Harvard study of unconventionally successful people; nearly all used short-term planning from present interests rather than long-term destination plans. (Ch 7)

**Deliberate amateur** — Arturo Casadevall's ideal: a trained expert who deliberately works at the edge of their competence, where naivety enables questions insiders won't ask. (Ch 12)

**Deliberate practice** — Ericsson's narrow construct: effortful repetition with immediate feedback on well-defined tasks. Valid in kind domains; overextended everywhere else. (Intro, Ch 3)

**Desirable difficulties** — Robert Bjork's term for obstacles that slow learning down and make it stick: spacing, testing, interleaving, generation. (Ch 4)

**Dropping your familiar tools** — Karl Weick's concept: under pressure, experts cling to overlearned routines even when those routines are killing them; survival requires letting them go. (Ch 11)

**Eduction** — Raven's term for making meaning out of confusion — solving novel abstract problems without prior instruction. (Ch 2)

**Einstellung effect** — A fixed mental set: the first familiar approach that comes to mind blocks better alternatives. (Ch 8)

**Empowering innovation** — Technology that makes an existing capability available to far more people, rather than pushing the frontier for a few. (Ch 9)

**End of history illusion** — Quoidbach/Gilbert finding that people at every age know they've changed but predict they won't change further. (Ch 7)

**Fadeout** — The pattern where early head-start advantages evaporate, and sometimes reverse, over time. (Ch 4)

**Far transfer** — Applying knowledge to a genuinely novel context; the rare, valuable form of transfer that breadth enables. (Ch 4)

**Fermi-izing** — Decomposing an unanswerable question into estimable parts to get a defensible order-of-magnitude answer. (Ch 2)

**Figlie del coro** — The all-female musicians of Venice's ospedali, Europe's finest, trained by wide instrument sampling rather than early specialization. (Ch 3)

**Flynn effect** — The steady rise in abstract-reasoning IQ scores across the 20th century, reflecting modern "scientific spectacles." (Ch 2)

**Foxes vs. Hedgehogs** — Berlin's distinction, tested by Tetlock: hedgehogs know one big thing and forecast worse the more famous they are; foxes know many small things and forecast better. (Ch 10)

**Functional fixedness** — Seeing an object or idea only in terms of its conventional use. (Ch 9)

**Generation effect** — Struggling to produce an answer before being shown it produces far better retention than being given the answer. (Ch 4)

**Good Judgment Project** — Tetlock's forecasting tournament; superforecasters were broad, numerate, and above all frequent belief-updaters. (Ch 10)

**Hypercorrection effect** — The more confident you were in a wrong answer, the more durably you remember the correction. (Ch 4)

**Interleaving** — Mixing problem types within practice so the learner must first identify *which* strategy applies. Feels worse, transfers far better. (Ch 4)

**Kind learning environment** — Hogarth's term: patterns repeat, rules are clear, feedback is fast, accurate, and unambiguous. Chess, golf, firefighting. (Ch 1)

**Knowledge as a double-edged sword** — Expertise that supplies solutions also constrains which solutions get considered. (Ch 8)

**Lateral thinking with withered technology** — Gunpei Yokoi's doctrine: take cheap, mature, well-understood technology and deploy it in a radically new use, rather than competing on the frontier. (Ch 9)

**Local search** — Solving a problem by looking only in the domain where it arose; the default that outsider searches beat. (Ch 8)

**Match quality** — The degree of fit between a person's abilities and interests and the work they do. Epstein's central optimization target — it is *found*, not planned. (Ch 6, Ch 7)

**Moravec's paradox** — Machines excel at what humans find hard (calculation) and fail at what humans find easy (perception, judgment in open situations). (Ch 1)

**Multi-armed bandit process** — The formal explore/exploit trade-off; sampling widely early has informational value that justifies its short-term cost. (Ch 6)

**Naturalistic decision making (NDM)** — Klein's research program on expert intuition; valid where environments are kind. (Ch 1)

**Network of enterprise** — Howard Gruber's term for creative people running many loosely connected projects at once. (Ch 9)

**Oculostenotic reflex** — The cardiologist's impulse to stent a visible blockage because it's visible, regardless of whether it helps. (Ch 11)

**Outside view** — Kahneman/Tversky: estimating by finding structurally analogous cases (a reference class) rather than reasoning from your case's vivid details. (Ch 5)

**Overlearned behavior** — A routine practiced to automaticity; efficient in kind conditions, lethal when conditions change. (Ch 11)

**Possible selves** — The range of future identities you might become; discovered by trying, not introspection. (Ch 7)

**Principle of limited sloppiness** — Max Delbrück's rule: be sloppy enough that unexpected things can happen, not so sloppy you can't tell what happened. (Ch 12)

**Process vs. outcome accountability** — Being answerable for following procedure vs. for results; process accountability alone breeds conformity and suppresses dissent. (Ch 11)

**Range** — Breadth of experience, training, and perspective; the book's title virtue. (Intro)

**Restriction of range** — The statistical artifact where studying an already-selected population (e.g. West Point cadets) hides how much a trait actually varies. (Ch 6)

**Sampling period** — The early phase in which future elite performers try many activities, learn their own abilities and interests, then narrow. Integral, not a delay. (Intro, Ch 3)

**Scientific spectacles** — Luria's finding that modern schooling teaches people to classify abstractly rather than reason from concrete experience. (Ch 2)

**Sensemaking** — Weick's alternative to decision making: figuring out what situation you are actually in, especially when the familiar frame has broken. (Ch 11)

**Serial innovators** — Employees who repeatedly create breakthroughs; characteristically broad, high in "systems thinking," and poorly served by narrow hiring rubrics. (Ch 9)

**Single-loop learning** — Argyris: responding to feedback by refining the existing approach rather than questioning the goal or model. (Ch 1)

**Superforecasters** — Good Judgment Project's top performers; distinguished by granular probability estimates and aggressive belief updating. (Ch 10)

**Surface analogy** — A match based on visible similarity rather than causal structure; the failure mode of analogical reasoning. (Ch 5)

**T-shaped / I-shaped / π-shaped** — Breadth-plus-one-depth, single narrow depth, and breadth-plus-two-depths skill profiles. (Ch 9)

**Test-and-learn (vs. plan-and-implement)** — Choose a promising next step, try it, learn from the result, revise. The Dark Horse strategy. (Ch 7)

**Undiscovered public knowledge** — Don Swanson's insight that answers already exist in the literature, unlinked across specialties nobody reads together. (Ch 8)

**Whole Candidate Score** — West Point's admissions composite; predicted who got in, not who stayed. (Ch 6)

**Wicked learning environment** — Hogarth's term: rules are unclear or incomplete, patterns don't reliably repeat, feedback is delayed, inaccurate, or absent. Most of the modern world. (Ch 1)

**Withered technology** — Mature, cheap, thoroughly understood technology — the raw material of lateral innovation. (Ch 9, Ch 12)
