# Chapter 3: When Less of the Same Is More

## Core Idea
Even in classical music — the most golflike, blueprint-driven skill domain there is — the exceptional performers are the ones who spread effort across multiple instruments, started later, and took fewer early structured lessons. **Breadth of training predicts breadth of transfer.**

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Breadth of training predicts breadth of transfer**: the more contexts in which something is learned, the more the learner creates abstract models, and the less they rely on any particular example — so they become better at applying knowledge to situations they've never seen, which is the essence of creativity.
  - When to use: designing any curriculum, practice regime, or personal skill-building plan.
  - How: deliberately vary the context — instruments, genres, problem types, tools — rather than accumulating reps in one. Spread effort across roughly three variants before narrowing.
  - Why it works / failure mode: varied contexts force abstraction rather than memorization of a single instance. Failure mode is the single-context specialist whose skill collapses the moment the surface details change.
- **The sampling period in music**: a lightly structured phase with some lessons and a breadth of instruments and activities, followed only later by a narrowing of focus, increased structure, and an explosion of practice volume.
  - When to use: at the start of any skill, and any time you're about to assign a child (or yourself) a single track.
  - How: let the practitioner try multiple instruments and let the instrument drive the practitioner — Sloboda's students who became most successful only started practicing much more *after* they identified an instrument they wanted to focus on, whether because they were better at it or just liked it more.
- **Learning like improv masters / like babies**: dive in and imitate and improvise first, learn the formal rules later. Cecchini's formulation: you acquired the sound first, and the grammar later — nobody handed you a book about dangling participles before you spoke.
  - When to use: creative and improvisational domains, and any domain where finding your own voice matters more than fidelity to a blueprint.
  - How: imitate by ear, experiment, solve problems yourself; add notation and theory afterward. Shinichi Suzuki designed the Suzuki Method explicitly to mimic natural language acquisition — children do not practice exercises to learn to talk, and learn to read only after speaking is well established.
- **Low prior restraint parenting** (via Adam Grant): households with extremely creative children had an average of one rule, versus six for typical children; the parents made their opinions known *after* their kids did something they disliked, rather than proscribing it beforehand.
  - When to use: nurturing creativity in anyone you supervise. Creativity is difficult to nurture but easy to thwart.

## Key Concepts
- **Figlie del coro**: "daughters of the choir," the all-female musicians of Venice's ospedali who dominated European music for a century, learning every instrument their institution owned.
- **Ospedali**: charitable institutions in Venice founded to ameliorate particular social ills; the Ospedale della Pietà raised abandoned girls and trained them in music.
- **Scaffetta**: the drawer built into the Pietà's outer wall through which infants small enough to fit were surrendered.
- **Musical laboratory of unlimited resources** (Marc Pincherle): what Vivaldi effectively had in the multiskilled figlie and their menagerie of instruments.
- **The Tiger Mother model**: Amy Chua's regime — choose early, focus narrowly, never waver; on page one, the children may play no instrument other than the piano or the violin.
- **Learning by osmosis** (Paul Berliner): how professional improvisers' childhoods actually proceeded — not formal instruction; most explored the band room's diverse options as a prelude to selecting an instrument of specialization.
- **Creative vs. re-creative artist** (Cecchini): the jazz musician creates, the classical musician re-creates — and it is easier for a jazz musician to learn classical literature than the reverse.
- **Deliberate practice framework (in music)**: counts only highly focused attempts at exactly the skill to be performed, which makes multiple instruments look like wasted time.

## Mental Models
- Use a third instrument (or third tool, third language, third domain) when you feel pressure to consolidate — the modest investment in a third instrument paid off handsomely for the exceptional children.
- Think of the instrument as driving the practitioner, not the reverse: pick the thing that pulls you, then ramp volume.
- Use imitation-then-theory when you want originality, and theory-then-execution only when the task is faithful reproduction.
- Think of versatility as insurance: when Pelegrina's teeth fell out in her sixties, she switched from oboe back to violin and played into her seventies; when Django lost two fingers, he re-invented his fret hand.

## Anti-patterns
- **Picking a child's instrument for them**: in a study of twelve hundred young musicians, those who quit reported a mismatch between the instruments they wanted to play and the ones they actually played. Chua's daughter Lulu — called a "natural musician" — told her mother "You picked it, not me," and quit at thirteen.
- **Heavy structured lessons early**: every single student who had received a large amount of structured lesson time early in development fell into the "average" category at the selective British boarding school; not one was in the exceptional group.
- **Refusing to leave your first instrument**: less skilled students spent their time on the first instrument they picked up, as if they could not give up a perceived head start.
- **Training only in error correction**: Limb's MRI work found that brain areas for focused attention, inhibition, and self-censoring turn *down* during improvisation — it is almost as if the brain turned off its ability to criticize itself. Improvisers do the opposite of stopping to fix errors.
- **Producing interchangeable graduates**: Cecchini's jazz-school students "all sound the same" and don't find their own voice, because they never had to solve problems themselves.

## Worked Example
The Pietà was a foundling hospital: infants, mostly girls, many born to women in Venice's sex industry, were left in a drawer in the wall and named for the building — Anna Maria della Pietà, Agata della Pietà, on through Vittoria. From that population came the most celebrated musicians in Europe. Anna Maria was declared the premier violinist in Europe by a German baron; Vivaldi spent four months' salary on a violin for her at sixteen and wrote twenty-eight concertos preserved in her scarlet leather notebook. Rousseau declared their music had no like in Italy or the rest of the world — and was shocked when he finally saw the performers behind the crepe-covered grille, disfigured by smallpox and syphilis, some missing fingers or a foot. The training that produced them contradicts every head-start prescription. Chores meant only about an hour a day of music study early on, with formal lessons three days a week. What was extraordinary was the *number of instruments*: Charles Burney was astounded that "these young persons frequently change instruments"; the figlie learned every instrument the institution owned, including a tromba marina, a zink, a viola da gamba, a chalumeau, a psaltery, and a "violoncello all'inglese" nobody can now identify. Anna Maria died having been, by stages, Anna Maria del violino, del theorbo, del cembalo, del violoncello, del luta, della viola d'amore, and del mandolin. Sloboda's modern data reproduces the finding exactly: at a British boarding school admitting purely by audition, the exceptional students came from less musically active families, did not start younger, had taken fewer lessons, and had practiced a lot less — but had distributed their effort more evenly across three instruments. A follow-up two decades later found nearly all students admitted to a competitive conservatory had played at least three instruments, and more than half played four or five. What this demonstrates: multi-instrument breadth is not a detour from virtuosity in a kind domain — it is the mechanism that produces it, and the sampling period is integral rather than excisable.

## Key Takeaways
1. Spread early effort across about three instruments/tools/domains before narrowing; the third one pays disproportionately.
2. Let the learner choose the specialty from experience — mismatch between assigned and desired instrument is the main quitting cause.
3. Delay heavy structured instruction; too many lessons at a young age may not be helpful.
4. Total practice volume early is not an indicator of exceptionality — sheer lesson or practice time predicts little.
5. In creative domains, acquire the sound before the grammar: imitate, experiment, then formalize.
6. Cut rules, not effort — if you want creativity in a household or team, run low on prior restraint.
7. Build versatility as a hedge against physical or market disruption to your one skill.
