# Chapter 5: Thinking Outside Experience

## Core Idea
For problems you have never seen before, direct experience is a trap; the tool that works is a *volume* of distant analogies drawn from unrelated domains, which surfaces deep structural similarities and forces the outside view. Our intuition does the opposite — it reaches for one superficially similar case and stays inside the problem's details.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Deep analogical thinking** (Dedre Gentner): recognizing conceptual similarities across multiple domains or scenarios that seem to have little in common on the surface.
  - When to use: wicked problems, novel problems, and any situation with no relevant experience database — Kepler's condition.
  - How: when stuck, generate a fusillade of analogies from *different base domains*, interrogate each one until it breaks, and let the break generate the next question. Deliberately include analogies that are, on the surface, far removed from the problem.
  - Why it works / failure mode: multiple structurally similar but surface-dissimilar cases isolate the causal structure. Failure mode: a single close analogy is worse than none — in the movie-forecasting study, using only the single most analogous film collapsed predictive power.
- **The outside view** (vs. the **inside view**, Kahneman and Tversky): the inside view judges from the details of the particular project in front of you; the outside view probes for deep structural similarities in *different* problems.
  - When to use: any forecast, budget, timeline, or ROI estimate on a project you are close to.
  - How: (1) describe your project and estimate the outcome; (2) list a batch of other projects with broad *conceptual* similarity, ignoring surface features; (3) estimate each of those; (4) revise your own estimate against that class.
  - Why it works: it strips the unique-surface-feature detail on which you are the expert and which reliably drives judgments toward the extreme.
- **Reference class**: use the full set of analogous cases, not the best-matching one — "the pillar of the outside view."
- **Ambiguous Sorting Task** (Gentner): 25 cards describing real-world phenomena, each classifiable by domain (economics, biology) or by deep structure (positive-feedback loop, negative-feedback loop, causal chain).
  - When to use: as a diagnostic and a training exercise for structural thinking.
  - How: force sorting by causal structure, not subject matter — economic bubbles with melting ice caps; sweating with Federal Reserve rate policy.

## Key Concepts
- **Surface analogy**: a match on visible features; adequate in a kind world where patterns repeat, dangerous otherwise.
- **Deep structure**: the causal architecture of a problem, independent of its subject matter.
- **Kind-world hypothesis**: the assumption that the analogy that pops to mind is relationally apt because the environment repeats — "perfectly fine if you stay in the same village or the same savannah all your life."
- **Ill-defined problems**: problems for which human intuition is poorly engineered to select the best available tools.
- **Base domains**: the variety of fields a thinker can draw analogies *from*; range in base domains is the raw material for analogical reasoning.
- **Action at a distance**: the then-unthinkable notion Kepler reached only through analogy — a power invisibly traversing space to appear at its target.
- **Typing of the problem**: successful problem solvers determine deep structure *before* matching a strategy; "problem solving begins with the typing of the problem."

## Mental Models
- Use "who is this like?" rather than "what is this?" — Netflix predicts by analogizing you to customers with similar histories, not by decoding film traits.
- Think of your first analogy as a bias, not an answer: Stanford students primed with WWII chose war, primed with Vietnam chose diplomacy, on the identical scenario.
- Use more internal detail as a warning sign, not a confidence signal — the more details considered, the more extreme the judgment becomes.
- Think of a lab, team, or firm as "Keplers by committee": breakthrough capacity scales with the diversity of professional backgrounds available to supply analogies.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using one analogy, and a near one**: business students believed the best strategy was a single same-industry example; those given Nike and McDonald's generated more strategic options than those given Apple and Dell. "That's usually exactly the wrong way to go about it."
- **Staying inside the problem**: most solvers of the radiation problem summoned more medical knowledge instead of reaching outside the domain.
- **Trusting expert-detailed bottom-up estimates**: ~90% of major infrastructure projects go over budget (average 28%); Edinburgh's tram was rigorously costed at £320 million from the inside and headed toward £1 billion.
- **Monocultural teams**: the one lab in Dunbar's study that produced no new findings had uniformly specialized backgrounds and almost never used analogies — "a group of similar minded individuals will not provide more information to make analogies than a single individual."
- **Explaining away the anomaly**: patching the hypothesis to preserve five years of work, which is precisely what Kepler refused to do.

## Worked Example
**Kepler's march of analogies, and the eight minutes of arc.** Assigned the job nobody wanted — Mars's baffling retrograde orbit — Kepler spent five years building an accurate model, then discarded it because two of Brahe's observations were off by eight minutes of arc, an eighth the width of a pinkie at arm's length. "If I had believed we could ignore these eight minutes, I would have patched my hypothesis accordingly." With no concept of gravity, force, or momentum to inherit, he had no evidence base and had to reason by analogy: smells and heat (dissipate with distance), light (exists at source and target but not visibly between — proof that action at a distance is conceptually possible), then ruled light out because motion continued through eclipses; then magnets with poles, then a whirlpool sweeping boats, then boatmen steering across the current — each analogy interrogated until it broke and generated the next question. He arrived at bodies pulling one another in proportion to size, correctly predicted the moon's influence on tides (Galileo mocked him), and invented astrophysics. This demonstrates the chapter's mechanism precisely: when there is no experience to draw on, *quantity and distance* of analogies substitute for data — and Kevin Dunbar found the same behavior live in modern molecular biology labs, where the most productive labs offered a new analogy every four minutes and drew them from a wide variety of base domains, while the E. coli-only lab spent weeks experimenting on a problem a mixed-background lab solved in one meeting.

## Key Takeaways
1. When facing a novel problem, generate many analogies before generating solutions — and deliberately include ones from unrelated fields.
2. Distrust the first analogy that comes to mind; it is a surface match and it will steer the whole decision.
3. Before estimating any project outcome, build the reference class and estimate that first.
4. Sort problems by causal structure (feedback loops, causal chains) rather than by subject-matter domain.
5. Treat an unexpected finding as a doorway, not an error — but only after ruling out your own miscalculation.
6. Staff teams for variety of base domains; analogical range is a property of the group, not just the individual.
7. Build institutional supports for wide analogizing (BCG's intranet of exhibits sorted by discipline, concept, and strategic theme).
