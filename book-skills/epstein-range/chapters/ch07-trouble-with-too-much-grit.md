# Chapter 6: The Trouble with Too Much Grit

## Core Idea
Persistence toward a goal you chose before you knew yourself is not a virtue; finding a goal with high **match quality** is the harder and more valuable problem, and quitting is the mechanism that finds it. Grit predicts well only inside narrow, preselected, goal-given contexts — which is exactly where most of its evidence comes from.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Match quality**: the economists' term for the degree of fit between the work someone does and who they are — their abilities and proclivities.
  - When to use: whenever evaluating a career decision, a switch, a hire, or a retention problem.
  - How: treat every course, job, and assignment as producing two returns — domain-specific skills *and* information about fit. Optimize the second early, the first later.
  - Why it works / failure mode: match quality gains compound faster than skill losses ("the benefits to increased match quality outweigh the greater loss in skills"). Failure mode: an organization that offers money instead of fit — the Army's $500M retention bonus was taken by officers already staying and refused by those already leaving.
- **Multi-armed bandit process** (Robert A. Miller): model career matching as a gambler facing a row of slot machines, each with unknown payoff, who must allocate pulls between testing machines and exploiting the best one found.
  - When to use: early career, or any time you hold little information about your own fit.
  - How: start with the paths that carry the highest **informational value** — high-risk, high-reward, fast unforgiving feedback (athlete, actor, startup founder). Extract the fit signal as quickly as possible, then reallocate.
  - Why it works: "young and foolish" is not foolish; with the least experience you should buy information at the highest rate, because the cost of a wrong pull is lowest then.
- **Enumerate your quit conditions in advance** (Seth Godin): before undertaking an endeavor, write down the conditions under which you should quit.
  - When to use: at the start of any long commitment.
  - How: specify in advance what evidence would constitute a fit signal rather than a difficulty signal — the trick is "staying attuned to whether switching is simply a failure of perseverance, or astute recognition that better matches are available."
- **Grit Scale** (Angela Duckworth): a twelve-statement self-assessment combining two components — work ethic/resilience, and **consistency of interests** (knowing exactly what one wants).
  - When to use: understand it as two separable traits; the second is the one that penalizes healthy re-specification of goals.

## Key Concepts
- **Restriction of range**: when subjects are preselected on a variable, that variable stops predicting within the group and other traits look inflated — like studying height's importance using only NBA players.
- **Whole Candidate Score**: West Point's composite admissions metric (test scores, class rank, fitness, leadership); the top admissions factor, useless for predicting Beast dropouts *because* it was the selection filter.
- **Informed switching**: leaving because new information revealed a poor fit; distinct from quitting under difficulty.
- **Sunk cost fallacy**: continuing to invest because of what has already been spent and is already gone.
- **Altered gospel** (Naifeh and Smith, on Van Gogh): a pattern of total devotion to a style or creed followed by total disavowal.
- **Company man era**: the industrial-era model of highly specialized firms, pensions, repeating challenges, and career control held by the employer.
- **Talent matching market mechanism**: the internal market that lets people move toward fit — present in the private sector since the 1980s, absent in the Army.
- **Consistency of interests**: the Grit Scale component measuring unchanging goals; the one Epstein and West Point graduates both score low on.

## Mental Models
- Think of a career like dating, not marriage at sixteen — "if we treated careers more like dating, nobody would settle down so quickly."
- Use grit when the goal is given and measurable (Beast Barracks, an 800-meter season); use sampling when choosing the goal is the actual problem.
- Think of a quit as the purchase of information, not the forfeiture of investment.
- Use disengagement as a signal: with 85% of workers globally not engaged or actively disengaged, quitting takes more guts than being carried along.

## Anti-patterns
- **Pick and stick as early as possible**: the "one-size-fits-all Tiger story"; treats a change of interest as an imperfection rather than a response to lived experience.
- **Over-extrapolating grit research**: Duckworth herself warned she may have contributed to "high-stakes character assessment," an idea she vigorously opposes; her team noted preselected samples "necessarily limited the external validity."
- **Paying people to stay in a poor match**: $500 million wasted; the problem was matching, not financial.
- **Quoting truncated maxims**: Churchill's "never give in, never, never, never, never" ends "except to convictions of honor and good sense."
- **Escalating on sunk costs**: conmen ask for several small investments first, because the mark will then persist past the point any rational observer sees disaster.
- **Reading turnover as lost drive**: departing junior officers had "a strong drive for personal development" that changed their goals; teachers who switch schools become *more* effective because they leave poor matches (Kirabo Jackson).

## Worked Example
**West Point's leaky officer pipeline as a match quality problem.** Duckworth's Grit Scale outperformed the Whole Candidate Score at predicting who would drop out of Beast Barracks — but only 71 of 1,218 plebes dropped out that year, from a population already filtered on the Whole Candidate Score (restriction of range). The more informative pattern comes later: since the mid-1990s about half of West Point graduates leave active service at the first legal opportunity (five years), and three-quarters before the twenty-year pension mark — the *highest* attrition of any officer-training route. Retention runs exactly inverse to investment: OCS trainees stay longest, then unfunded ROTC, then two-year, then three-year scholarships, then West Point and full-scholarship ROTC last. The Army's own Strategic Studies Institute monograph, written by current and former West Point professors, diagnosed this not as a collapse of grit but as match quality: the Army identifies the most promising people, develops them, and those people then discover how many options they have. The $500M retention bonus failed; the Officer Career Satisfaction Program — trading three extra service years for choice of branch or geographic post — got 4,000 cadets to extend in four years. This demonstrates the chapter's thesis institutionally: money cannot buy persistence in a bad match, but a matching mechanism can. Van Gogh is the same demonstration individually — art dealer, teacher, bookseller, would-be pastor, itinerant preacher, each pursued with punishing work ethic and each abandoned, scoring only the 40th percentile on the Grit Scale when his biographer filled it out — a multi-armed bandit run at maximum intensity that zigzagged him to a place no one else had ever been.

## Key Takeaways
1. Separate the two questions: "am I persisting enough?" and "am I in the right thing?" Grit only answers the first.
2. Before committing, write your quit conditions; decide in advance what counts as fit information versus mere difficulty.
3. Sample deliberately and specialize later — Scottish students, forced to study broadly for two years, switched careers less and caught up in income quickly.
4. Prefer early paths with fast, unforgiving feedback; they are the cheapest way to buy self-knowledge.
5. Treat switching as compounding: switchers take a skills hit but have higher growth rates afterward.
6. Discount grit findings drawn from preselected populations; check for restriction of range before applying them.
7. If you lead an organization, build a matching mechanism — internal mobility beats retention bonuses.
