# Chapter 10: Fooled by Expertise

## Core Idea
In wicked domains, narrow expertise makes forecasts worse, not better: specialists bend every event to fit one big theory and get more dogmatic as they accumulate information. Accuracy comes from breadth — integrators who collect many perspectives, hold beliefs as hypotheses, and update hard on surprise.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Foxes vs. Hedgehogs** (Tetlock, borrowing Isaiah Berlin): hedgehogs "know one big thing"; foxes "know many little things."
  - When to use: any prediction about a complex, human, or political system where feedback is slow and noisy.
  - How: notice whether you are reaching for a formulaic solution from one tradition, or drawing on an eclectic array of traditions and accepting ambiguity and contradiction. Hedgehogs "toil devotedly" inside one specialty; foxes range outside a single discipline or theory.
  - Why it works / failure mode: hedgehogs' deep knowledge lets them cherry-pick details that fit their all-encompassing theory, so more information makes them worse. They are outstanding at predicting the past and dart-throwing chimps at predicting the future — and they make great TV, which is why fame and accuracy run in a "perverse inverse relationship."
- **Active open-mindedness** (Jonathan Baron): treat your own ideas as hypotheses in need of testing.
  - When to use: whenever you have a strong view and a live decision.
  - How: aim not to convince teammates of your expertise but to get them to help you falsify your notions. Proactively cross disciplines hunting for contrary evidence rather than waiting for it. "Depth can be inadequate without breadth."
  - Why it works / failure mode: the default human move is to generate "myside" ideas and never search for why you might be wrong; two-thirds of one study's participants declined payment rather than read counterarguments.
- **Foxes with dragonfly eyes**: synthesize tens of thousands of separate lenses into one view.
  - When to use: building a forecasting or decision team.
  - How: aggregate many perspectives rather than pursue agreement; practice "extremely polite antagonism" — disagreeing without being disagreeable.
- **Outside view via structural analogies** (an hour of foxy training measurably improved accuracy):
  - How: before touching the details, generate a list of separate, apparently unrelated events with deep structural similarities to the case at hand. Uniqueness is a matter of degree. Starting with the inside view — the details — is dangerous.
- **Rigorous feedback in a wicked environment**: ferociously dissect prediction results, especially the bad ones, to manufacture the feedback the domain does not supply.

## Key Concepts
- **Superforecasters**: ordinary volunteers with wide-ranging interests, no relevant credentials, who beat intelligence analysts with classified access.
- **Good Judgment Project**: Tetlock and Mellers's IARPA-tournament team, built by open call and weighted toward the foxiest forecasters.
- **Integrators**: forecasters not vested in a single approach who take from each argument and combine contradictory worldviews.
- **Science curiosity** (Kahan): appetite for new evidence regardless of whether it confirms you — distinct from science knowledge, which increases dogmatism.
- **Myside ideas**: the flood of confirming arguments people generate automatically on a contested question.
- **Belief updating**: "Good judges are good belief updaters" — they embrace the logic of a loss as they would a win.
- **Wicked learning environment**: a domain where feedback is delayed, noisy, or absent, so experience alone does not improve performance.
- **Superteams**: small teams of foxes whose members became ~50% more accurate individually; they beat both crowd wisdom and prediction markets.

## Mental Models
- Use the outside view first when an event feels totally unique — "no country has ever left the eurozone" still has analogues in failed negotiations and forced currency conversions.
- Think of a specialist as a source of facts, not opinions — Ellen Cousins's rule: take facts from experts, not conclusions, because they may have blinders on.
- Use "flip-flopping" as a competence signal in forecasting; unlike politicians, the most adept predictors change positions constantly.
- Think of confidence as inversely informative: Tetlock found a "curiously inverse relationship" between how well forecasters thought they were doing and how well they did.

## Anti-patterns
- **Claiming total victories and near-miss defeats**: if every hit proves your expertise and every miss was "just a timing issue," you have made yourself unfalsifiable and stopped learning.
- **Updating in the wrong direction**: some hedgehogs, after wildly wrong predictions, became more convinced of the beliefs that misled them.
- **Deferring to the subject-area expert on the forecast**: Scott Eastman found his own areas of deep study (Russian) were among his weakest — "if you're too much of an insider, it's hard to get good perspective."
- **Mistaking a proxy for the thing**: picking a metric that will "prove" your worldview when it has little to do with it.

## Worked Example
Paul Ehrlich (biologist, *The Population Bomb*) bet economist Julian Simon that five metals would rise in price as overpopulation depleted resources; in 1990 Simon collected $576.07 because every price fell. Both men declared their worldview vindicated or merely mistimed, and both dug in — yet later analysis showed Ehrlich would have won the same bet in 62% of ten-year windows since 1900. Commodity prices were a bad proxy for population effects; a recession moved them. Ehrlich was right about environmental degradation and wrong about famine; Simon was right about ingenuity and wrong to credit markets for air and water gains that came from the regulation Ehrlich pushed. The point: two hedgehogs each amassed more information for a single big idea and grew *more* dogmatic and *less* accurate, and might as well have flipped a coin. Tetlock's twenty-year, 82,361-forecast study then generalized it — the average expert was a horrific forecaster, "impossible" events happened 15% of the time, "sure things" failed over a quarter of the time — while the fox integrators who held that Gorbachev was a genuine reformer *and* that the USSR had lost legitimacy outside Russia were the only ones who saw the collapse coming.

## Key Takeaways
1. Before forecasting, ask whether you are reasoning from one big idea or many little ones — and deliberately import a discipline you don't work in.
2. Generate the reference class of structurally similar events before you look at the specifics.
3. Score yourself, especially on misses; build feedback the domain won't give you.
4. Recruit for range and curiosity, not credentials, then run the team on polite antagonism with individual predictions and collective scoring.
5. Treat every belief as a hypothesis you are trying to falsify; if you can't state what would change your mind, you are a hedgehog on that topic.
6. More information about your own specialty is not neutral — it is fuel for cherry-picking.
