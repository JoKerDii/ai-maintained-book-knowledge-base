# Chapter 2: How the Wicked World Was Made

## Core Idea
Modernity rewired how humans think — away from concrete, experience-bound reasoning and toward abstract classification — and that shift is what makes range valuable; yet education has responded to the broadening of the mind by pushing specialization instead of teaching transferable conceptual tools.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Scientific spectacles** (Flynn's term for Luria's "categorical" thinking): rather than relying on our own direct experiences, we make sense of reality through classification schemes, using layers of abstract concepts to understand how pieces of information relate to one another.
  - When to use: whenever a problem is one you have no direct experience of.
  - How: Luria's own criterion — classify by substance, material, size, color, or other property, and above all be able to *move freely, shifting from one category to another*. That ability to shift is the chief characteristic of abstract thinking.
  - Why it works / failure mode: conceptual schemes are flexible, able to arrange information for a wide variety of uses and to transfer knowledge between domains. The failure mode is the modern inverse of the villagers' — premodern people miss the forest for the trees; modern people miss the trees for the forest.
- **Fermi-izing (Fermi problems)**: breaking a problem down so you can leverage what little you know to start investigating what you don't — estimating, by reasoning alone, to the right order of magnitude.
  - When to use: any numeric claim you cannot verify, any estimate with no lookup available, any news article or advertisement quoting statistics.
  - How: decompose into sub-quantities you can bound (population, households, fraction owning the thing, frequency of the service, time per job, working days per year), estimate each roughly, and multiply. No individual estimate has to be particularly accurate to get a reasonable overall answer.
  - Why it works / failure mode: errors in independent rough estimates partially cancel; the lesson is that detailed prior knowledge matters less than a way of thinking. A little training in broad thinking strategies like Fermi-izing goes a long way and transfers across domains.
- **The conceptual Swiss Army knife**: a portable kit of broadly applicable reasoning tools, contrasted with narrow critical competence in one field. Jeannette Wing's **computational thinking** — "using abstraction and decomposition when attacking a large complex task" and "choosing an appropriate representation for a problem" — is one candidate tool she argues should be as fundamental as reading.
  - When to use: designing your own learning, or curriculum, or onboarding.
  - How: teach students to think before teaching them what to think about; foreground the broad concept rather than burying it in information specific to that class alone.

## Key Concepts
- **The Flynn effect**: the increase in correct IQ test answers with each new generation across the twentieth century — about three points every ten years, documented in more than thirty countries.
- **Eduction**: working out guiding principles when given facts or materials, in the absence of instructions, even when you have never seen the material before — precisely what Raven's Progressive Matrices tests.
- **Raven's Progressive Matrices**: a "culturally reduced" test of the ability to make sense of complexity by completing patterns of abstract designs; the domain where generational gains were largest and least expected.
- **Utilitarian vs. conceptual spectacles**: Flynn's framing — utilitarian spectacles (the world seen through direct practical use) have been swapped for spectacles through which the world is classified by concepts.
- **Knowledge transfer**: the ability to apply knowledge to new situations and different domains, which modern work demands.
- **Cognitively flexible**: the trait correlated, across six industrializing nations, with exposure to modern work involving self-directed problem solving and nonrepetitive challenges.
- **Narrow critical competence**: Flynn's verdict on university departments — no sign that any department attempts to develop anything else.
- **Narrow vocational training** (Bryan Caplan): what students mostly get, for jobs few of them will ever have — three-quarters of American college graduates go into a career unrelated to their major.
- **"No tool is omnicompetent"** (Arnold Toynbee): no single instrument suffices for analyzing a world of technological and social change.

## Mental Models
- Think of abstraction as a scaffolding for connecting knowledge — it makes knowledge accessible and flexible, not just compressed.
- Use Fermi-izing whenever a number is asserted and you have no way to check it; it cuts through deceptive statistics fast.
- Think of the remote villager as the specialist's limit case: perfectly capable of learning *from* experience, and helpless at learning *without* it.
- Use the rule that the more constrained and repetitive a challenge, the more likely it will be automated — rewards accrue to whoever can carry conceptual knowledge from one domain into an entirely new one.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rushing into specialized research without learning the reasoning behind it**: Epstein ran a standard statistical test on a big database without understanding its limitations, produced an almost certain false positive, and was rewarded with a master's degree — a very wicked learning environment.
- **Burying broad concepts in course-specific minutiae**: Flynn indicts himself for it; professors are too eager to share favorite facts from acceleratingly narrow study.
- **Mistaking subtle value judgments for scientific conclusions, and correlation for causation**: on the causation question, students performed worse than random.
- **Assuming a top major confers general reasoning**: Flynn found the correlation between broad conceptual thinking and GPA was about zero.

## Worked Example
In 1931, Alexander Luria caught a natural experiment: Soviet collectivization was pushing remote Uzbek and Kyrgyz villages through centuries of social and economic change in a few years, while the most remote villages remained untouched — a control group. Luria's team asked people to sort skeins of colored wool. Collective farmers and lightly schooled students formed color groups easily. The most remote villagers refused: "None of them are the same, you can't put them together," and instead named them cotton in bloom, decayed teeth, sky, pistachio. A solid-line square and a dotted-line square were, to Alieva, a map and a watch — how could they go together? Asked which of hammer, saw, hatchet, and log does not belong, Rakmat objected that the tools are useless without the log. Told that three adults and one child form a group with an odd one out, he insisted the boy must stay: the adults are working, and the boy can do the running for them. Logic followed the same rule — villagers who could answer a syllogism about cotton growing (which they had grown) would not answer the identical syllogism about white bears in the Far North: "Your words can be answered only by someone who was there." Even the Ebbinghaus illusion failed to fool them, because they were not drawn to the holistic context. Then modernity arrived, and with it *eduction* — deriving principles from materials with no instructions — plus curiosity itself: where the isolated farmers could not produce a single question about the outside world, nineteen-year-old Siddakh asked how to make his fellow collective farmers better people and why the rich are rich and the poor poor. What this demonstrates: the abstract, category-shifting mind is not innate equipment but a product of modern work — which is exactly why the Flynn effect's largest gains appeared on Raven's matrices and on the most abstract vocabulary, and why the modern world both demands and enables range.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build a conceptual Swiss Army knife: acquire a small number of broadly transferable reasoning tools rather than more facts inside one field.
2. Learn Fermi-izing and use it routinely on numbers in news, advertising, and your own work.
3. Judge your education (or your team's training) by whether it transfers — can you apply your field's methods for evaluating truth to another area?
4. Prefer breadth of classification: practice shifting freely between attributes and categories rather than settling into one.
5. Expect constrained, repetitive work to be automated; position yourself where conceptual knowledge must travel between domains.
6. Learn to think before deciding what to think about — sequence broad reasoning ahead of narrow content.
7. Watch for the wicked learning environment you build for yourself when a flawed method is rewarded with credentials.
