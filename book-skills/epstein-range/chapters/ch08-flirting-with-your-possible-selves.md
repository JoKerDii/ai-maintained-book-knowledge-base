# Chapter 7: Flirting with Your Possible Selves

## Core Idea
You cannot introspect your way to the right career, because the person you are planning for does not exist yet; match quality is discovered by doing, not deciding. Treat identity as a hypothesis to be tested through short-term experiments, then revised.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Test-and-learn (vs. plan-and-implement)**: Herminia Ibarra's formulation — "Test-and-learn, not plan-and-implement." We maximize match quality by sampling activities, social groups, contexts, jobs, and careers, then reflecting and adjusting our personal narratives — and repeating.
  - When to use: any career, role, or life-direction decision where you feel obligated to name a destination first.
  - How: (1) Notice a flicker of dissatisfaction or a newly visible world. (2) Ask the small testable question — "Which among my various possible selves should I start to explore now? How can I do that?" — instead of "Who do I really want to become?" (3) Run a fast, cheap, temporary experiment: a fill-in role, a class, a side gig, a new network, a new role model. (4) Reflect, rewrite your story of me, then choose the next short-term plan.
  - Why it works: personality, values, and preferences change more than we predict, so a priori commitments are bets on a stranger. Failure mode: the industry of quizzes and strengths-finders that promises the answer through introspection alone — Ibarra: they "give people license to pigeonhole themselves."
- **Short-term planning (the Dark Horse strategy)**: Ogi Ogas and Todd Rose found dark horses ask "Here's who I am at the moment, here are my motivations, here's what I like to do, here are the opportunities. Which of these is the best match *right now*?"
  - When to use: whenever you feel behind relative to people who started earlier.
  - How: optimize for the present match, expect to switch, and formulate a long-term goal only *after* a period of discovery.
  - Why it works: fulfilled long-term goals are outputs of exploration, not inputs to it.
- **Work forward from promising situations (Paul Graham)**: Don't work back from a twenty-year goal; look at the options available now and choose those that give the most promising range of options afterward. Graham's name for the alternative: **premature optimization**.
- **If-then signatures / the context principle**: Ask not whether someone is gritty or introverted, but *when* they are. Diagnose a mismatch as a context problem before treating it as a character problem.

## Key Concepts
- **Match quality**: the degree of fit between the work someone does and who they actually are.
- **End of history illusion**: Dan Gilbert's finding that we recognize how much we changed in the past but believe we will barely change in the future — "works in progress claiming to be finished."
- **Dark Horse Project**: Rose and Ogas's study of fulfilled high achievers with winding paths; 45 of the first 50 apologized for a path that was actually the norm.
- **Standardization covenant**: Ogas's term for the cultural bargain trading self-exploration for a rigid early goal in exchange for the promise of stability.
- **Possible selves**: the multiple viable identities each person contains, knowable only through action — "Be a flirt with your possible selves."
- **Plan-and-implement**: the discredited model of making a long-term plan and executing without deviation.
- **Context principle**: Ogas and Rose's label for the fact that traits express differently across situations.
- **Cult of the head start**: the belief that dispensing with a long-term plan for short-term experiments cannot possibly make sense.

## Mental Models
- Use test-and-learn when the goal itself is uncertain; use plan-and-implement only when the destination is already validated by experience.
- Think of your career plan as having a shelf life measured in months, not decades — your personality changes most between eighteen and your late twenties, so specializing early means predicting match quality for a person who does not yet exist.
- Treat every new experience as a big basket: "You have to carry a big basket to bring something home."
- Think of yourself as a scientist of yourself — small testable questions beat one grand a priori answer.

## Anti-patterns
- **Introspection-only career choice (quizzes, strengths-finders)**: they freeze a moving target and ignore documented personal change across time and context.
- **Committing to a credential before knowing the fit**: nothing wrong with a law or medical degree, but it is riskier to commit *before* you know how it fits you.
- **Treating an early plan as binding ("don't give up on your dreams")**: premature optimization against a world whose jobs may not exist yet.
- **Reading a lack of drive as a lack of grit**: put the person in a fitting context and the same drive reappears.

## Worked Example
Frances Hesselbein had four professional positions, all president or CEO, never applied for one, and tried to turn down three. She dropped out of Junior Pitt when her father died, colored photographs in her husband's studio, and at thirty-four agreed to lead Girl Scout Troop 17 for six weeks — she stayed eight years. She chaired the local United Way as a volunteer, uniting Bethlehem Steel and the steelworkers' union, and Johnstown led the nation in per capita giving. At fifty-four she took her first professional job, agreeing to fix the local council's finances for six months only; she stayed four years. At sixty she declined the national CEO interview and only attended because her husband drove her there to decline in person — which is where, relaxed precisely because she didn't want the job, she described circular management, four age-targeted handbooks, and diversity as *the* primary organizational problem. Thirteen years as CEO: minority membership tripled, a quarter million members added, the cookie business past $300 million, and Peter Drucker naming her the best CEO in the country.

What it demonstrates: a vocation reached entirely by short-term planning. "I was unaware that I was being prepared. I did not intend to become a leader, I just learned by doing what was needed at the time." Every job she nearly refused supplied a capability — resourcefulness from the photography business, shared leadership from a troop where she had less experience than her charges, stakeholder alignment from United Way — that only resolved into a career in retrospect. No introspective exercise available to her at seventeen, thirty-four, or even fifty-four would have produced that path.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask "which possible self should I explore now?" instead of "who do I want to become?"
2. Run short, cheap, reversible experiments; formulate long-term goals only after a period of discovery.
3. Discount your own forecasts about your future preferences — the end of history illusion means you will change more than you predict.
4. Choose the option that maximizes your range of promising options afterward, not the one that shortens the path to a goal you set while inexperienced.
5. When motivation is absent, change the context before changing your self-assessment.
6. Say yes to temporary and "just filling in" roles; they are the cheapest possible match-quality tests.
7. Reflect deliberately after each experiment and rewrite your narrative — "I know who I am when I see what I do."
