# Part Four: Be a Leader — Change People Without Giving Offense or Arousing Resentment

## Core Idea
When you must change someone's behavior, do it so their pride stays intact and their motivation rises. Every correction is wrapped in appreciation, aimed indirectly, and framed so the person *wants* to change.

## Frameworks Introduced (the Nine Principles of Leadership)
- **Principle 1 — Begin with praise and honest appreciation.** It's easier to hear hard things after a dose of sincere praise — like the dentist who numbs before drilling.
- **Principle 2 — Call attention to mistakes indirectly.** Don't lecture; let people infer the correction. (See the "but → and" example below.)
- **Principle 3 — Talk about your own mistakes before criticizing the other person.** Admitting you've made the same error yourself makes the fault easier to hear.
- **Principle 4 — Ask questions instead of giving direct orders.** "What if we tried this?" invites cooperation; "Do it this way" invites resentment. Questions preserve dignity and initiative.
- **Principle 5 — Let the other person save face.** Even when you're right and they're wrong, protect their dignity publicly. Humiliation destroys; face-saving preserves the relationship.
- **Principle 6 — Praise the slightest improvement and praise every improvement.** "Be hearty in your approbation and lavish in your praise." Specific praise reinforces the exact behavior you want repeated.
- **Principle 7 — Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.** Assign a virtue and people strain to fulfill it: "You've always been dependable, so I know you'll handle this well."
- **Principle 8 — Use encouragement; make the fault seem easy to correct.** Tell them the thing is easy and you have faith in their ability; they'll practice to excel.
- **Principle 9 — Make the other person happy about doing what you suggest.** Frame the request so it aligns with their benefit and self-image; make them glad to comply.

## Key Concepts
- **Praise-sandwich, done right** — genuine praise → indirect correction, *without* the word "but" severing the two.
- **Face-saving** — protecting someone's dignity is often more important than being proven right.
- **Reputation-to-live-up-to** — give a person a good name and they'll work to deserve it.

## Mental Models
- Lead like a **coach, not a critic**: notice and name the smallest improvement instead of only flagging faults.
- Replace **orders with questions** — "Would it work to...?" turns a command into a shared decision.
- Think of every criticism as needing an **anesthetic (praise) and an exit ramp (make it seem easy)**.

## Anti-patterns
- **The "but" sandwich** — "Great work, *but*..." makes the praise sound like a set-up and cancels it.
- **Public correction** — costing someone face guarantees resentment even when you're right.
- **Direct orders** — bruise pride and kill initiative; people resist being commanded.

## Worked Example
**Change "but" to "and" (Principle 2).** To a child raising their grades: "We're really proud of you, Johnnie, for raising your grades this term — *but* if you'd worked harder on algebra, the results would have been better." The moment Johnnie hears "but," he doubts the praise was sincere. Rewrite it: "We're really proud of you, Johnnie, for raising your grades this term, *and* by keeping up the same effort next term your algebra grade can be right up with the others." Now the praise stands, and the correction lands as encouragement. One three-letter word decides between offense and cooperation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Always open a correction with sincere, specific praise — then correct indirectly.
2. Never use "but" to pivot from praise to criticism; use "and."
3. Ask questions and let people save face rather than issuing orders or humiliating them.
4. Praise every small step and give people a fine reputation to live up to.
5. Make the fault seem easy to fix and make the person glad to do what you ask.
