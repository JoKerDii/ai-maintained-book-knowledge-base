# Chapter 8: Want Stronger Relationships at Work? Change the Way You Listen
*Manbir Kaur*

## Core Idea
The *attitude* you bring to listening — protect, judge, or co-create — determines the neurochemistry of the conversation for both people. Choosing to listen to co-create is what converts an exchange from threat-and-cortisol into trust-and-oxytocin.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Listening Attitudes** (from Judith Glaser's conversational intelligence):
  - **Listening to protect** — you're on the defensive, guarding your identity and your space. Effect on the speaker: they feel ignored.
  - **Listening to accept or reject** — you're listening in order to judge. Effect: the speaker feels labeled. Common in team meetings.
  - **Listening to co-create** — you're listening to connect, with psychological safety, beginning from an intent to explore and understand.
  - When to use co-create: any conversation where the relationship matters more than winning the point.
  - How: hold four questions in mind — *What are they trying to say? What are they thinking? What are they expecting to explore with me? How can I connect to their world?*
  - Why it works: openness recruits the neurochemistry of both bodies; you stop being protective and start building trust.

- **The four practices of a good listener**:
  1. **Join with the right intention** — enter believing they have something of value to say and that it matters to give them the chance.
  2. **Use both your head and your heart** — understand not just the *what* but the *why*; connect with the reasons and the emotions behind the words.
  3. **Put yourself in the other person's shoes** — listen with empathy and compassion. Even if your constraints prevent you from helping, you can still listen thoughtfully.
  4. **Show that you're engaged** — full attention, and open-ended questions that deepen understanding.

## Key Concepts
- **The 0.07-second impression** — the brain forms an initial judgment of the other person's intent, and whether to trust them, in roughly 0.07 seconds; your actual response is then shaped by that snap read.
- **Protection mode** — the amygdala's threat response, releasing cortisol; you become reactive, emotional, impulsive, and read situations more negatively.
- **Bonding response** — cooperation and understanding release oxytocin, which reinforces connection and mutual trust.
- **Psychological safety** — the precondition that lets co-creative listening happen at all.
- **Co-creation** — treating the conversation as joint exploration rather than transmission or evaluation.

## Mental Models
- **Think of your listening attitude as a chemical choice.** You cannot control what the other person says, but by choosing how you listen you influence the neurochemical reaction in both brains.
- **Use "the other person also thinks nobody understands their situation" as a reset.** The feeling that your constraints are uniquely invisible is universal — which means it applies to them too.
- **Treat listening as an information gain, not a concession.** Listening yields fresh perspectives; "the more actively you listen, the more you will be heard."

## Anti-patterns
- **Half-attending** — one earbud in, half your mind on the music and half on your own schedule; the speaker reads it instantly as being ignored.
- **Leading with the problem** — opening a conversation with the defect before any greeting or context.
- **Ambushing someone on arrival** — raising an urgent demand the moment a person walks in, before they've landed.
- **Silent noncompliance** — shaking your head and walking on rather than responding; it reads as refusal, not as processing.
- **Assigning blame instead of checking your own inputs** — faulting the output without asking whether your instructions were clear.

## Worked Example
Roan arrives at work with earbuds in, listening to his morning mix. His manager Andy intercepts him at his desk. Roan removes *one* earbud. Andy: "There's a problem with the report you submitted yesterday. I think it needs to be reworked. Could you get to it ASAP?" Roan thinks, "Was that really the first thing Andy could say to me this morning?" — and with half his attention still on the music and half on rearranging his morning, shakes his head without a word and walks on. Andy, offended, thinks: "Was he even listening?" Given a 0.07-second trust judgment on both sides, this conversation is already lost.

The rewrite, practice by practice:

- **Right intention** — Roan removes *both* earbuds immediately, so Andy doesn't feel ignored. Andy, instead of assigning blame, sets context and asks whether any of his instructions were unclear.
- **Head and heart** — Roan: "I'm sorry that the report didn't meet your expectations. Please help me understand how I can make improvements." Andy: "I am sorry for catching you now, as I see you're just getting into work, but this is really urgent. Do you have a minute?"
- **Their shoes** — Roan: "Of course I understand the client needs the report urgently. I'll see what I can do to improve it right away." Andy: "I know you worked hard on the report, but it's not what the client wants. Maybe my instructions weren't clear. Can I help you with additional information?"
- **Show engagement** — Roan asks open-ended: "I may need help. Could you give me more information so I can make the report stronger? What did the client not like about the report?"

Same facts, same urgency, opposite neurochemistry.

## Key Takeaways
1. Name your listening attitude before you respond — protect, judge, or co-create.
2. Remove the literal and figurative earbuds; partial attention is read as rejection.
3. Ask "why," not just "what" — the emotion behind the message is half the content.
4. Acknowledge the other person's constraint out loud, even when you can't relieve it.
5. Apologize for the timing when you must interrupt; it costs one sentence and preserves the relationship.
6. Close with an open-ended question; it converts a correction into a collaboration.
