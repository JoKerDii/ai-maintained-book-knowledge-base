# Chapter 9: How to Navigate Conflict with a Coworker
*Amy Gallo*

## Core Idea
You cannot transform a difficult colleague, but you can change your own perception, goals, and behavior — seven strategies that make interactions more tolerable, build interpersonal resilience, and sometimes resolve the conflict outright.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Seven Strategies for Difficult Colleagues**
  1. **Remember that your perspective is just one among many** — resist naive realism.
  2. **Be aware of your biases** — fundamental attribution error, confirmation bias, affinity bias.
  3. **Don't make it "me against them"** — work the third entity.
  4. **Know your goal** — write it down, refer to it before interactions.
  5. **Avoid workplace venting and gossip — mostly** — choose confidants deliberately.
  6. **Experiment to find what works** — run timeboxed behavioral tests.
  7. **Be — and stay — curious** — replace conclusions with genuine questions.

- **The Third Entity**
  - When to use: whenever the conflict has become personal and polarized.
  - How: stop imagining two parties and imagine three — you, your colleague, and *the dynamic between you*. That third thing may be concrete (a decision you must make together, an assignment to finish) or general (ongoing rivalry, bad blood from a failed project). Then make progress on the third thing rather than trying to change the person.
  - Why it works: it converts "problematic coworker" into "colleague with whom I share a problem to be solved."

- **Perspective-challenging questions**: *How do I know that what I believe is true? What if I'm wrong? How would I change my behavior? What assumptions have I made? How would someone with different values and experiences see things?* The answers matter less than the act of asking.

- **Flip it to test it** (Kristen Pressner): if your colleague were a different gender, race, or sexual orientation, or held a different place in the hierarchy, would you make the same assumptions, say the same things, treat them the same way?

- **Experiment design for behavior change**: pick two or three methods to test; define exactly what you'll do differently; set a time period; observe. Then keep, tweak, refresh, or abandon. Treat every experiment as informative even when it fails.

## Key Concepts
- **Naive realism** — the belief that you see the issue objectively and correctly, so anyone who disagrees is uninformed, irrational, or biased.
- **Fundamental attribution error** — attributing others' behavior to their personality and your own to your circumstances.
- **Confirmation bias** — reading new events as proof of what you already believe, which makes it progressively harder for the other person to disprove you.
- **Affinity bias** — the unconscious pull toward people similar to you in appearance, beliefs, and background; research shows we're less comfortable with, and less willing to work with, colleagues unlike us.
- **Interpersonal resilience** — feeling less stressed during conflict and bouncing back faster afterward.
- **Interrupting the conflict pattern of the past** (Jennifer Goldman-Wetzler) — breaking a stuck loop by doing something the other person doesn't expect.
- **Ego/reputation protection** — the short-term goal ("I need to win this argument") that displaces the long-term one (behaving honorably, preserving collegiality), especially under stress.

## Mental Models
- **Think of the seesaw, not the storm cloud.** Andre saw his colleague Emilia — who met every idea of his with reasons it would fail — as a dark cloud to his bright sun, an image that made him brace for battle every time. Reimagining the conflict as a seesaw, where opposite ends could work together toward balance, let him see her as a collaborator.
- **Use "Certainty is the enemy of change"** (Salvador Minuchin). Curiosity wards off confirmation bias, prevents stereotyping, and lets you meet a hard situation with creativity rather than fight or flight.
- **Stop litigating the facts.** You and your colleague don't need consensus on what happened or who's to blame; shift the focus to what should happen going forward.
- **It's fine to set your sights low.** A merely functional relationship — not dreading their name in your inbox, not losing sleep — is a legitimate goal.

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing your read is the objective one**: in the classic tapping study, people tapping out well-known songs like "Happy Birthday" predicted listeners would identify them ~50% of the time; the actual rate was **2.5%**.
- **Vowing to simply stop caring**: Gallo's own attempt held in a good week and collapsed the rest — clenched teeth, eye-rolls, complaining to coworkers.
- **Habitual venting**: it feels validating (adrenaline, dopamine, in-group belonging) and can deter selfish behavior, but it entrenches confirmation bias — occasional missteps get recast as inherent traits and "Michael is difficult" hardens into a storyline. It can also make *you* the one who looks unprofessional or difficult.
- **Repeating a tactic that isn't working**: if post-meeting confirmation emails haven't fixed a colleague's follow-through, sending more won't.
- **Resignation** — "it's always going to be this way," "that person will never change."
- **Trusting your comfort as a signal**: Gallo hesitated to challenge a Black woman client's ideas — part of her job as a consultant — out of fear of a strong reaction, despite that client never having raised her voice. She had absorbed the "angry Black woman" stereotype.

## Worked Example
Early in her career Gallo took a job reporting to a manager she calls Elise, widely reputed to be difficult. Gallo prided herself on getting along with anyone. Two months later she was ready to quit.

The specifics: Elise worked long days and weekends and expected her team to match. Her assumptions about a day's output were wildly unreasonable. She would follow up at 8:30 a.m. on a request made at 6:00 the previous evening. She disparaged teammates in front of Gallo, questioning their work ethic and commitment. She scrolled through colleagues' calendars to point out how little they'd accomplished on meeting-free days.

Gallo's coping strategy — stop caring, be kind anyway — worked in a good week and failed most weeks. The moment Elise insinuated she wasn't working hard enough, Gallo clenched her teeth, rolled her eyes behind her back, and complained to coworkers. The point of the story is that willpower and good character are *not* the mechanism; the seven strategies are, because they change what you attend to and what you're aiming at rather than asking you to suppress a reaction.

Applied concretely: arguing with an insecure boss about which metrics to report to senior leadership, your written goals might be (1) find stats you can both live with, (2) ensure the senior team knows about your expertise, (3) find a way to avoid heated disagreements before big meetings in future. Research indicates people who vividly describe or picture goals are 1.2–1.4 times as likely to achieve them, and handwritten objectives are more likely to be realized.

Curiosity in practice: when Jada complains again that she does more work than anyone, don't think "here we go with Jada's attitude." Ask: What's going on with her? What have I missed before? Who gets along well with Jada, and how do they interact? Have there been times she was more cooperative — what was different then?

## Key Takeaways
1. Assume your view is a view; ask the challenge questions even if the answers are inconclusive.
2. Audit for fundamental attribution error, confirmation bias, and affinity bias by name, and use "flip it to test it."
3. Reframe the conflict as a shared third problem rather than a person to fix.
4. Write down your goals and read them before each interaction; low-ambition goals are legitimate.
5. Vet your confidants — constructive, in your corner, willing to disagree with you, discreet.
6. Run small timeboxed experiments (e.g., "for two weeks I'll ignore the tone and respond to the underlying message"); abandon what doesn't move.
7. Stay curious and hopeful; when nothing works, cut your losses and protect your career and well-being.
