# Chapter 11: The Science of Strong Business Writing
*Bill Birchard*

## Core Idea
Good writing physically rewards the reader's brain — dopamine in the reward circuit, opioids in reward hot spots. Eight properties, the **Eight S's**, reliably trigger that response, which makes writing choices far more objective than the "innate talent" story suggests.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Eight S's of satisfying writing**: **Simplicity, Specificity, Surprise, Stirring language, Seductiveness, Smart thinking, Social content, Storytelling.**
  - When to use: revising anything from a one-line email to a board report. Diagnose which S is missing rather than "making it better."
  - How, per S:
    - **Simplicity** — short sentences, familiar words, clean syntax; raises *processing fluency*. Cut extraneous words, use the active voice, and drill to what's salient while scrapping tangential detail.
    - **Specificity** — concrete words activate more visual and motor neurons than general ones ("pelican" over "bird," "wipe" over "clean"). Also: coin a memorable shorthand phrase.
    - **Surprise** — the brain constantly predicts the next word; confirming the prediction is fine but forgettable. Novelty makes a message stick, and unusual wordplay works too.
    - **Stirring language** — emotional connotation is processed within **200 milliseconds**, faster than meaning; feeling comes first, reason follows, and meaning is the combination. Prefer emotionally packed verbs and metaphor.
    - **Seductiveness** — exploit *anticipatory utility*. Put readers in a state of uncertainty, then lead them somewhere better.
    - **Smart thinking** — engineer an "aha." Draw fresh distinctions; phrase a pragmatic message so it also evokes a universal truth.
    - **Social content** — passages about people activate social-signal regions, which in turn trigger the reward circuit. Show traces of yourself; humanize the topic; use the second person.
    - **Storytelling** — combines most of the other elements; the single highest-leverage move.
  - Why it works: each element triggers the same neural responses as other pleasurable stimuli — "well-executed prose makes us feel pleasure, which makes us want to keep reading."

- **The writer's Golden Rule**: *Reward readers as you would yourself.*

## Key Concepts
- **Reward circuit** — a cluster of midbrain neurons that lights up in response to good prose, as visible on MRI and PET. Berridge: it handles not just sensory cues but social and cultural rewards too.
- **Processing fluency** — how little brainpower the reader must spend to extract your meaning.
- **Anticipatory utility** — the pleasure of expectation itself; people are often happier planning a vacation than after taking it, and readers' reward circuitry peaks *several seconds before* the high points of emphatic poetic lines.
- **Embodied language** — neurons enact word meanings; we "taste," "feel," and "see" traces of the real thing.
- **Attentional blink** — the momentary lapse that causes people to miss words in a list. Emotionally significant words produce **no blinks**.
- **Neural coupling** (Uri Hasson) — when a story begins, listeners' brains glow in a pattern that mirrors the storyteller's exactly.

## Mental Models
- **Think of every word as a stimulus posing three questions to the reader's brain**: Does this promise value? Will I like it? Can I learn from it?
- **Use simplicity as a persuasion tool, not just a courtesy.** Kolmogorov proved people infer that simpler patterns yield better predictions, explanations, and decisions — so "you're more persuasive when you reduce overdressed ideas to their naked state."
- **Get your feelings straight along with your facts before composing.** Zeal shows through; if you express emotion, readers feel it.
- **Assume the reader is racing to read people, not prose.** Van Overwalle found readers infer the goals of people they read about in under 350 ms and discern character traits within 650 ms.

## Anti-patterns
- **Clauses nested in the middle of sentences**: measurably slower to read, more comprehension errors.
- **Passive voice**: "Profits are loved by investors" versus "Investors love profits" — swapping the standard verb/object positions can cut comprehension accuracy by **10%** and add a tenth of a second per sentence.
- **The exhaustive approach**: listing every pro and con for every option instead of pitching the top two with their principal pluses and minuses.
- **Abstraction where a person belongs**: "trucking disconnect" instead of mixed signals between the driver and the dispatcher.
- **Impersonal third person in technical explanation**: Mayer's UCSB experiments paired 100 words of narration with simple animations of the respiratory system; listeners who heard "*your* diaphragm… *your* lungs" scored significantly higher on a learning test than those who heard "*the* diaphragm… *the* lungs."
- **Relying on logic to persuade**: emotion is processed first and fastest.
- **No stories**: Murphy's crowdfunding study found richer narratives earned higher marks for entrepreneur credibility and business legitimacy, and more willingness to invest and to share. "No stories, no great funding success."

## Worked Example
Before-and-after rewrites, one per S, drawn from the article:

| Weak | Rewritten | S invoked |
|---|---|---|
| "Profits are loved by investors." | "Investors love profits." | Simplicity |
| "We're facing strong competition." | Bezos: "Third-party sellers are kicking our first-party butt. Badly." | Specificity |
| "challenge the competition" | "outwit rivals" | Stirring |
| "promote innovation" | "prize ingenuity" | Stirring |
| "What a very good idea!" | "What a gem of an idea!" | Stirring (metaphor) |
| "Be careful what you say" | "Watch your back." | Stirring (metaphor) |
| "trucking disconnect" | mixed signals between the driver and dispatcher | Social |
| "During inhaling, *the* diaphragm moves down…" | "…*your* diaphragm… *your* lungs" | Social (second person) |

Seductiveness, worked end to end — Steve Jobs's 2005 Stanford commencement address opens: "I never graduated from college. Truth be told, this is the closest I've ever gotten to a college graduation. Today I want to tell you three stories from my life. That's it. No big deal. Just three stories." The withheld content creates anticipation before a single story begins. The business translation: start a report with a question, pose the customer problem as a conundrum, position product development work as solving a mystery.

Smart thinking, worked: Ginni Rometty's fresh distinction — "It will not be a world of man versus machine; it will be a world of man plus machine." And Max De Pree, aiming a pragmatic message at a universal truth: "The first responsibility of a leader is to define reality. The last is to say thank you. In between the two, the leader must become a servant and a debtor" — wisdom that extends past managers to parents, teachers, and coaches.

Surprise, evidenced: Dessalles gave participants unfinished narratives (a stolen car listed online, the seller's phone number traced) with candidate endings — an office colleague, a brother's colleague, someone in the neighborhood. For **17 of 18 stories** the vast majority chose the most unexpected ending. And Berger and Milkman, examining nearly 7,000 online *New York Times* articles, found those rated surprising were **14% more likely** to make the "most-emailed" list.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose weak writing by asking which of the eight S's is absent.
2. Simplify for persuasion, not just clarity — simpler reads as truer, and Okuhara's 400-reader study showed the simpler version also raised readers' self-efficacy.
3. Choose the concrete word; specificity recruits more of the reader's brain, and Tettamanti showed sentences like "I kick the ball" fire the reader's leg regions.
4. Lead with feeling — emotional connotation lands in 200 ms, before meaning does.
5. Withhold something. Build anticipation before you deliver.
6. Put people in the writing, use "you," and let your own voice show.
7. Tell a story whenever you can; it bundles most of the other seven.
