# Chapter 5: How to Get the Help You Need
*Heidi Grant*

## Core Idea
People are far more willing to help than you expect — but *how* you ask determines whether they want to. Use three reinforcements (**in-group, positive identity, effectiveness**) to let helpers feel autonomous and effective, and avoid the intuitive phrasings that quietly kill willingness.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Reinforcements** — cues to embed in specific requests *and* in day-to-day interactions to prime people for helpfulness.
  - **In-group**: assure the helper you're on the same team and that the team matters. How: use the word "together"; cite a common goal, enemy, or trait; but best of all, highlight *shared experience*, not shared category. Not "We're the only two women on the team" (trait) — instead "Have you noticed that we get interrupted all the time?" (experience).
  - **Positive identity**: make them see they are uniquely placed to help *and* that they are a helpful person, not just a person who might help. How: use identity nouns over verbs — "be a generous donor" beats "donate"; children as young as three do more when told they can "be a helper" versus "help." Tailor the identity to the person — pro-environment appeals land differently for liberals ("care for the natural world") and conservatives ("show your love for your country"). Gratitude also builds identity: Boomerang's study of 350,000 emails found "Thanks in advance" and "Thanks" drew 63–66% response rates versus 51–54% for "Best," "Regards," and "Cheers."
  - **Effectiveness**: be explicit about what you need and what impact it will have. Feeling effective — knowing your actions produced the result you intended — is arguably the fundamental human motivation. Promise to follow up and actually do it. Let people choose *how* they help and accept alternatives to your original ask.

- **What Helpers Need** (the four preconditions — check all four before blaming the person who didn't help)
  1. **The helper must realize that you need help.** People are preoccupied, especially in bad moods or positions of power.
  2. **The helper must believe that you want help.** They may see the need but fear misreading it, or assume you'd rather go it alone.
  3. **The helper must take responsibility for helping.** Diffusion of responsibility is the killer — the classic error is asking via group email. Ask directly, with unique appeals.
  4. **The helper must be able to provide what you need.** Be explicit and detailed, keep the request reasonable, stay open to a different form of help.

## Key Concepts
- **Reinforcements** — cues embedded in a request that make helping feel chosen and rewarding.
- **Diffusion of responsibility** — when a request goes to a group, no individual owns it.
- **Compliance gap** — Vanessa Bohns found actual help rates averaged **48% higher** than help seekers expected; we also underestimate how much effort helpers put in.
- **Social threats of asking** — uncertainty, risk of rejection, potential status loss, relinquished autonomy; these activate the same brain regions as physical pain.
- **Agency** — the helper's sense that the decision is theirs; the thing every unproductive phrasing destroys.

## Mental Models
- **Think of a request as an opportunity you're offering, not a debt you're incurring.** A 2017 Swiss study found people who merely pledge to spend a small amount on someone else feel happier than those planning to indulge themselves. There is "no better way to make someone feel good about themselves than to ask" for help.
- **Use "together" as a performance multiplier.** Carr and Walton found participants who believed they were working in tandem with others persisted **48% longer**, solved more problems correctly, and felt less depleted — while still working alone.
- **Assume 75–90% of workplace help comes from direct appeals.** Waiting to be noticed is not a strategy.

## Anti-patterns
- **"May I ask you a favor?"**: the preface makes people feel trapped before they know what's coming.
- **Profuse apology ("I feel terrible asking you for this")**: makes the experience feel negative for the helper.
- **Emphasizing reciprocity ("I'll help you if you help me")**: people dislike being indebted and dislike purely transactional exchanges.
- **Minimizing your need ("I don't normally ask," "it's just a tiny thing")**: implies the help is trivial or unnecessary, which strips its effectiveness.
- **Any language of obligation** — that you or someone else is instructing them, that they *should*, that they have no choice.
- **The group email ask**: guarantees no one owns it.
- **Never closing the loop**: helpers who don't see their impact don't feel effective, and won't re-engage.

## Worked Example
The head of product development at a learning software company was being crushed by sales, which kept promising highly customized orders on near-impossible schedules. He had pleaded to be included in client discussions and was ignored — sales saw him as a brake on their success. Both sides thought they were serving the company.

His fresh approach used all three reinforcements. First, he set up a meeting with sales leaders to walk through the product development process — because he realized most of them had no idea what the work involved, meaning they didn't understand *why* their help was needed (precondition 1). Second, in every interaction he stressed a shared goal: pleasing the customer to earn repeat business — **in-group**, making it obvious everyone was on the same side. Third, he began describing sales leaders as "the protectors of customer experience" who wielded real power over the future of the brand — **positive identity**, which shifted how they saw their own work. Finally, whenever a salesperson did include him in the proposal process, he followed up specifically to say how much it had mattered to the delivery's success — **effectiveness**, letting them see their help land. Over time, relations between the teams improved dramatically, and the company saw gains in both client satisfaction and profitability.

Grant's personal parallel: on a morning when she declined a journal review, ignored a school volunteer email, and refused to fold the laundry, she eagerly agreed to help a graduate-school friend assemble a complicated IKEA bookshelf — because the friend was long-standing and enjoyable (in-group), because Grant is her reliable go-to for such projects (effectiveness), and because the friend always closes with "Heidi, thank you. You are always so helpful and generous" (positive identity).

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask — directly, individually, and specifically; most people say yes far more often than you predict.
2. Never signal obligation; the helper must feel in control of the decision.
3. Build in-group through shared *experience*, which beats shared category or trait.
4. Frame the helper's identity as a noun ("be a helper"), tailored to what they value.
5. State the impact up front and report the outcome afterward.
6. Let people help their own way; accept a substitute for what you asked.
7. Front-load gratitude — it raises response rates and keeps helpers invested, as long as you focus on their generosity rather than your gain.
