# Chapter 7: When Diversity Meets Feedback
*Erin Meyer*

## Core Idea
Diversity raises the odds that feedback will be read as hostility, because cultures, genders, and generations hold different expectations for how feedback is delivered and by whom. Adjust delivery to the recipient, use the three A's, and build regular feedback loops so candor and DEI converge instead of colliding.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three A's of Feedback** — the universal filter.
  - **Assist**: give it with the genuine intention of helping the other person succeed, never to offload your own frustration.
  - **Actionable**: if it isn't crystal clear what they can do to improve, keep it to yourself.
  - **Ask**: solicit feedback on *your* work before offering yours — unless they explicitly requested it, in which case go ahead.
  - When to use: before any unsolicited feedback, and mandatorily across power or identity differences.
  - Why the third A matters: giving advice confers power. Asking first hands the power to the other person before you reverse it, so your input reads as help rather than dominance.

- **Upgraders and downgraders** — the cross-cultural calibration tool.
  - **Upgraders** intensify criticism: "absolutely," "totally," "completely" ("This is absolutely inappropriate"). Common in direct cultures.
  - **Downgraders** soften it: "kind of," "a little," "maybe" — plus deliberate understatement ("We are not quite there yet" for "this is nowhere close"; "This is just my opinion" for "this is obvious to everyone"). Common in indirect cultures.
  - How: listen for which your counterparts use, then match. The rule is not "Do unto others as you would have them do unto you" but **"Do unto others as they would have done unto themselves."**

- **Three feedback-loop mechanisms**, sequenced by team maturity:
  1. **One-to-one chats** — for teams that have never exchanged feedback. Each person meets individually with each teammate within the month; the feedback stays between the two.
  2. **"Speed-dating" sessions** — for closer teams. Pairs get six minutes, three minutes each way, then rotate. At the end, everyone reports one piece of feedback they'll act on.
  3. **Live 360-degree feedback circles** — for mature teams with strong relationships. Over a meal, each person receives feedback in turn from every colleague around the circle; the recipient listens and says thank you. Each ends by naming one key takeaway.
  - Structure the content either as *one thing done well + one thing to improve*, or as **start / stop / continue**.

- **Cortex vs. amygdala** — the delivery constraint. The cortex loves candid feedback; the amygdala hears "Danger! I might get kicked out of my tribe," floods with cortisol and adrenaline, and produces either *fight* ("I'm not the problem — you are") or *flight* ("Thanks, so helpful," followed by never speaking to you again). Your delivery's job is to help the cortex override the amygdala.

## Key Concepts
- **Radical candor** (Kim Scott) — the argument that even "obnoxiously aggressive" feedback beats "ruinous empathy," i.e., withholding what would help.
- **Radical transparency** (Ray Dalio) — employees rating and commenting on each other in publicly shared documents during the meeting itself.
- **Know-it-all vs. learn-it-all culture** (Satya Nadella) — status-seeking expertise display versus the humility to listen to criticism and diverse views.
- **Status incongruence** — when the status your role carries in society doesn't match the role you play in the current context (e.g., a younger manager supervising an older worker). German research with 8,000 employees found a measurable negative effect on older subordinates' happiness; the role reversal keeps implying they "failed to keep pace."
- **Manvising** — men giving women advice they neither asked for nor want. Men are **five times** as likely to give unrequested advice to women as vice versa; over 90% of women report recently receiving it, while most men don't think they do it.
- **Feedback as power transfer** — spontaneous feedback raises recipients' heart rates to levels indicating moderate or extreme duress; conversely, the *giver* feels more powerful (library-employee and student-advice studies both confirm).
- **Generational feedback expectations** — Boomers: formal, documented, annual, boss-to-subordinate (they invented the performance review). Gen X: informal, don't want to wait a year, first to give upward feedback, want it instantly. Millennials: expect frequent praise but are *less* sensitive to criticism than older colleagues (2019 research); the "snowflake" label is misleading — high self-esteem correlates with emotional resilience. Zoomers: raised on constant real-time social feedback, expect it frequently and in all directions.

## Mental Models
- **Think of a diverse team as raising the base rate of misread intent.** The question the recipient is silently asking is: do you mean to help me, or to embarrass me, take my job, or usurp my power?
- **Use explicit team norms as the leveler.** When expectations differ by generation, don't guess — state how and when feedback is given, so everyone converges on a common platform.
- **Treat regular loops as tooth-brushing.** Scheduled mutual exchange reframes feedback from condescension or malevolence into an integral part of the job.

## Anti-patterns
- **Applying your own culture's feedback norms abroad**: what is constructive in one culture reads as destructive in another.
- **Public criticism with others copied**: the specific behavior Olga's boss called "indelicate."
- **Unsolicited real-time hallway feedback from a junior or cross-identity colleague**: "See the problem, correct the problem" works when you're the boss; otherwise it puts colleagues on the defensive, makes enemies, and can stunt your career.
- **Assuming stated willingness equals behavior**: over 90% of 3,000+ INSEAD executives said they'd give a colleague difficult feedback — and nearly all said their teammates wouldn't. Most managers say yes in the classroom and stay silent in real life.
- **Men offering women "helpful" polish unasked**: even genuinely useful, actionable, well-meant advice from a majority member to an underrepresented colleague reads as belittling.
- **Explicit "areas in need of improvement" plus written recap in indirect cultures**: Thai HR read it as bullying and as signaling distrust.

## Worked Example
Two mirror-image cultural failures.

*Olga*, a Ukrainian HR executive, moved to West Virginia. In her culture, "if there is a problem, we say it clearly… We don't talk about what we liked and appreciated before getting to the point." Cathy, responsible for payroll, kept making monthly errors. Olga called her in: "Cathy, this absolutely cannot continue. Your mistakes are creating big headaches." When the seasonal-employee manager emailed privately to complain, Olga replied-all, copying Cathy, and wrote: "You are right. This is completely unacceptable, and it won't happen again." Her boss then corrected *her* — he called it "indelicate," reported that Cathy was so upset she'd asked to change jobs, told Olga never to criticize someone's work with others copied, and suggested "might" and "should" in place of "must" and "can't."

*Jethro*, an American in Silicon Valley, gave video feedback to Thai colleagues using standard U.S. methods: specific comments, verbal then written, naming what worked, what was problematic, and exactly what to change. Bangkok HR accused him of bullying. The head of HR explained that explicitly naming "the area in need of improvement" already feels aggressive to a Thai recipient, and that recapping in writing signals you don't trust them or are building a case. Her prescription: **praise what was good, specifically, and say nothing about the bad** — the omission communicates it clearly enough. Say "I especially liked the examples you gave in the presentation last week," not "the examples from this morning's presentation were not very good."

*The gender case*: Cassandra, a marketing VP, had just aced a high-stakes presentation to 2,000 colleagues and was elated in the speakers' lounge when Miles offered: "Your presentation was 90% perfect. The audience was eating it up! I do think you spoke a little too fast, which made you sound nervous. Also, maybe your mouth was too close to the mic because your voice somehow sounded tinny." It was accurate, actionable, and timely — she had a second talk coming. She still felt like "an inexperienced child receiving coaching from a teacher," and physically shifted her chair away. Meyer's fix is one sentence: had Miles opened with "I'd love to hear any thoughts you have about my presentation this morning," Cassandra would have held the power first, and his advice would have landed as help.

*The generational case*: Richard, a business writer in his late fifties, took feedback from Connor, a talented writer in his mid-twenties: "This is all right, but you completely left your personality out. Your audience wants to feel that you're with them, but your individual voice is absent." Richard's first reaction was rejection — status incongruence plus a Boomer norm that non-boss feedback is inappropriate. But because the team had an explicit feedback culture, he reflected instead of dismissing. He concluded Connor's was the most valuable input he got: his journalist training had taught him to write himself out, which made the piece sterile, and Connor's generation of open self-disclosure was exactly what let him spot it. Richard rewrote it "infinitely better."

## Key Takeaways
1. Run every piece of unsolicited feedback through assist / actionable / ask.
2. Ask for feedback on yourself first — especially across gender and power lines; it redistributes the power the act of advising creates.
3. Match upgraders and downgraders to the recipient's culture, not your own comfort.
4. In indirect cultures, praise specifically and omit the negative; the silence carries the message.
5. Set explicit team norms for who gives feedback to whom, how, and how often — generational defaults otherwise collide silently.
6. Build scheduled loops (one-to-one → speed-dating → live 360) matched to your team's maturity, rather than relying on spontaneous real-time correction.
7. Remember that public exchange, done well, prevents backchannel whispering and normalizes feedback — everyone gets tough advice, so no one is singled out.
