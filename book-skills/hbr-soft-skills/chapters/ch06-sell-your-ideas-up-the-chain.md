# Chapter 6: How to Sell Your Ideas up the Chain of Command
*Ethan Burris*

## Core Idea
Managers are more receptive than employees assume — if you get inside their heads. Selling an idea upward means protecting your boss's ego, choosing a single frame that matches their regulatory focus, pre-solving implementation, bringing allies, and pitching the person who actually holds the decision rights.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The five moves for upward influence**: (1) Understand your manager's insecurities, (2) Avoid mixed messages, (3) Make implementation easy, (4) Leverage colleagues, (5) Pitch to the right person.

- **Promotion focus vs. Prevention focus** — the frame-selection framework.
  - When to use: every time you pitch upward; pick one frame *only*.
  - How: diagnose from tells. **Prevention focus** — concerned with obeying rules, SOPs, and policy; very careful outlining and executing plans; meticulous about details and not messing anything up; playing not to lose. **Promotion focus** — starts projects without always finishing; talks about what the future holds; lets others handle details; lets small mistakes slide; playing to win. Then pitch a *threat/loss avoided* to the prevention-focused boss and an *opportunity with big upside* to the promotion-focused one.
  - Why it works: tailoring to the manager's orientation raised endorsement likelihood by **15–18%** across studies of 800+ frontline managers.

- **The manager's three implementation questions** (address all three in the pitch):
  1. What financial and human resources will implementation require?
  2. How difficult will it be to enlist the help of others?
  3. Is it worth devoting time, energy, and political capital to?
  - How: describe how budgets and people can be redeployed without straining other work; name the allies you'd need and volunteer to recruit them; tie the idea to organizational values and strategy — often the very measures your manager is evaluated on.

- **Ego-protection tactics before and during the pitch**: build trust and goodwill in advance with genuine positive feedback and gratitude, delivered *long before* the pitch ("I really enjoyed that presentation"). Routinely help peers, so your suggestions read as organization-improving rather than self-serving. State benevolent motive explicitly — prefacing negative feedback with "I really want the best for you" helps avoid the likability penalty messengers of bad news pay. Approach **in private**: managers felt **30% less threatened** one-on-one than when suggestions came in front of others. Link the suggestion to the boss's own stated goals: "You've spoken before about your focus on intuitive design — here's my idea for improving the user-friendliness of X."

## Key Concepts
- **Voice** — offering solicited or unsolicited suggestions and feedback upward.
- **Mixed message** — combining upside-of-acting with risk-of-inaction; garners the *least* support of any framing.
- **Regulatory focus** — whether a person is oriented toward gains (promotion) or losses (prevention).
- **Decision rights** — who actually has the authority to act on the issue.
- **Likability penalty** — the cost paid by the bearer of unwelcome news; people disparage those who tell them what they don't want to hear.
- **Squeaky wheel problem** — the manager's first question is whether the issue is a real, widely felt problem or one enthusiast's hobbyhorse.
- **Manager insecurity** — with each one-point drop in confidence (five-point scale), managers were **35% less likely** to solicit advice; insecure managers rated speakers-up **21% more negatively** and implemented their ideas **14% less often**.

## Mental Models
- **Think about your boss's ego, not just your own risk.** Most people ask "will I look like a complainer?"; almost no one asks "how will this make my boss feel?"
- **Use allies as both credibility and armor.** Amplifying others' voices made pitchers **15–20% more influential**; speaking up *on behalf of others* made them **57% more influential**. Numbers also diffuse knee-jerk messenger-shooting.
- **Treat your manager as coconspirator, not obstacle.** When they lack authority, don't go around them — recruit them to help craft the pitch for the people who do have decision rights.
- **Use informal surfaces.** Cafeteria, elevator, holiday party — bosses who'd balk at a scheduled meeting find impromptu chats unthreatening.

## Anti-patterns
- **Framing with both opportunity and threat**: across five studies and an analysis of 850+ ideas from 350 hospital employees, dual frames forced managers into extra cognitive work to parse the problem, its severity, the solution, and why it beat the status quo — which invited scrutiny and led to rejection.
- **Going to the boss before your colleagues**: nearly 60% of people do this, despite the manager's first question being whether the issue affects many stakeholders.
- **Pitching someone without decision rights**: the restaurant worker who raised pay compression with a shift manager who had no control over compensation — corporate HR did — produced frustration, not change.
- **Going around your boss**: ill-advised in most cases, especially in political, top-down organizations or with a sensitive manager.
- **Public pitching**: raises threat by ~30%.
- **Ignoring execution hurdles**: guarantees a fast no even for high-value ideas.

## Worked Example
Three ER cases, same hospital environment, opposite outcomes.

*Failure 1*: A physician proposed managing patient flow better by adding nurses to triage. The benefit was real — patients get critical care faster. But the costs were steep and unexamined: added head count, scheduling complexity, and skilled-staff shortages elsewhere (ICU, operating rooms). He hadn't worked through those hurdles before pitching, and the idea was shot down immediately.

*Failure 2*: A nurse proposed a better system for psychiatric and intoxicated patients overburdening her ER. The plan required coordinating police, social services, and policymakers — each with different contacts inside the hospital. Her boss simply could not see a path to making it happen. Had she mapped how those relationships would be managed, it would likely have gained traction.

*Success*: The same hospital, the region's only Level 1 trauma center, drew constant media attention — reporters chasing sensational injuries, photographers stalking local celebrities — pulling managers, nurses, and doctors away from care. Instead of proposing more PR staff or an elaborate media protocol, one physician suggested erecting a **privacy barrier at the ambulance entrance**. A few thousand dollars, zero staff time, problem gone. The idea won because it answered all three implementation questions before they were asked.

Framing corollary: an employee arguing to keep the team remote post-pandemic should pitch real-estate cost savings to a P&L-focused company, or eliminated commutes to one that touts wellness and rewards engagement. A study led by David Mayer found that in values-driven organizations, employees who invoked company values while advocating were **24% more successful**.

## Key Takeaways
1. Pick one frame — opportunity *or* threat — matched to your boss's promotion or prevention focus.
2. Build goodwill and gratitude well before you need it; it should never read as buttering up.
3. Pitch in private, and tie the idea to something your boss has already said they care about.
4. Answer resources, coalition difficulty, and political cost before being asked.
5. Socialize the idea with colleagues first; bring their support, or let a better-positioned colleague pitch it — ideally one who won't benefit.
6. Check who holds the decision rights; if it isn't your boss, enlist your boss to reach whoever does.
7. Remember the baseline: 70% of employees aren't comfortable raising important issues, and 85% withhold ideas out of fear — so a well-constructed pitch is a rare advantage.
