# Chapter 10: Coaching for Change
*Richard Boyatzis, Melvin Smith, and Ellen Van Oosten*

## Core Idea
Lasting change comes from **coaching with compassion** — helping someone envision their ideal self and pursue it — not from **coaching for compliance**, which drills into problems and targets and triggers a stress response that blocks learning. Your job is to help, not to guide, lead, push, or pull.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Intentional Change Theory** (Boyatzis, 1970) — the four elements:
  1. **Ideal self** — who you wish to *be* and what you want to *do* in work and life.
  2. **Real self** — the gaps to fill *and* the strengths that will help you fill them.
  3. **Learning agenda** — a road map turning aspirations into reality.
  4. **Experimenting and practicing** — new behaviors and new roles.
  - When to use: any change from "I'm unsatisfied in my career" down to "I'd like to interact with others differently."

- **The three-phase coaching process**: **Spot the opportunity** → **Set the groundwork** → **See things through**.

- **Coaching with compassion vs. coaching for compliance**
  - *Compliance*: drill into the problem, offer advice and solutions, emphasize targets and challenges to overcome. Works for a specific predetermined goal like earning a promotion. Fails for broad behavioral goals (becoming a dynamic leader, a great listener, finding work/life balance).
  - *Compassion*: genuine care and concern, curiosity, exploratory open-ended questions, the person's own vision as context.
  - Evidence: with Anthony Jack, students coached for compliance felt "guilty and self-conscious"; students coached toward personal dreams found it "inspiring and caring," and neuroimaging showed activation of brain areas associated with openness to new ideas, change, and learning. Passarelli found vision-focused coachees were happier, expressed higher aspirations, exerted significantly more effort, and found more joy in it.

- **Humble inquiry** (Ed Schein) — set aside your biases, assumptions, and experience; show sincere interest; convey empathy; communicate a deep desire to help; and **let them do at least 80% of the talking**.

- **The personal vision statement** — cap multiple ideal-self conversations by having the person write one. Dewitt Jones boils it to ~six words, memorized and repeated as a daily mantra. Purpose: keep them focused on their *desire* to change rather than their obligation to.

- **The personal balance sheet** (real-self tool) — not just current strengths and weaknesses, but also distinctive qualities and enduring characteristics: traits, habits, and competencies that have held steady over time. Clarifies what's going well and what must change *relative to the long-term vision*.

- **Two-component self-awareness** (Scott Taylor): what people know about themselves, and their understanding of how others experience and think of them. Coach to the gap between those — and, more critically, to the gap between ideal self and real self.

## Key Concepts
- **Coachable moment** — an opening to help someone develop: an evident challenge (promotion, big project, retooling feedback), a wake-up call (job loss, health diagnosis, milestone birthday), or just a vague unease.
- **Readiness to change** — whether the person is open, and willing to do the reflection and experimentation. Avolio and Hannah's research: companies should assess and sometimes *enhance* readiness before leadership development, or it won't take.
- **Resonant relationship** — the caring, trusting bond that makes coaching work.
- **Learning agenda ≠ performance improvement plan** — PIPs address shortcomings, feel like work, and inhibit development; the learning agenda leaves people energized and empowered.
- **Developmental network** (Kram and Higgins) — the extended circle of support beyond the coach; operationalized as a **personal board of advisers** made up of role models for the behaviors the person aspires to, who have a stake in their success and can provide inspiration and accountability.
- **Peer coaching** — training people in the intentional change framework so they become compassionate catalysts for each other; works one-on-one and in small groups of five to 12.

## Mental Models
- **Mine for gold, not dirt.** Carnegie: "several tons of dirt must be moved to get an ounce of gold, but one doesn't go into the mine looking for dirt — one goes in looking for gold." Even when discussing development areas, hold the person in a positive emotional state.
- **Think of people as people, not problem-bearing platforms.** The failure mode, in one executive's own words: "When people come to me with a problem, I see the problem, not the person."
- **Expect long timelines.** Lally and colleagues found habit formation takes **18 to 254 days**; skill building, relationship management, and career change take longer still, with many stops and starts.

## Anti-patterns
- **Drilling into the problem and offering solutions** — the near-universal instinct at a coachable moment, and the wrong one for behavioral change.
- **Setting targets for people and fixing their problems** — triggers stress and self-consciousness rather than openness.
- **Listing weaknesses in the real-self assessment** — highlighting where someone needs improvement defeats the purpose; identify perception gaps and ideal/real alignment instead.
- **Dressing up a PIP as a learning agenda** — the framing is the mechanism.
- **Coaching someone who isn't ready** — without openness to reflection and experimentation, the investment underperforms.
- **Doing more than 20% of the talking.**

## Worked Example
Two executives, coached through the same framework.

**Karen Milley**, head of R&D at a large consumer goods company, oversaw 60 engineers and scientists. Driven and direct, focused on solving immediate problems, and she got results. Enrolled by her manager in a leadership-development program, she began wondering whether her transactional, no-nonsense style was getting the best from her team. *Opportunity*: a standout manager aiming at the C-suite who hadn't developed an inspiring leadership style — and, critically, was ready to look at it differently.

Ideal-self questions used with her: Who are you at your very best? What kind of leader do you want to be? How do you want others in the organization to see you? What does success look like to you? What position do you ultimately want to attain? Her **personal vision statement**: "Live freely, in good health, with integrity, in a future filled with love and hope."

*Real self* (personal balance sheet): she excelled at composure under pressure and at reading organizational power dynamics — but wasn't adequately demonstrating the care and empathy she genuinely felt. *Learning agenda*: be more coach than commander; become more emotionally aware and mindful of others. *Seeing it through*: regular progress reviews with her coach; deliberately shifting out of always-busy problem-solver mode toward being approachable, kind, and playful; committing more time with direct reports to understand their experience. More authentic relationships followed.

**Ray Lewis**, corporate accounts manager at his family's environmental emergency response business, was on track to succeed his father as president and had enrolled in an executive MBA. He was increasingly uneasy — he had never actually decided on his ideal career path, just followed the one laid out for him, and his lack of genuine excitement about succeeding his dad showed.

Ideal-self questions used with him: What kind of work do you feel drawn to do? What gives you the greatest energy and excitement about your future? What do you really want to do, and how does that differ from what you feel you *should* do? Twenty years from now, what would you like to say you've accomplished? (Best closing question, always: *What other ideas come to mind as you think about this?*)

His **vision statement**: "Enjoy the freedom to travel the world, meet interesting people, and pursue an exciting, passion-filled life of learning." *Real self*: strong as a visionary and adaptable across diverse environments — and unwilling to keep subordinating his dreams to perceived obligations. *Learning agenda*: integrate personal passions with professional goals; build stronger relationships inside and outside the business; make time to reflect — hiking, martial arts, sports, youth group work, meals with friends and family, extended breaks. *Outcome*: after an extended trip abroad gave him the deep reflection he'd wanted, he left the family business and started his own successful company.

Peer coaching at scale: Carlos De Barnola, then HR director for Covidien's Iberian division, paired each person with one teammate, with a facilitator present. People quickly showed more concern, asked better questions, and built trust. Pairs then joined other pairs into quartets — and the professional facilitators were able to withdraw entirely while the coaching continued.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start from the person's dreams, not their deficits; the emotional state determines whether learning is even neurologically available.
2. Check readiness before investing; enhance it if needed.
3. Ask open, exploratory questions and let them talk 80% of the time.
4. Produce a written personal vision statement — short enough to remember and repeat.
5. Build a personal balance sheet covering enduring qualities, not just a strengths/weaknesses list.
6. Frame the learning agenda around leveraging existing strengths and the change they're most *excited* to try.
7. Surround the person with a developmental network or personal board of advisers; change is not a solo act.
8. Expect months, not weeks, and keep checking in.
