# Chapter 3: Making Empathy Central to Your Company Culture
*Jamil Zaki*

## Core Idea
Empathy spreads through social norms — what people see others actually doing — not through announced values. To build an empathic culture, work *with* norms (make existing kindness visible, recruit the connectors) rather than proclaiming new ideals that current behavior contradicts.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Ideals vs. Norms**: *ideals* are prescriptions for how people ought to behave; *norms* are how most members of the group actually behave. When the two clash, people gravitate toward what others do, not what they're told to do.
  - When to use: before launching any values rollout, culture initiative, or new behavioral expectation.
  - How: measure the current norm honestly. If the gap to the proposed ideal is large, do not announce the ideal — instead find and amplify the pockets where the desired behavior already exists, then let conformity do the work.
  - Failure mode: hastily declaring a new value in an aggressive, competitive culture "shifts the goalposts." It doesn't inspire; it makes leadership look hypocritical and out of touch, and it makes people who followed the old culture feel betrayed.

- **The three moves for building empathic culture**:
  1. **Acknowledge the potential for growth** — establish that empathy is a skill, not a trait.
  2. **Highlight the right norms** — foreground the quiet majority's kind behavior via incentives and recognition.
  3. **Find culture leaders and co-create with them** — identify connectors and recruit them to champion the cause.

- **Phantom norms**: behaviors that appear dominant only because a few loud or prominent individuals display them.
  - When to use: whenever a team "feels" toxic or cynical but you can't name who exactly.
  - How: separate volume from prevalence. Count who actually behaves how, then deliberately raise the visibility of the majority behavior. "The loudest voices are seldom the kindest."

## Key Concepts
- **Empathy** — the ability to share and understand others' emotions.
- **Norms** — how most members of a group actually behave (descriptive, and behaviorally decisive).
- **Ideals** — how a group says people ought to behave (prescriptive, and behaviorally weak when unmatched by norms).
- **Phantom norm** — a minority behavior mistaken for the majority because it is loud or prominent.
- **Fixed mindset about empathy** — the belief that empathy is a trait you have or don't; people holding it work less hard to connect with others.
- **Connectors** — the most-connected people in a group who encourage cohesion outside any formal role; information, ideas, and values flow through them. Not necessarily the most popular or powerful.
- **Emotional contagion / prosocial conformity** — people "catch" one another's care and altruism, just as they catch bad behavior.

## Mental Models
- **Think of empathy as contagious, not mandated.** After seeing others vote, conserve energy, or donate, people do those things more. Design for exposure, not for compliance.
- **Use "quiet majority" as your default hypothesis.** In most groups empathy already exists and is simply inaudible; your job is turning up its volume, not manufacturing it.
- **Think of culture change as bottom-up and organic.** Cultures build and change collectively; a CEO announcement is the weakest available lever.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rolling out a new value hastily to fix a culture it contradicts**: creates ideal-norm distance, which produces cynicism rather than aspiration.
- **Treating empathy as an innate trait**: if such beliefs permeate an organization, encouraging empathy as a collective value "will fall flat."
- **Letting the loudest voice define the room**: colleagues mistake one member's toxic attitude for majority opinion — the college binge-drinking effect, where students overestimate peers' enthusiasm because the hard partiers narrate their weekends.
- **Picking champions by rank or popularity**: influence flows through connectedness, not status.

## Worked Example
Betsy Levy Paluck and colleagues ran a social-network experiment across 56 middle schools. Students were deputized to create anti-bullying campaigns that then spread around campus. Critically, the deputies varied in how socially well-connected they were. Peer-led campaigns worked overall — but they worked *substantially better* when helmed by the most-connected students. The organizational translation: before launching an empathy or inclusion initiative, map who the connectors are (the people others route information through, regardless of title), co-create the initiative with them, and let them carry it. This has a second payoff — publicly recognizing people for connecting with others itself models and reinforces a positive norm.

Supporting evidence for step one: Schumann, Zaki, and Dweck found that people with a fixed mindset about empathy exert less effort to connect. In a follow-up, simply presenting people with evidence that empathy behaves more like a skill than a trait made them work harder at it — even when it didn't come naturally.

## Key Takeaways
1. Never announce an ideal far ahead of the actual norm; close the distance by amplifying existing good behavior instead.
2. Start by teaching that empathy is buildable — mindset assessment precedes any empathy program.
3. Hunt for phantom norms: ask whether the attitude you think is dominant is merely the loudest.
4. Use recognition and incentives as a visibility tool, not just a reward — their main job is showing the quiet majority to itself.
5. Identify and deputize connectors; ideals that travel through them "take."
6. Expect and design for contagion: empathy spreads person to person.
