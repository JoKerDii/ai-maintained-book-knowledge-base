# Chapter 13: Building an Ethical Career
*Maryam Kouchaki and Isaac H. Smith*

## Core Idea
Good intentions are insufficient. Start from **moral humility** — the recognition that anyone can transgress without vigilance — and work a three-stage approach: prepare in advance for moral challenges, make good decisions in the moment, and reflect on and learn from both successes and failures.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Moral humility** — admitting that temptations, rationalizations, and situations can lead even the best of us to misbehave; treating ethics as pursuing the good rather than only avoiding the bad; and seeing character development as a lifelong pursuit.

- **The three-stage approach**: (1) Plan to be good, (2) Make good decisions, (3) Reflect after the fact.

- **Résumé virtues vs. eulogy virtues** (David Brooks): *résumé* virtues are skills, abilities, and accomplishments you can list ("increased ROI by 10% on a multimillion-dollar project"); *eulogy* virtues are what people praise after you've died (loyal friend, kind, hard worker). Résumé virtues largely concern what you've done for yourself; eulogy virtues concern who you are and what you've done for others.
  - How: write out eulogy-virtue goals that are challenging but attainable. Ask Drucker's questions — "What do you want to be remembered for?" and "What do you want to contribute?" Framing professional life as a quest for *contribution* rather than *achievement* changes how you approach a career. Do it early, before habits harden.

- **If-then planning / implementation intentions** (Gollwitzer) — tie a situational cue to a desired behavior, and say it aloud. Must be specific and made *before* you encounter the situation. Examples given: *If* my boss asks me to do something potentially unethical, *then* I will turn to a friend or mentor outside the organization before acting. *If* I am solicited for a bribe, *then* I will consult my company's legal team and formal policies. *If* I witness sexual harassment or racial prejudice, *then* I will immediately stand up for the victim.

- **Three philosophical lenses for a decision** — apply all three, because addressing rules, consequences, and virtues together makes you less likely to miss something:
  - **Deontology** — what rules or principles apply? Would this violate honesty or respect for others?
  - **Utilitarianism** — what outcomes fall on every party, directly and indirectly? What's the greatest good for the greatest number?
  - **Virtue ethics** (Aristotelian) — which course of action would best reflect a virtuous person?

- **The three rationalization tests**:
  - **The publicity test** — would you be comfortable with this choice, and your reasoning, on the front page of the local newspaper?
  - **The generalizability test** — would you be comfortable with it as a precedent for everyone facing a similar situation?
  - **The mirror test** — would you like the person you see in the mirror afterward; is that who you want to be?
  - If any answer is no, think carefully before proceeding.

- **Questions for resisting social pressure** (from *The Business Ethics Field Guide*): Do they have a right to request this? Would others in the organization feel the same way I do? What are the requesters trying to accomplish? Could it be accomplished a different way? Can I refuse in a manner that helps them save face?

## Key Concepts
- **The ethical mirage** (Tenbrunsel et al.) — overestimating the virtuousness of our future selves; we know what we *should* do prospectively but focus on what we *want* in the present.
- **Personal safeguards** — habits that strengthen self-control: quality sleep, personal prayer (for the religious), mindfulness.
- **Ethical mentor** — an adviser chosen for shared values and moral counsel rather than for accelerating your climb; build these inside and outside the organization.
- **Subtle moral signaling** — building a reputation for doing things right and openly discussing potential moral challenges, without a holier-than-thou tone.
- **Moral fit** — how well an organization's ethics match yours; systematically underweighted next to compensation and promotion. Ethical stress strongly predicts fatigue, lower job satisfaction, lower motivation, and higher turnover.
- **Self-control-depleting conditions** — high uncertainty, excessive cognitive demands, long days and late nights, and consecutive stretch goals all correlate with increased unethical behavior.
- **Identity segmentation** — compartmentalizing personal and professional life and living by a different moral code in each; reflection guards against it.
- **Job crafting** (Amy Wrzesniewski) — proactively reshaping your tasks, workplace relationships, and perception of your job so work becomes more meaningful.

## Mental Models
- **Treat ambiguity as the breeding ground for self-interested rationalization.** So engage coworkers: ask clarifying questions tactfully and state expectations — "I think it's important that we don't cross any ethical lines here."
- **Assume the environment shapes you more than you think.** Choose the workplace accordingly, and use the interview: when asked "Do you have any questions for me?", try "What types of ethical dilemmas might be faced in this job?" or "What does this company do to promote ethical business practices?"
- **Use time as an ethical instrument.** Very few decisions must be made in the moment; rushing raises the odds of acting unethically. "Sleep on it," and use the delay to consult your ethical mentors — or, if they're unavailable, imagine explaining your action to them. Discomfort in that imagining is the warning.
- **Set ethics goals the way you set work targets.** Franklin tracked 13 virtues on a daily chart; Clayton Christensen, after cancer, concluded the metric that mattered most was "the individual people whose lives I've touched."

## Anti-patterns
- **Relying on good intentions and the hope you'll rise to the occasion**: the ethical mirage guarantees your future self is overrated.
- **The standard rationalizations**: "Everyone does this," "I'm just following my boss's orders," "It's for the greater good," "It's not like I'm robbing a bank," "It's their own fault — they deserve it."
- **Deciding under time pressure**: the Princeton Theological Seminary study found students rushing to deliver a lecture — on the parable of the Good Samaritan — were much less likely to stop for a stranger lying helpless on the ground.
- **Deferring to authority or the crowd**: the Milgram experiments showed how readily people comply with instructions from those in power. Be wary of anything done because "everybody else is doing it" or because your boss said so; take ownership of your actions.
- **Choosing a job on compensation and promotion alone**: moral misfit is a durable cost. One study found employees of a large international bank cheated more, on average, when reminded of their professional identity than non-bankers given the same reminder.
- **Analyzing only cost-benefit and ROI**: step back and search deliberately for the moral issues at stake.
- **Reflecting only by yourself**: personal histories, biases, and perspective limits mean you need trusted counsel — approach it like performance feedback, asking specific questions, avoiding defensiveness, expressing gratitude.

## Worked Example
Wrzesniewski's early job-crafting research followed hospital housekeepers, many of whom understood their work in a way that made them "healers, not janitors" — they weren't cleaning rooms, they were creating a peaceful healing environment. One custodian deliberately used her smile and humor to help cancer patients relax, seeking out chances to interact with them because she believed she could be a momentary bright spot in the darkness of ongoing chemotherapy. Nothing in her formal job description changed. What she crafted were the tasks she took on, the relationships she built, and how she construed the role — and the effect was to develop and cultivate eulogy virtues: love, compassion, kindness, loyalty. The lesson is that an ethical career is built from bottom-up changes to ordinary work, not only from resisting dramatic temptations.

A second, compact example of preparation working: in a study by Kouchaki, participants were much less likely to ask an online partner to do something unethical after receiving an email from that partner with a virtuous quotation in the signature line — such as **"Success without honor is worse than fraud."** A single low-cost signal shifted others' behavior toward her, which is the practical case for subtle moral signaling.

## Key Takeaways
1. Adopt moral humility first — assume you *can* transgress, and plan accordingly.
2. Write eulogy-virtue goals; reframe your career as contribution rather than achievement.
3. Build specific if-then plans before you're in the situation, and say them out loud.
4. Recruit ethical mentors deliberately, alongside career mentors.
5. Screen for moral fit when choosing employers, and ask about it in interviews.
6. Run decisions through rules, consequences, and virtues — then through the publicity, generalizability, and mirror tests.
7. Slow down; refuse to be rushed, and use delay to consult.
8. Reflect on successes as well as failures, with trusted counsel, to prevent repeat lapses and identity segmentation.
9. Be extra vigilant during periods of uncertainty, overload, long hours, and back-to-back stretch goals.
