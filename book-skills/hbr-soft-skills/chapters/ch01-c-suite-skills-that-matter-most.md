# Chapter 1: The C-Suite Skills That Matter Most
*Raffaella Sadun, Joseph Fuller, Stephen Hansen, and PJ Neal*

## Core Idea
Employers now weight **social skills** above financial and operational expertise when hiring C-suite leaders — and the shift is sharpest for CEOs, in large, complex, technology-intensive firms. Treat social skill as a primary qualification to be built and assessed systematically, not as a bonus attribute.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The four components of "social skills"** (the article's operational definition, not a vague label): (1) high self-awareness, (2) ability to listen and communicate well, (3) facility for working with different types of people and groups, (4) **theory of mind** — inferring how others think and feel.
  - When to use: writing a senior job description, designing a development plan, or auditing why a leader is stalling.
  - How: score the candidate/leader on each of the four separately; a strong communicator with weak theory of mind is a different problem than the reverse. Do not accept "good with people" as an assessment.
  - Why it works: it converts an unfalsifiable trait into four things you can observe and train.

- **The six skill clusters of executive work** (derived from O*NET, used to code ~5,000 real job descriptions): managing financial and material resources; monitoring corporate performance; tending to human resources; handling administrative tasks; processing and using complex information; exercising social skills.
  - When to use: comparing what a role *says* it wants against what it actually rewards.
  - How: map the language of the job description onto the six clusters and check the balance. Since ~2007, social-skill language has risen and operational-expertise language has fallen across all five C-suite roles studied.

- **Four New Areas for Focus**: (1) systematically build social skills, (2) assess them innovatively, (3) develop them at all levels ("grow your own"), (4) assess the *collective* social skills of the C-suite as a team.

## Key Concepts
- **Theory of mind** — the capacity to infer others' mental and emotional states; the hardest-to-fake component of social skill.
- **Managerial pedigree** — the traditional credential (GE/IBM/P&G/McKinsey lineage, functional rotations) that no longer predicts C-suite success.
- **Skill adjacency** — mapping which capabilities a person can plausibly grow into; used by internal talent marketplaces.
- **Grow your own** — developing internal candidates rather than betting on outside hires for interpersonal ability.
- **Systems effect** — one weak member of the C-suite degrades the whole team's performance, especially the CEO's.
- **Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test** — an established measure of social intelligence shown to predict individual performance in team settings.
- **Stakeholder capitalism** — the shift that makes CEOs public figures accountable in real time to many constituencies.

## Mental Models
- **Think of automation as a social-skill amplifier**: the more routine information handling is automated, the more competitive advantage rests on judgment, creativity, and coordination — all delivered through people.
- **Use "same tools, different management" as a differentiation test**: when every rival runs on the same platforms (AWS, Salesforce, Workday), the only remaining edge is how well you manage the people using them.
- **Treat interview rapport as evidence of similarity, not of skill.** Feeling an easy connection with a candidate mostly tells you they resemble you.

## Anti-patterns
- **Hiring on hard skills because they're easy to measure**: measurability, not predictive value, is driving the criteria; the easily assessed qualifications are the ones that have lost relative importance.
- **Relying on references and confidential search networks**: both candidates and referees come from the same small homogeneous networks, which multiplies bias.
- **Using psychometrics as a proxy for social skill**: they detect outgoingness and behavioral style, not effectiveness across diverse groups.
- **Using scenario simulations to judge interpersonal ability**: they are built around specific crises (product-integrity failure, activist investor) and mostly test administrative/technical response, not spontaneous coordination — plus they are expensive and rare.
- **Outsourcing constituency relations to comms/government-affairs functions**: leaders are now expected to show up personally, transparently, and accountably.

## Worked Example
The research method itself is the worked example of assessing something "soft" rigorously. Facing ~5,000 unstructured C-suite job descriptions from Russell Reynolds Associates (2000–2017), the authors did not read them for impressions. Step one: define the target — pull the skills listed for "chief executive" in the U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET database and sort them into six clusters. Step two: score each job description for semantic similarity to each cluster, using a model of managerial language trained (word2vec) on every *Harvard Business Review* article since 1922. Controlling for industry and firm variables, the coefficients after 2007 show social-skill language rising and operational-skill language falling — significantly, and across CEO, CFO, CIO, HR head, and CMO. The transferable move: define the construct first, then measure language against it, rather than eyeballing the documents.

## Key Takeaways
1. Make social skills an explicit criterion for hiring, promotion, performance measurement, and compensation — not an unwritten tiebreaker.
2. Decompose "soft skills" into self-awareness, listening/communication, cross-group facility, and theory of mind before assessing anyone.
3. Place high-potential leaders in roles that force interaction with varied internal and external constituencies, then monitor performance there closely.
4. Expect the biggest demand for social skill in large, public, multinational, M&A-active, and automation-heavy firms.
5. Assess the C-suite's collective interpersonal capability, not just each individual's.
6. Build new assessment tools (behavioral research, AI-based matching, social-intelligence measures); traditional résumé signals cannot see this skill.
