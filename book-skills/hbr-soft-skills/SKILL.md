## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The Triad of Awareness** (Goleman, Ch 2) — Focus on yourself, on others, on the wider world. Failure to focus inward leaves you rudderless; on others, clueless; outward, blindsided. Inward + others build emotional intelligence; outward builds strategy and innovation. These use different neural pathways, so strength in one does not transfer.

**The Empathy Triad** (Ch 2) — *Cognitive empathy* (understand their perspective; think about feelings rather than feel them) to explain yourself and to de-escalate. *Emotional empathy* (feel what they feel) to mentor, manage clients, read a room. *Empathic concern* (sense what they need from you) to decide how to act — balanced so you neither burn out nor go numb.

**The four components of social skills** (Sadun et al., Ch 1) — self-awareness, listening/communication, facility across different groups, and **theory of mind**. Since ~2007 these have displaced financial and operational expertise as the top C-suite hiring criterion. Assess them separately; never accept "good with people."

**Ideals vs. Norms** (Zaki, Ch 3) — Ideals are what a group says people ought to do; norms are what most members actually do. When they clash, people follow the norm. So amplify existing good behavior and recruit **connectors** rather than announcing new values. Watch for **phantom norms** — behaviors that look dominant only because a few loud people display them.

**The Four Attributes of Great Learners** (Andersen, Ch 4) — aspiration, self-awareness, curiosity, vulnerability. All four are raised by editing **self-talk**: "I don't need to learn this" → "What would my future look like if I did?"; "This is boring" → "I wonder why others find it interesting"; "I'm terrible at this" → "I'm making beginner mistakes, but I'll get better."

**The Three Reinforcements** (Grant, Ch 5) — Every request for help should carry: **in-group** (we're on the same team — built from shared *experience*, not shared traits), **positive identity** (you're a helpful person, uniquely placed), and **effectiveness** (here's exactly what I need and what it will change; I'll report back). Never signal obligation, apologize profusely, offer reciprocity, or minimize the need.

**Promotion vs. Prevention Focus** (Burris, Ch 6) — Diagnose your boss, then pitch **one** frame: opportunity for promotion-focused leaders, threat avoided for prevention-focused ones. Never both — mixed frames get the least support of any option. Pitch in private, tie it to something they've already said they care about, and pre-answer their three implementation questions: what resources, how hard to enlist others, is it worth the political capital.

**The Three A's of Feedback** (Meyer, Ch 7) — Feedback must be meant to **assist**, be **actionable**, and be **asked for**. If it wasn't requested, solicit feedback on your own work first — advising confers power, and asking first hands it back. Across cultures, match **upgraders** ("absolutely," "totally") and **downgraders** ("a little," "maybe"): *do unto others as they would have done unto themselves*.

**The Three Listening Attitudes** (Kaur, Ch 8) — Listening to *protect* (defensive; speaker feels ignored), to *accept or reject* (judging; speaker feels labeled), or to *co-create* (exploring; builds trust). Choose co-create and hold four questions: What are they trying to say? What are they thinking? What do they expect to explore with me? How can I connect to their world?

**The Third Entity** (Gallo, Ch 9) — In conflict, imagine three parties: you, your colleague, and the dynamic between you. Work on the dynamic. Also guard against **naive realism**, **fundamental attribution error**, **confirmation bias**, and **affinity bias**; use "flip it to test it"; write down your goals; run small timeboxed behavioral experiments.

**Intentional Change Theory** (Boyatzis et al., Ch 10) — ideal self → real self → learning agenda → experimenting and practicing. Coach *with compassion* (start from their dreams, ask open questions, let them talk 80%) rather than *for compliance* (drill the problem, set targets) — the latter triggers stress that blocks learning. Mine for gold, not dirt.

**The Eight S's of strong writing** (Birchard, Ch 11) — simplicity, specificity, surprise, stirring language, seductiveness, smart thinking, social content, storytelling. Each triggers the brain's reward circuit. Golden rule: *reward readers as you would yourself*.

**The Five Leadership Voices** (Su, Ch 12) — character (constant principles, invoked before hard conversations), context (the bigger picture, before the details), clarity (priorities, plus empowering the team to say no), curiosity (default response to pushback), connection (stories, specific thanks, rapport).

**Moral humility and the three-stage ethical approach** (Kouchaki & Smith, Ch 13) — Assume you *can* transgress. Prepare (eulogy-virtue goals, if-then plans, ethical mentors, moral fit), decide in the moment (rules/consequences/virtues, plus the publicity, generalizability, and mirror tests), and reflect afterward with trusted counsel.
