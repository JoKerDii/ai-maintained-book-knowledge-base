# Chapter 6: The Science of Pep Talks
*Daniel McGinn*

## Core Idea
Motivating people before a performance is a learnable science, not a talent copied from movie bosses. Nearly every winning pep talk mixes three elements — direction giving, empathetic language, and meaning making — and the skill lies in choosing the right proportions for this audience, this task, this moment.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Motivating Language Theory (MLT)** — Jacqueline and Milton Mayfield, Texas A&M International University, ~three decades of research. Three elements:
  - **Direction giving** — "uncertainty-reducing language": precisely *how* to do the task. Understandable instructions, clear task definitions, explicit criteria for how performance will be evaluated.
  - **Empathetic language** — concern for the performer as a human being: praise, encouragement, gratitude, acknowledgment of difficulty. "I know this is a challenge, but I trust you can do it." "Your well-being is one of my top priorities."
  - **Meaning making** — *why* the task matters: linking the organization's purpose or mission to the listeners' own goals, usually through stories about people who succeeded or about how the work changed customers' lives.
  - When to use: any pre-performance moment — a sales push, a launch, a mission brief — delivered to one person or 650.
  - How to balance: include all three, then adjust. Experienced workers on a familiar task need little direction. Followers already tightly bonded to you need less empathy. Meaning making helps almost everywhere, but can be dialed down when the end goal is self-evident.
  - Note: meaning making is almost always the hardest of the three to deliver.
- **The McChrystal Sequence** (Gen. Stanley McChrystal's pre-mission pattern): direction giving ("here's what I'm asking you to do") → meaning making ("here's why it's important") → empathy ("here's why I know you can do it"; "think about what you've done together before") → recap ("now let's go and do it").
- **Vargas's Sports Rule** (Tiffanye Vargas, CSU Long Beach) — choose the mix by game situation. Coaches' pregame remarks do matter: 90% of players enjoy listening and 65% say the speech affects how they play.
  - *Unknown opponent, or a team you narrowly lost to*: information-rich, uncertainty-reducing. "We beat this team with tough man-to-man. Joe, neutralize that shooting guard; Jimmy, box out the rebounder every play."
  - *Underdog, or a high-stakes game*: more emotional — heavier empathy and meaning making. "No one expects us to win. But I expect you to win. For your teammates, for the fans — because you deserve this."

## Key Concepts
- **Uncertainty-reducing language** — the technical name for direction giving; it removes ambiguity about method and evaluation.
- **Self-efficacy framing** — telling people that whatever has happened so far, they can still make today successful.
- **Negative self-talk** — attributing a colleague's success to an unfair advantage (a better territory) rather than to method.
- **Battle exhortation** (Keith Yellin) — precombat oratory back to the Greeks and Romans; 23 recurring "common topics," mostly appeals to reason (our army versus their weaker one) or emotion (God is with us; the enemy is evil).
- **Professionalization effect** — career volunteers know their purpose and need less rah-rah than draftees or new recruits.
- **Micro-delivery** — continuing all three MLT elements one-on-one after the speech, not just from the stage.

## Mental Models
- Use "audience state sets the mix": low knowledge → direction; low confidence or high stakes → empathy and meaning; obvious purpose → less meaning making.
- Think of a pep talk as three questions the listener is silently asking: *How exactly?* *Do you see me?* *Why does this matter?*
- Treat the pharmaceutical-startup case as the ceiling and the fast-food case as the floor: a CEO whose staff have lost loved ones to the diseases they treat can say "we're here to save lives" and be believed; a fast-food supervisor with part-time teenagers must build meaning deliberately ("stable jobs so you have money for your family, for college, for yourself").
- Don't stop at the stage — Alioto's speech was followed by individual conversations with more than a hundred reps.

## Anti-patterns
- **Learning by mimicry from Glengarry Glen Ross or The Wolf of Wall Street**: entertaining models, no evidence base.
- **Relying solely on direction giving**: instructions alone won't move people who don't feel seen or don't know why it matters.
- **Piling on empathy for seasoned performers**: veterans mid-operation are "all business"; they need the plan, not reassurance.
- **Emotional rallying for an unfamiliar opponent**: when uncertainty is the real problem, inspiration doesn't substitute for information.
- **Stopping short of the end user**: the Mayfields' one critique of Alioto — she connected reps' work to the company's goals but not to how Yelp actually improves users' lives.

## Worked Example
Erica Galos Alioto, SVP of local sales at Yelp, in gold "lucky LDOM pants," addresses 650 reps in the New York office on the last day of the month. She speaks for 20 minutes; the office is $1.5 million from target. The Mayfields graded the transcript and found all three elements in a deliberate order:

- **Empathy first** — thanks the group for being Yelp's top office, name-checks Colleen at $80,000 and others who've been crushing it, and reframes the lagging: "No matter what's happened to you up to this point in the month, you can make it a successful day." She emphasizes that if one rep can post spectacular numbers, all of them can — same skills, same training.
- **Meaning making woven through** — LDOM isn't about the calendar, it's about how the office approaches the day, the "grit and determination, the ability to make the unthinkable happen." The people who said no all month get turned into yeses.
- **Direction giving, concrete and small** — everyone takes out pen and paper and writes down what success looks like today (calls made, hearts and minds won), then sticks that one goal on a Post-it on the monitor.
- **Direction giving on mindset** — she names the negative self-talk explicitly ("Why is Jon at target? He must have a great territory"), counters it with the fact that new reps put on supposedly bad territories routinely crush them, and instructs reps to look for *similarities* with high performers rather than differences.
- **Meaning-making close** — "every time you win the heart and mind of a business owner, you're not only helping yourself — you're helping your team, your office, your company, and helping Yelp get where it wants to be."
- **Then she doesn't stop.** She walks the floor and talks individually with more than a hundred reps: how to close an ambivalent prospect more forcefully, category specifics for a rep about to call an auto mechanic, confidence for others, team goals for others.

Result: $1.45 million in new ads that day — quota met, $50,000 short of the stretch target, with many reps hitting their BME ("best month ever"). Alioto's own read: "My speech wasn't anything groundbreaking, but it helped them think about where they are and what they are capable of in a different way."

## Key Takeaways
1. Build every pep talk from three deliberate ingredients rather than borrowed charisma.
2. Diagnose the audience first: what do they lack — clarity, confidence, or purpose?
3. Give direction that is concrete enough to act on in the next five minutes (write the goal on a Post-it).
4. Name the specific discouraging thought your people are having, then supply the counter-evidence.
5. Reserve the emotional peak for the close, and connect the day's task to the largest true purpose you can support.
6. Push meaning making one link further than feels natural — down to the end user's life.
7. Continue the three elements one-on-one after the speech; the floor walk may matter more than the stage.
