# Chapter 5: The Necessary Art of Persuasion
*Jay A. Conger*

## Core Idea
Persuasion is not selling or convincing — it is a learning and negotiating process that leads colleagues to a shared solution. It rests on four steps: establish credibility, frame for common ground, provide vivid evidence, and connect emotionally.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Essential Steps of Persuasion**: Establish credibility → Frame for common ground → Provide vivid evidence → Connect emotionally.
  - When to use: any cross-functional, authority-free environment where people ask not "What should I do?" but "Why should I do it?"
  - How:
    1. **Establish credibility** — audit yourself honestly on *expertise* (track record, product/process knowledge) and *relationships* (a history of listening and working in others' interest, plus steady emotional character). Test your self-assessment with trusted colleagues; most managers considerably overestimate their credibility.
    2. **Frame for common ground** — describe your position in terms of tangible benefits to the people you're persuading. If no shared advantage exists, adjust the position itself.
    3. **Provide evidence** — supplement numerical data with examples, stories, metaphors, and analogies. Listeners absorb information in proportion to its vividness.
    4. **Connect emotionally** — show your own commitment (heart and gut, not just mind), and match your emotional fervor to the audience's current emotional state.
  - Why it works: it treats persuasion as give-and-take. A persuader rarely changes another's viewpoint without altering their own (Kathleen Reardon). Prepared, judicious compromises are themselves persuasive: seeing flexibility, colleagues stop fearing being bowled over.
- **Two Sources of Credibility — Expertise and Relationships**:
  - *Expertise gaps*: learn more (formal/informal education, conversations with in-house experts); get relevant on-the-job experience; **buy or borrow credibility** by hiring outside experts or enlisting internal experts as substitutes; cite respected periodicals, books, and independent research; launch small pilot projects.
  - *Relationship gaps*: meet one-on-one with every key person — not to pitch, but to gather perspectives and offer help; involve like-minded coworkers who already have strong standing with your audience.

## Key Concepts
- **The John Wayne approach** — stating a strong position up front and pushing to a close through persistence and exuberance.
- **The lion tamer** — engaging your "partner" by showing him the legs of a chair; giving opponents nothing to set their jaws into.
- **Persuasive framing** — identifying and naming shared benefits (the lollipops-by-the-register move); not deception.
- **Vivid evidence** — stories, metaphors, and analogies that make abstract data emotionally memorable.
- **Emotional matchmaking** — canvassing key individuals to learn the audience's mood before choosing your emotional register.
- **Discovery, preparation, dialogue** — the three phases; dialogue happens both before and during persuasion.
- **Compromise as signal** — flexibility demonstrates that persuasion isn't a one-way street.

## Mental Models
- Think of persuasion as a process, not an event: listen → test a position → revise to reflect input → test again → incorporate compromises → try again.
- Use "there's as much strategy in *how* you present your position as in the position itself — in fact, the presentation strategy is more critical."
- When framing seems impossible, treat that as evidence your position needs changing, not as a communication problem.
- Ask "what will this cost them in time and money, and where is my evidence weakest?" before you open your mouth.

## Anti-patterns
- **The up-front hard sell**: a strong opening position gives opponents a clear target to fight.
- **Resisting compromise**: read as surrender by the persuader, read as one-way traffic by everyone else.
- **Believing great arguments are the whole game**: credibility, framing, vividness, and emotional match matter just as much.
- **Treating persuasion as a one-shot effort**: shared solutions almost never arrive on the first try.
- **Overestimating your own credibility**: research says most managers do, considerably.
- **Mismatching emotional register**: scare tactics aimed at a proud team read as a scolding.
- **Rationalizing headquarters' view to a skeptical field**: argue *their* benefit, not the center's logic.

## Worked Example
**Emotional mismatch — the biplane.** The president of an aeronautics manufacturer feared that competitors' maintenance costs and turnaround times would cost him customers. He gathered senior managers, projected a half-covered slide of a smiling pilot in an old biplane, scarf in the wind — "this is how I felt about our record year" — then uncovered the rest: the pilot flying directly into a wall. "This is what I see happening to us." The room turned hostile. The managers had just broken company records for sales and profitability and had walked in expecting recognition; instead they got scolded, and they left calling it scare tactics. The fix: canvass a few senior team members first to learn the room's emotional state; hold one meeting devoted purely to praising the achievement; then, in a *second* meeting, calmly describe the emerging threats and ask the team to help develop initiatives.

**Emotional match — Robert Marcell and the Neon.** Chrysler wanted a foreign partner for its new subcompact. Marcell, head of small-car design, wanted it built in-house, and faced two audiences: a demoralized team that had lost confidence, and skeptical senior management. From countless informal conversations he learned that people shared his feeling that surrendering the subcompact meant surrendering the company's soul and its jobs. He built a 15-minute slide talk out of photographs of his hometown, Iron River, Michigan — a mining town gutted by foreign competition: his boarded-up high school, shuttered friends' homes, the crumbling ironworks, closed churches, an abandoned rail yard. After each image, the same refrain: "We couldn't compete." Then he turned hopeful, expressing pride in his group and challenging them to build a made-in-America subcompact. His team started drafting designs. Shown to Lee Iacocca — a fighter and a patriot — the same talk kept Iacocca in the room for two hours and won approval for the car that became the Neon. Marcell's closing line: if we dare to be different, our kids and grandkids won't end up working at fast-food chains.

**Credibility bought and built — Tom Smith.** A new COO at a retail bank, an outsider from financial services, believed excessive overhead would sink the bank; his complacent colleagues, riding recent success, disagreed. Three moves: (1) hired a respected external consultant who showed in interactive presentations, later circulated as written reports, that competitors were aggressively containing costs; (2) visited all 135 branches in his Ontario region over three months, listening to branch managers about strengths, weaknesses, competitor moves, and cost ideas — buying both a broader perspective than senior management had and dozens of relationships; (3) launched a small, highly visible win — a mortgage program with no payments for 90 days — which succeeded and made him look like a savvy retail banker.

**Framing and vividness — Microsoft's BOB.** Karen Fries (deep relationship credibility: ex-recruiter who had hired many of the decision-makers) and Barry Linnett (technical expertise) proposed social-interface software with animated characters, and programmers ridiculed it. They substituted for their missing domain expertise by hiring internal guru Darrin Massena to build prototypes, market-testing those prototypes with enthusiastic users, and enlisting Stanford human-computer-interaction professors Clifford Nass and Bryon Reeves to present rigorous research to Gates. Then they made it vivid: today's software is a *supermarket* — overstuffed aisles labeled "sundries" and "condiments," and after you find everything you still have to cook it yourself, and if you're a novice the meal is bad. BOB is a *restaurant*: we find the ingredients, we bring the menu, you relax. Gates approved a full team; BOB sold over half a million copies.

**Framing — Monica Ruffo.** Franchisees of a fast-food chain resisted headquarters' new bundled low-price campaign, fearing margin damage. Rather than defend headquarters' logic, Ruffo argued the franchisees' own profits: a Tennessee pilot had markedly increased sales of fries and drinks (the two most profitable items); medium-sized meal packages rolled out to 80% of U.S. outlets had lifted franchisee fries-and-drinks sales 26%; published research showed a 10% rise in perceived value produces a 1% sales rise, and she projected the meal plan would double value perception, implying ~10% sales growth. She closed with the founder's old letter extolling the franchisees' importance and the company's low-price leadership. Standing ovation; unanimous vote to adopt.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reframe persuasion as learning and negotiating, and expect it to take weeks or months.
2. Audit expertise and relationship credibility separately, then buy, borrow, or build whichever is short.
3. Enter with judicious compromises already prepared — flexibility is itself persuasive.
4. Never open with your strongest statement of position; open with dialogue.
5. Frame every argument in terms of the listener's tangible benefit; if there isn't one, change the plan.
6. Replace charts with one vivid analogy that maps your idea onto something the audience already feels.
7. Canvass the room's emotional state before choosing your tone — and end with hope rather than doom.
