# Chapter 9: What's Your Story?
*Herminia Ibarra and Kent Lineback*

## Core Idea
In a career transition, the single factor that most determines whether people help you is your ability to tell a coherent story — one that acknowledges the discontinuity openly while grounding the change in continuity and causality, so listeners (and you) believe the move makes sense.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Coherence = Continuity + Causality** (drawing on linguist Charlotte Linde): a coherent life story presents your life as a series of unfolding, linked events — the past relates to the present, and from that trajectory a future is visible.
  - When to use: any moment you must explain a professional jump — interviews, networking, internal transfers, fundraising.
  - How, five moves:
    1. **Ground your reasons for change in character** — "I discovered I'm good at that," "that gives me real pleasure." Internal reasons let you fold learning and self-discovery into the story. External reasons ("I got fired") are facts to incorporate, not justifications; they make you look like someone who merely accepts fate.
    2. **Cite multiple reasons** — personal *and* professional, complementary rather than contradictory. Richer, more varied motives make the change more comprehensible.
    3. **Point to explanations that extend back in time** — a goal rooted in the past beats one recently conceived. Show why you couldn't pursue it then; here external causes (illness, family, being drafted) can legitimately lead.
    4. **Reframe your past in light of the change** — not concealment. We all continually retell our lives; dissect prior experience and surface the pieces that connect to the new goal.
    5. **Choose a familiar story form** — most useful for reinvention are the **maturation (coming-of-age) plot** and the **education plot**.
  - Why it matters: coherence is what generates trust, and its loss is what makes transition unbearable — the cartoon character over the abyss, legs churning, solid ground gone. Incoherence suggests, in Linde's phrase, that a life is random, accidental, unmotivated.
- **Key Elements of a Classic Story** (Aristotle's three acts): a protagonist the listener cares about → a catalyst forcing action (act one) → trials and tribulations that test and reshape character (act two) → a turning point, a point of no return that closes act two → a resolution, magnificent success or tragic failure (act three).
- **Multiple Stories for Multiple Possible Selves**: In early transition you should actively hold several options — but explore each with a *different* audience, telling the story that fits that self. This is not deception; it's how you learn which option to pursue hardest.
- **The Story-Shaped Résumé** (vs. CV): A CV is exhaustive and chronological, and any story lives only in the reader's head. A résumé should be built in three parts, all pointing at one goal: (1) the position you want; (2) a bulleted list of experience highlights demonstrating you can do that job — mine everything, including volunteer work; (3) a reverse-chronological professional summary whose descriptions are couched in the *same terms* as the highlights, so every claim above is supported below. Expect to need more than one version. Drafting it is as valuable as having it.

## Key Concepts
- **Fact tellers** — people who recite chronological credentials; listeners can't see how to help them and don't feel compelled to try.
- **Turning point** — the moment of no return; for most people, ordinary events made significant by the telling, not Damascus-road revelations.
- **Profluence of development** (John Gardner) — the felt sense that a story is moving somewhere; supplied by change, conflict, tension, and discontinuity.
- **The trust paradox of transition** — the very discontinuity that makes your story compelling also raises doubts about your dependability and predictability.
- **Maturation plot** — becoming your own person, ceasing to live by others' expectations.
- **Education plot** — change generated by growing insight and self-understanding.
- **Board of advisers** — a small designated circle of friends and colleagues who repeatedly listen and react to your evolving story.

## Mental Models
- Think of your transition as inherently dramatic: the protagonist is you, the stakes are your career, and the world has already changed — you have the raw material most storytellers lack.
- Use "the turning point is made, not found" — Lucy's triggers were ordinary events that became decisive because she assigned them significance.
- Ask of every story "does it elicit *what happened next?*" — if not, you've buried the discontinuity.
- Treat storytelling as self-reinvention, not just persuasion: each public declaration of intent commits you further and sharpens what actually excites you.

## Anti-patterns
- **The chronological laundry list**: two minutes spent on history, attention lost before the punch line — what you're actually seeking.
- **Ending with four or five disparate options you might pursue**: signals no direction and makes helping you impossible.
- **Framing a big jump as an incremental, logical extension**: listeners actively look for coherence, and glossing over the magnitude of change undermines trust rather than protecting it.
- **Making yourself safe by making yourself dull**: stripping out the transformation removes exactly what makes people care.
- **Basing the change on external causes alone**: reads as passivity.
- **Getting everything off your chest in an interview**: exploring all your options aloud doesn't build trust; it dissolves the story.

## Worked Example
**Sam Tierman, corporate HR executive → start-up CEO.** Eighteen years running HR at regional banks ended badly three times: downsized out of one job, quit another in frustration, fired from the third. The pattern he found on examination: he thrived under bosses who treated HR as strategic and seated the HR head at the executive table, and hated the work under bosses who saw HR as a body shop — "find the bodies, run the benefits, keep the government off our back." His last employer hired him under a CEO of the first type, who then left and was replaced by one of the second. On the surface he had nothing start-ups want.

He built coherence from character, not circumstance: he realized he had felt most alive during "big change fast" — a bankruptcy, a turnaround, a rapid reorganization. He supported this with multiple reasons reaching back in time: unusual projects showing entrepreneurial thinking even inside a big company, an undergraduate degree in electrical engineering, and an MBA in finance from a prestigious school — evidence of the technical and analytical bent start-ups prefer.

Then he told **three stories to three audiences**: HR contributions on a team at a consulting firm specializing in rapid change; work for a firm that buys and turns around troubled companies; and work at a start-up between its first and second or second and third financing rounds. He tested them on friends and at networking events and got referrals and interviews for all three. The market taught him which to abandon: consulting firms he respected wouldn't hire someone of his age and credentials without perfectly relevant experience, and turnaround firms didn't pan out. Start-ups did. One engaged him for consulting assignments, which he converted into a chief administrative officer role — which stamped him as a bona fide member of that world and stripped the stodgy corporate aura from his résumé. Four years and many narrative revisions later, he became CEO of a start-up commercializing technology spun out of a large company.

**Lucy Hartman, 46, IT manager → organizational development (education plot).** Coaching opened a possible new self, but she wasn't ready. She moved to a smaller company to apply what she'd learned, stayed in the high-tech world she knew, and started a master's in organizational development, hoping it would be "the impetus for a real makeover." She agonized for months, convinced it wasn't sane to quit without another job lined up. Three ordinary incidents in quick succession decided it: a conference on organizational change where she met the community she wanted to belong to; an acquisition that handed her a politically fraught new role; and her husband asking, "Are you happy? You don't look happy. When I ask how you are, all you ever say is that you're tired." She quit and went to school full time.

**Gary McCarthy, 35, strategy consultant (maturation plot).** He quit with no idea what came next. Looking back after a negative performance appraisal, he saw he had always bent to social pressure and others' views of the right path. His formulation: you'd better be sure when you wake up that you're doing what *you* want to be doing, as opposed to what you feel you ought to do or what somebody else thinks you ought to do.

## Key Takeaways
1. Lead with where you're going, not where you've been; the goal is the climax of the story.
2. Name the size of the jump explicitly — acknowledged discontinuity builds more trust than glossed-over discontinuity.
3. Root the "why" in a character trait you discovered, supported by several complementary reasons.
4. Find the oldest true version of your goal and explain what previously blocked it.
5. Pick a recognizable plot — coming-of-age or education — as the vessel.
6. Develop a separate coherent story for each possible self, and let audience reactions tell you which to pursue.
7. Rebuild your résumé backward from the target job so every highlight is supported by a job description.
8. Practice out loud, repeatedly, with a small board of advisers and eventually with strangers — each retelling both refines the story and deepens your own commitment.
