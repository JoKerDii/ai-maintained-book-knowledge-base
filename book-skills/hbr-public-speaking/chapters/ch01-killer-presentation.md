# Chapter 1: How to Give a Killer Presentation
*Chris Anderson (curator of TED)*

## Core Idea
A talk succeeds or fails on the quality of the idea, the narrative, and the speaker's passion — not on style or multimedia. You can coach out delivery problems, but you cannot coach in a story that isn't there; if you don't have an idea worth sharing, decline the invitation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Five Keys to a Great Presentation**: Frame your story → Plan your delivery → Work on stage presence → Plan the multimedia → Put it together.
  - When to use: any high-stakes talk, from a TED-style keynote to an IPO road show or VC pitch.
  - How: (1) Decide where the journey starts and ends, calibrated to what the audience already knows and cares about. (2) Choose one of three delivery modes — script/teleprompter (rejected), bullet points on note cards, or full verbatim memorization. (3) Still the lower body, gesture with hands, make sustained eye contact with 5–6 people. (4) Use visuals only if they carry meaning; never recite slide text. (5) Rehearse to final form a month out, then play to your strengths.
  - Why it works: it forces substance to be settled before style, so rehearsal polishes a real idea rather than disguising the absence of one.
- **The Talk as a Journey**: Frame the presentation as taking the audience somewhere; the two biggest decisions are where to start and where to end.
  - When to use: at the very first outline, before any slide exists.
  - How: pick a start point matched to the audience's actual knowledge (no jargon, no assumed interest), then a destination that leaves them seeing the world differently. Cut everything that doesn't move along that line.
  - Failure mode: "random pontification without narrative" — no progression, so the audience never feels it is learning.
- **The Detective-Story Structure**: Present a problem, describe the search for a solution, deliver an "aha" moment that shifts the audience's perspective.
- **The Valley of Awkwardness**: The predictable dip in the memorization learning curve where a talk is half-memorized — recited words, eyes drifting upward. Either rehearse past it or don't memorize at all; use bullet points instead.

## Key Concepts
- **Coach out vs. coach in** — Delivery flaws can be removed by coaching; a missing central idea cannot be added by it.
- **Frame the story** — Conceptualizing and bounding what you will say; the most vital part of preparation.
- **Sweeping too broadly** — The most common first-draft flaw: covering a whole career or field instead of one contribution.
- **Conversational tone** — Sound like yourself talking, not like someone orating, being authoritative, or projecting ego.
- **Vulnerability as engagement** — Visible, authentic nerves make an audience root for the speaker.
- **Report–story continuum** (Nancy Duarte sidebar) — Every presentation sits between data-rich report and engaging story; great presenters layer both like a cake.
- **Crutch words / filler** (Noah Zandan sidebar) — "Um," "ah," "like," "you know" — verbal fillers that erode credibility; replace with pauses.
- **Prep ratio** — Roughly one hour of practice per minute of performance; minimum three full trial runs.

## Mental Models
- Think of the audience as five or six friends you haven't seen in a year and are catching up with — this sets the right tone and fixes eye contact at once.
- Use "go deeper, not broader" when a draft feels thin: don't tell them about your field, tell them about your unique contribution, with specific examples.
- Treat nerves as fuel, not failure — the audience expects them, and they sharpen the mind. Just breathe deeply before going on.
- Use silence deliberately: great speakers pause two to three seconds; the average speaker pauses only ~3.5 times per minute, which is too few.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading from a script or teleprompter**: the moment the audience senses reading, intimacy evaporates and the talk turns formal.
- **Reciting slide text aloud**: information is interesting only once; hearing and seeing the same words is repetitive, and it is the teleprompter problem twice over.
- **Listing achievements / boasting about the organization**: people care about ideas and stories; organizations bore them. Talk about the problem you're solving, not your company.
- **Swaying and shifting weight**: reads as weakness and distracts. Keep the lower body still.
- **Long or self-promotional video**: clips over 60 seconds lose people; corporate infomercials and clips of yourself on TV read as an ego trip.
- **Overexplaining**: drawing out every implication insults an intelligent audience — let them draw conclusions.
- **Formulaic talks**: applying every piece of advice mechanically produces the worst talks. Take the advice on board, then make it your own.

## Worked Example
Richard Turere, a 12-year-old Masai boy, was invited to TED 2013 — painfully shy, halting English, incoherent when describing his invention. His raw material was strong: lions were killing his family's livestock at night; he noticed lamps didn't deter them but a moving torch did, so he rigged solar panels, a car battery, and a motorcycle indicator box into sequenced flashing "lion lights," and the attacks stopped. TED's coaching did not change the substance; it framed the story — finding the right place to begin and building a succinct, logical arc of events — and then had him rehearse repeatedly in front of a live audience until his confidence let his personality through. Onstage he was visibly nervous, and that made him more engaging; the talk ended in a sustained standing ovation.

Contrast case: at an energy conference, a mayor listed his city's impressive projects — it read as a report card and got boring fast. The former governor who followed shared one idea and used anecdotes from office only to illustrate it. Same raw material class, opposite effect: his subtext was "look how great I am," hers was "here's a compelling idea that would benefit us all."

## Key Takeaways
1. Settle the idea before the slides. If the central theme isn't there, decline the invitation.
2. Choose a start point from the audience's knowledge level, not your own; jargon and assumed interest lose them immediately.
3. Narrow the scope to what you can explain and illustrate with examples in the time available.
4. Pick delivery honestly: fully memorize only if you can get past the valley of awkwardness; otherwise use bullet points and focus on transitions.
5. Fix the lower body, use hand gestures, and lock eye contact with 5–6 people in different parts of the room.
6. Use slides only when the image carries meaning the words can't; many of the best talks use none.
7. Practice roughly an hour per minute of talk, be choosy about who gives you feedback (prefer experienced presenters), and finalize a month out.
