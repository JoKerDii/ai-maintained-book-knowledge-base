# Chapter 8: The Organizational Apology
*Maurice E. Schweitzer, Alison Wood Brooks, and Adam D. Galinsky*

## Core Idea
For serious transgressions, senior leaders must immediately express candor, remorse, and a commitment to change in a high-profile setting — and mean it. Most organizations botch this because they default to a legal lens, but effective apologies address the recipients' *feelings*, not liability.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Diagnostic Questions (should we apologize?)**:
  1. **Was there a violation, real or perceived?** Perceptions of responsibility matter as much as facts, and the judgment must be made fast — before the investigation concludes. Test it against the **psychological contract**: stakeholders' expectations of what's right and fair, which extend well beyond any explicit contract.
  2. **Was the violation core or noncore?** Core = central to your products, services, or mission (unsafe cars, food poisoning, Arthur Andersen certifying Enron's statements). Noncore = peripheral (Apple's transfer pricing offends people, but tax accounting isn't its central activity). Core violations threaten the mission, so a robust apology is critical and a botched one does severe damage; noncore violations allow flexibility.
  3. **How will the public react?** Weigh relative size and status: a violation by a large, powerful, high-status organization against a low-power person invites outrage. Any single complaint can go viral.
  4. **Are we committed to change?** If you can't or won't behave differently, the apology will sound hollow — but don't get so focused on the new course of action that you skip the remorse, or it reads as whitewashing.
- **The Apology Formula — Who, What, Where, When, How**:
  - **Who**: the more serious and core the violation, the more senior the apologizer, up to the CEO. When in doubt, err senior — like being overdressed rather than underdressed. Deliver directly to the people harmed; if the group is large and diffuse, make an "open" apology via press or social media.
  - **What**: three ingredients — **candor** (no equivocation; acknowledge both the harm and your responsibility), **remorse** (explicit sorry language, even self-abasing), and **commitment to change** (fire the responsible party, institute new procedures, appoint an independent investigator and pledge to implement recommendations). Explanations help — an airline delay apology lands better if it names the broken part, the fix, the timing, and why safety isn't at risk — but never sound defensive or justifying.
  - **Where**: written statements broadcast widely; on-camera video adds a personal touch; a live statement raises perceived importance; traveling to the site shows the leader cares enough to see the damage. Risk: on-site apologies are uncontrolled — if victims reject the apology in person it becomes a public confrontation, or it reads as a publicity stunt.
  - **When**: fast. Speed signals sincerity and dispels the impression that leaders are unsure of their responsibility. If facts aren't in yet, issue a **placeholder apology** rather than staying silent. The window never fully closes — a belated apology beats none.
  - **How**: informal, personal language works ("we really messed this one up"). Speech beats text for tone because nonverbal cues carry emotion, humility, and empathy — but in-person apologies are hard for leaders trained to project power, so plan and rehearse.
- **The Apology Rehearsal**: Anticipate the events that would require an apology and role-play them in advance. Industries that apologize constantly (restaurants, hotels) have it down to a science.

## Key Concepts
- **Psychological contract** — stakeholders' unwritten expectations of an organization's responsibilities.
- **Core vs. noncore violation** — whether the failure strikes at the mission itself.
- **Placeholder apology** — a fast, honest interim statement while facts are still being gathered.
- **Old self / new self** — the distancing an apology must create between the organization that erred and the one that won't again.
- **The legal lens** — treating an apology as an admission of liability rather than an act of empathy; research shows hospitals that allowed or mandated apologies saw litigation *reduced*.
- **Strategic unapologeticness** — sometimes refusing to apologize communicates values credibly (Cisco's John Chambers declining to apologize for unequal innovation payouts because innovation mattered more to him than equitable pay; McDonald's ads promoting the Big Mac as "not Greek yogurt" and a sandwich that "will never be kale").

## Mental Models
- Think of an apology as low cost and often value-creating: it defuses tension, and litigation fears are usually unfounded.
- Use "the more core and the more people affected, the more every dimension must be maxed out" — senior who, sincere what, fast when, high-profile where, empathetic how.
- Recognize the psychological pull to delay: apologizing rearranges the status hierarchy and makes you temporarily beholden, so defensiveness and blame-shifting feel natural and are usually wrong.
- Ask "who is actually the victim here?" before drafting — misaddressing the apology destroys it.

## Anti-patterns
- **The three-step non-apology** (Facebook, 2014): defending the 9,000-word user agreement as informed consent for a week, then a COO's half-hearted regret about "poorly communicating," then a CTO statement three months later conceding "there are things we should have done differently" while never saying *sorry* or *apologize*.
- **Waiting for the investigation / hoping it blows over** (Coca-Cola, 1999): after a Belgian schoolboy fell ill, hundreds reported fevers, dizziness, and nausea; the company blamed bad carbon dioxide at an Antwerp plant, and CEO Douglas Ivester decided to "take a lower profile." Over 50 million beverages were pulled in France, Germany, and Belgium before he expressed regret more than a week later.
- **Tone-deaf self-pity** (BP's Tony Hayward, 2010): "There's no one who wants this thing over more than I do. I'd like my life back." He resigned weeks later — an object lesson in improvised apology.
- **Apologizing to the wrong victim** (Lululemon's Chip Wilson, 2013): after blaming see-through yoga pants on the women wearing them, his video said "I'm sad for the people of Lululemon … I'm sorry to have put you all through this" — addressed to employees, not customers.
- **The contentless apology** (Goldman's Lloyd Blankfein, 2009): a vague apology for unspecified industry acts; as the New York Times noted, he never said what he was sorry for or to whom, nor how the bank would change.
- **Failing to anticipate obvious perception risks**: Mattel's Hello Barbie recorded and uploaded children's conversations, and the "eavesdropping Barbie" reaction was foreseeable.

## Worked Example
**United Breaks Guitars (2008).** United allegedly damaged a Canadian musician's guitar on a Halifax-to-Nebraska flight and then put him through a Kafkaesque customer service process. Pre-internet, this stays invisible. Instead he wrote a song, "United Breaks Guitars," and posted the video: ~15,000 views on day one, more than 14 million since. United's managing director of customer solutions, Rob Bradford, eventually phoned him, apologized directly, and asked whether the airline could *use the video* to improve its customer service — converting the artifact of the failure into the commitment-to-change component.

**The full formula executed well — Goldman's second attempt.** After the public rebuke of Blankfein's vague statement, he held another press conference admitting Goldman had participated "in things that were clearly wrong and we have reasons to regret and apologize for," and the firm pledged $500 million to help small businesses recover from the recession — candor, remorse, and commitment in one package.

**Small-scale mastery — the Ritz-Carlton.** A missed wake-up call made a guest late for an important meeting. The front desk manager apologized immediately and sent up a complimentary breakfast. That evening the guest returned to a handwritten apology from the general manager plus fresh strawberries, dried fruit, and candy. She spent the following days telling friends about the five-star service rather than the failure — the apology left the relationship stronger than before the error.

**Change made concrete — Vancouver Taxi Association (2014).** A driver abandoned a mother and her sick child roadside because he wrongly believed his company wouldn't accept a hospital-issued taxi voucher. The association expressed remorse *and* suspended the driver *and* instituted an explicit policy: all cabs accept all local hospital vouchers, always.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the four diagnostic questions before debating wording; the core/noncore answer sets how much the apology must carry.
2. Drop the liability lens — apologies address feelings, and evidence suggests they reduce rather than invite litigation.
3. Match seniority to severity, and address the people actually harmed.
4. Include all three "what" ingredients; a change plan without remorse reads as whitewashing, and remorse without change reads as hollow.
5. Move fast; use a placeholder apology when the facts aren't in.
6. Use informal, plain, self-implicating language — "we suck at this, I suck at this" outperforms corporate hedging.
7. Rehearse apologies before you need them, and treat the moment as a chance to end up better positioned than before the error.
