# Chapter 2: How to Become an Authentic Speaker
*Nick Morgan*

## Core Idea
Gesture precedes conscious thought, which precedes words — so rehearsing specific body language makes you gesture a beat too late and read as fake. Rehearse the four underlying *intents* (be open, connect, be passionate, listen) instead, and the body language emerges naturally at the right moment.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Aims (Intents)**: Rehearse a speech four separate times, each time inhabiting one intent — **be open**, **connect**, **be passionate**, **listen**.
  - When to use: preparing any presentation where credibility and buy-in matter more than information transfer; especially when audiences have called you "calculated," "phoned in," or "not real."
  - How:
    1. **Open** — practice while imagining you're speaking to someone you're totally relaxed with (spouse, close friend, child). Register how it *feels*, not how it looks. If imagining fails, rehearse in front of a patient friend and push yourself to be open.
    2. **Connect** — practice while imagining a young child you know isn't heeding you and you must capture their attention; then upgrade the child to a distractible teenager to practice *keeping* attention.
    3. **Passionate** — ask what's at stake and what result you want; focus on *why* you're speaking and how you feel about it, not what you'll say. Raise the stakes: imagine one audience member can take everything away unless you win them over.
    4. **Listen** — imagine what the audience will be feeling as you step up (excited? worried about layoffs?) and picture yourself watching them closely for response signals.
  - Why it works: it produces genuine felt states, and genuine states fire gesture *before* speech — the natural sequence audiences unconsciously check for.
- **The Second Conversation**: The nonverbal channel, which starts first — in the instant after an emotion fires but before it's articulated. When words and body contradict, audiences believe the body every time.
  - When to use: diagnosing why a technically flawless talk fell flat.
  - Failure mode: traditional coaching ("maintain eye contact," "spread your arms," "come out from behind the podium") installs gestures that arrive simultaneous with or after the words — audiences can't name what's wrong, but they feel it.

## Key Concepts
- **Authenticity** — the perceived and real alignment of message, emotion, and body; a leadership attribute, not a speaking flourish.
- **Intent** — an underlying aim you embody physically and emotionally, as opposed to a gesture you execute.
- **Winging it** — refusing to prepare so as not to look over-rehearsed; produces off-kilter body language under stress and reads as "learning as I go."
- **The authenticity paradox** — real authenticity is achieved through a deliberate, calculated rehearsal process.
- **Openness as the ante, connection as what keeps them playing** — openness buys entry; connection sustains attention.
- **Signposting** (John Coleman sidebar) — explicit verbal markers ("to begin," "secondly," "finally") that would be inelegant in writing but are essential in speech.
- **A speech is not an essay** (Coleman sidebar) — reading runs ~300 wpm, listening only ~150–160 wpm; 10 minutes of speech is only ~1,300 words.

## Mental Models
- Think of nonverbal signals as *predictions*: unstudied gestures indicate what a person will think and say next, so words are after-the-fact explanations of why we just gestured.
- Use "feel it, don't think it" when rehearsing — like grooving a golf swing, make only tiny mental notes so they don't block the feeling you're trying to store and replicate.
- Use the audience's bodies as live data: pick up the pace, vary language, ask an impromptu question, or cut a section based on what you read.
- When taking questions, listen with your whole body — hold still as if the speaker might say something you dare not miss; the leaning-in and nodding will happen on their own.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rehearsing individual gestures**: they fire late, and the audience senses the wheels turning.
- **Winging it to seem spontaneous**: stress plus no preparation produces exactly the shakiness you feared.
- **Waiting until the end to make contact with the audience**: connection established late can't rescue a talk.
- **Focusing on what you want to say rather than why you're saying it**: produces crystal-clear, entirely dull commentary.
- **Reciting strings of statistics or citations**: in speech, lead or close a point with one relevant number and carry the argument with vivid stories instead.
- **Assuming the audience shares your urgency**: the unexamined premise that quietly kills the ask.

## Worked Example
Carol, a VP of sales (a composite client), opens a companywide meeting flawlessly by the textbook: a field-rep anecdote, colorful growth slides, animated gestures, and a walk to the stage edge to challenge the room with a stretch goal. She rehearsed with trusted colleagues who liked her message. Yet the room stays flat.

Working the four intents rewrites her, one layer at a time:
- **Open** — as she practices imagining a relaxed conversation, her face lights up and her shoulders drop; she discovers she'd been coming across as so serious she alienated audiences.
- **Connect** — she notices she habitually waits until the very end to make contact. Her next talk *opens* by acknowledging the salespeople who drove the year's results, and the connection runs throughout.
- **Passionate** — she identifies what she actually cares about (beating a close competitor) and lets that fuel show; she reads as more human immediately.
- **Listen** — reading the room over several speeches, she realizes she wrongly assumed her salespeople shared her urgency about that competitor, so she spends real time up front explaining why stretch goals matter.

A parallel case: a retiring consulting-firm senior partner's talk on commitment and hard work was clear and dull. Digging for the emotion beneath it, she remembered her mother, a dancer, who taught her to persist through obstacles and who at 92 had never let career pain obscure her joy in performing. She put her mother into the talk. She cried through most of the rehearsals — and the delivered speech was memorable.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose flat talks as an intent problem, not a technique problem.
2. Rehearse four times, one intent per pass, feeling each state rather than analyzing it.
3. Never rehearse specific gestures; let felt intent generate them so they arrive before the words.
4. Establish connection at the *start* of the talk, not the close.
5. Find the emotion under your content — often a personal memory — and let it into the room.
6. Read the audience live and adapt pace, language, or content on the fly; that requires knowing your material cold.
7. Write for the ear: short, signposted, story-heavy, and about 130 words per minute of airtime.
