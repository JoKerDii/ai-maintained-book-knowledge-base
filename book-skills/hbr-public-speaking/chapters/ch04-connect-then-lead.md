# Chapter 4: Connect, Then Lead
*Amy J.C. Cuddy, Matthew Kohut, and John Neffinger*

## Core Idea
People judge you on two dimensions — warmth and strength — and they read warmth first. Most leaders lead with competence, which is exactly backward: establish warmth first, then strength lands as reassurance rather than threat.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Warmth–Competence Matrix (the two dimensions of social judgment)**: Every social judgment answers two questions — "What are this person's intentions toward me?" (warmth, communion, trustworthiness) and "Is he or she capable of acting on those intentions?" (strength, agency, competence). These two dimensions account for more than 90% of the variance in positive/negative impressions.
  - When to use: diagnosing why your influence is stalling, or preparing for a first impression with a new team, board, or audience.
  - How: place yourself on both axes and read the predicted reaction — **competent but cold → envy** (respect mixed with resentment; you're exposed to harsh reprisal); **warm but incompetent → pity** (compassion mixed with neglect); **neither → contempt**; **both → trust and influence**.
  - Why it works: warmth is the conduit of influence — it lets ideas be heard and absorbed. Failure mode: leading with strength elicits fear, a "hot" emotion that burns into memory, undermines cognition, creativity, and problem solving, and produces outward compliance without private conformity.
- **The Happy Warrior**: Combine high warmth with high strength — face troubles without being troubled; behavior isn't relaxed, but emotions are.
  - When to use: crises and high-uncertainty periods, when people tolerate uncertainty far better if the leader seems calm, clearheaded, and courageous.
  - How: build a genuine sense of personal control (which buffers stress), then pair assertiveness and authority with a big smile and quick wit — the Ann Richards model.
  - Physiological signature: relatively high testosterone, relatively low cortisol, regardless of gender.
- **Inside-Out vs. Outside-In**: Prepare by *getting into the right state of mind* (inside-out) rather than consciously executing specific nonverbal behaviors in the moment (outside-in) — method acting rather than classical acting. Inside-out is generally more effective; efforts to control nonverbal signals directly come off wooden.
- **Power Poses**: Open, expansive, space-occupying postures (hands on hips, feet apart) held for two minutes before a social encounter; research shows increased testosterone and decreased cortisol. These build *personal* power — agency and self-regulation — not dominance over others.

## Key Concepts
- **Warmth as the conduit of influence** — trust must exist before ideas can be heard.
- **Private conformity vs. outward compliance** — trust changes attitudes and beliefs; fear changes only behavior.
- **Spontaneous trait inferences** — snap judgments from a brief glance at a face; warmth is picked up faster than competence.
- **The need to affiliate** — ostracism, even by strangers, produces pain akin to strong physical pain.
- **"Us" vs. "them"** — as soon as you become "the management," you start losing people; be the aspirational member of the group.
- **Power reduces perspective-taking** — having power over others diminishes your ability to see them as individuals.
- **Poise** — stillness plus good posture; telegraphs equilibrium and stability.
- **Impostor feelings** — if you see yourself as an impostor, others will too; self-doubt destroys projected presence.
- **Tragedy of the commons (organizational)** — in low-trust workplaces, people hoard effort because reciprocity isn't assured.

## Mental Models
- Use "before people decide what they think of your message, they decide what they think of *you*" as the ordering rule for any persuasive communication.
- Think of a strongly disliked manager's odds of being rated a good leader as about 1 in 2,000 (27 of 51,836 leaders in the Zenger/Folkman study were bottom-quartile likability and top-quartile effectiveness).
- Use "if you want colleagues to agree with you, first agree with them" — validating a shared worldview reads as empathy *and* as common sense, the ultimate qualification for being listened to.
- Treat stand-up-straight-and-smile as the single most efficient move available: posture projects strength without the warmth cost of cutting gestures, furrowed brows, or an elevated chin.

## Techniques: Projecting Warmth
- **Find the right level**: Don't amp up enthusiasm and volume — unearned adulation reads as faking or indiscriminate fawning. Instead, lower your pitch and volume as if comforting a friend; aim for a "leveling with you, no pretense" tone. Occasionally share a personal story in a confiding register.
- **Validate feelings**: Name the elephant in the room. "I know everybody's feeling a lot of uncertainty right now, and it's unsettling."
- **Smile and mean it**: A real smile engages the eyes (crow's feet), not just the mouth; it is contagious and self-reinforcing. Find some genuine reason to feel happy; introverts can single out one person to focus on.

## Techniques: Projecting Strength
- **Feel in command**: connect with yourself first; power poses help you shed inhibitions before a pitch or speech.
- **Stand up straight**: reach your full height by straightening the spine's S-curve — not the military chest-out attention pose.
- **Get ahold of yourself**: move deliberately and precisely to a specific spot, then be still. Twitching and fidgeting signal loss of control.

## Anti-patterns
- **Leading with credentials and competence**: alienates colleagues and elicits fear rather than trust.
- **Consciously executing nonverbal cues in the moment**: produces woodenness — the outside-in trap.
- **Smiling with eyebrows raised** (at anyone over age five): reads as over-eager to please and signals anxiety, which is contagious; costs more in strength than it gains in warmth.
- **Assuming your own trustworthiness needs no demonstration**: we're sure of our own intentions, yet trustworthiness is the first thing we look for in others.
- **Choosing competence training for yourself and soft-skills training for everyone else**: the Abele/Wojciszke asymmetry — we narrate ourselves through competence and others through warmth.

## Worked Example
A manager who had risen through the ranks as a highly analytic engineer couldn't connect with her employees. She projected competence and determination in abundance — and almost no warmth. Coaching noticed one exception: when she talked about where she grew up and what the tight-knit community in her neighborhood taught her about life, her demeanor relaxed and she broke into a broad, genuine smile. The intervention was small and structural: open meetings and presentations with a brief anecdote about her upbringing. That single move gave the same competent manager a warm, relatable side her colleagues could trust — and it worked because the warmth was real rather than performed.

## Key Takeaways
1. Establish warmth before demonstrating strength; reversing the order elicits fear.
2. Diagnose your position on the warmth–competence matrix by the emotion you elicit: envy means cold, pity means weak.
3. Prepare inside-out — set the state, not the gestures.
4. Open by validating what the room is actually feeling, especially in reorganizations and crises.
5. Lower your voice rather than raising your enthusiasm to convey warmth.
6. Combine full-height posture with genuine stillness for strength; add a real smile for both at once.
7. Stay a member of the group; the moment you read as "them," influence drains away.
