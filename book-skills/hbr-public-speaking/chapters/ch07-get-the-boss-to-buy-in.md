# Chapter 7: Get the Boss to Buy In
*Susan J. Ashford and James R. Detert*

## Core Idea
Senior executives dismiss good ideas from below mainly because they don't perceive the idea's relevance to organizational performance. **Issue selling** is the middle manager's craft of altering that perception through an extended, multi-tactic campaign — not a single presentation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Seven Issue-Selling Tactics**: Successful sellers use these significantly more than failed ones; combining all seven accounted for about **40% of the difference between successes and failures**.
  1. **Tailor your pitch** — the single tactic most associated with success. Learn the audience's specific blend of goals, values, and knowledge; ask decision makers what they expect before drafting anything.
  2. **Frame the issue** — connect it to organizational priorities, name the business benefits, bundle it with related initiatives, highlight the opportunity.
  3. **Manage emotions on both sides** — regulate your own passion so it doesn't read as anger, and actively protect the decision maker's ego.
  4. **Get the timing right** — catch the wave of a trend; know where the decision is in its life cycle.
  5. **Involve others** — mobilize allies, neutralize blockers, convert fence-sitters; include people the *target* trusts, and people whose networks don't overlap yours.
  6. **Adhere to norms** — learn what data your leaders trust, how they like to receive it, and whether formal or informal is expected.
  7. **Suggest solutions** — never bring a bare problem; if you have no fix, propose a credible process for finding one.
- **Framing hierarchy — business > opportunity > threat > moral**:
  - *Business framing* is the safest and most effective: spell out concrete benefits, ideally for multiple departments.
  - *Opportunity framing* ("something we shouldn't miss") gives the audience a sense of control and inspires optimism; successful sellers used it significantly more.
  - *Threat framing* is a coin flip — it can produce fight or flight; usage didn't differ between successes and failures.
  - *Moral framing* is the weakest: the few instances in the research were tied to failures or uneven results, because principles peddled aggressively read as a judgment of the audience's character.
- **Three Knowledge Types for Sellers**: **strategic** (organizational goals, plans, and each decision maker's role in them), **relational** (who is affected, who cares, who will object), and **normative** (what data, channels, and formats this organization actually respects).
- **The Informal-Then-Formal Sequence**: Roll ideas out informally early to gauge interest, then switch to formal presentation. Successful sellers used *more* formal and *fewer* informal tactics overall — but the sequencing is what distinguishes them.

## Key Concepts
- **Issue selling** — the concept introduced by Jane Dutton and Susan Ashford; winning attention and resources for an idea from below.
- **Catching the wave** — positioning your issue as part of a trend more people are starting to care about.
- **Bundling** — attaching a small idea to a larger initiative (elder-care leave becomes a work/life balance issue).
- **Complainer vs. change agent** — how decision makers classify subordinates who show unregulated negative emotion.
- **Choose your battles** — decide with two questions: How important is this to my company? How important is it to me?
- **Conditions for speaking up** — managers voice ideas when they identify with the organization, have a positive relationship with the audience, feel psychologically safe, believe someone above will act, and care enough to spend the energy.
- **Solution-suggestion trap** — if people only raise issues they can already solve, organizations where problems outpace fixes are at a disadvantage, and group-solvable problems get decided badly.

## Mental Models
- Think of a pitch as a campaign with groundwork, pacing, and patience — the big meeting is the visible tip, not the process.
- When a deadline is far off and decision makers are still exploring, prefer open-ended inquiry over a specific proposal; when a launch or release is imminent, propose now.
- Use "who should carry this?" as an explicit decision: going only to your boss risks the idea dying or being pitched badly; going over their head costs political capital. Ask to accompany whoever sells on your behalf, and if you go direct, keep your boss in the loop.
- Treat an idea that indicts the status quo — or the audience's intelligence, judgment, or morality — as an uphill battle regardless of technique.

## Anti-patterns
- **Letting impressive work speak for itself**: without framing that creates urgency, a strong performer is someone to promote "whenever convenient."
- **Leading with moral argument**: reads as character judgment and triggers defensiveness.
- **Unregulated frustration**: decision makers reclassify you from change agent to complainer.
- **Making the boss look bad for a past decision**: the reason Healy foregrounded that reviews hadn't existed a year earlier.
- **Presenting a problem with no proposed fix or process**: signals you haven't done the thinking or respected their time.
- **Ignoring house norms**: at one company, senior managers claimed to want blue-sky thinking but chastised anyone not using approved slide templates — employees sold formally and innovation suffered.
- **Going it alone**: coalitions generate buy-in faster and at larger scale.

## Worked Example
**John Healy (engineering manager, energy company).** He wanted to replace a gas-scrubbing system — one his own general manager had personally selected only a year before. Three moves, three tactics: (1) *managed the GM's emotions* by tactfully noting that user reviews of the new technology had become available only in the past several months, so the original decision hadn't been wrong at the time; (2) *suggested a solution backed by data* — a detailed side-by-side comparison drawing on implementations at comparable plants, showing more efficient contaminant removal and roughly $700,000 a year in savings; (3) when the GM was still on the fence, *involved a trusted outsider* — a bio-gas expert his boss already respected. The company invested and adopted the new system.

**The Canadian sales reorganization (regional sales manager, international oil company).** Sales teams were organized by region rather than by customer, so teams offered competing deals to the same multi-region clients; incentives were misaligned, the customer experience was fragmented, and most reps were on salary rather than commission. A competitor poached more than half the division's sales force, and nearly all the top performers left. His first attempt failed: he raised concerns with his boss and a few executives, who said the answer was to push people harder. So he switched tactics and *asked other, more powerful leaders what they expected from sales* — learning, for instance, that the new VP of marketing and sales for Canada wanted to stop teams from undercutting each other in front of clients. He then rebuilt the pitch around their goals *and* the CEO's publicly announced target of doubling revenue in four years: assign teams to clients (fixes the VP's problem), fix ~40% annual rep attrition with commission-based pay to attract and retain seasoned people, and train greener reps in relationship management. The VP approved and funded it. Results: a dozen experienced hires, exactly one departure over four years, turnover costs near zero, and a $75,000 training investment that paid for itself via a sales contest that generated $2.7 million in new business in a single week. Revenue doubled in five years rather than four — and executives stopped blaming laziness.

**The five-year database campaign (chief investment officer, financial firm).** He wanted a proprietary real estate database in a lean, technology-resistant organization where everyone saw it as a luxury. Roughly every six months for about five years, he floated the idea at moments when the database would obviously have helped, with a tech-savvy ally in asset management vocally agreeing. The tipping point came when he found a *second* department's need: the database would help accounting meet public-reporting and audit requirements. With business benefits spelled out across multiple departments, the firm subscribed.

**Peru timing (managing director, Ecuadoran holding company's luxury division).** He had the idea in 2007 but held it — Peru had recent civil unrest and his home market still had headroom. He waited until 2009, when Peru had the best-performing stock market in the world and Ecuador had saturated. His team visited and read the signals: modern minimalist construction replacing the high-walled buildings of the guerrilla era, only one prominent shopping mall, hard luxury goods scarce or sold informally, and Starbucks cafés full and expanding. They proposed a watch-focused luxury boutique, arguing department stores couldn't deliver the luxury experience. He also matched his conservative firm's norms: informal conversations first, proxy businesses in other industries, customer and partner interviews, then a formal review with traditional financial tools and outsourced market studies. Two stores opened November 2010; the first sold its entire perfume inventory on opening day (one customer bought all the ink stock for his luxury pen out of fear it wouldn't be available again). That store produced 40% of divisional profits over three years — and by 2011 every prestigious luxury brand had entered Peru, but this company got there first.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask decision makers what they want *before* drafting your proposal; the tailored pitch is the highest-yield tactic.
2. Anchor every idea to an announced organizational priority — a CEO revenue target, a stated strategic goal.
3. Frame in business benefits and opportunity; use threat sparingly and moral argument almost never.
4. Protect the decision maker from looking wrong about the past.
5. Wait for the wave: shifting priorities, new players, changed preoccupations, external trends.
6. Build a coalition that includes people your target already trusts and networks that don't duplicate yours.
7. Start informal to gauge interest, then go formal to create seriousness and pressure to respond.
8. Always arrive with a solution — or with a defensible process for finding one.
