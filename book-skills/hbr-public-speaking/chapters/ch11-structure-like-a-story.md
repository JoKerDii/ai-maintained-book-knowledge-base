# Chapter 11: Structure Your Presentation Like a Story
*Nancy Duarte* (Bonus article from HBR Guide to Persuasive Presentations)

## Core Idea
The most effective presenters create tension by oscillating between **what is** and **what could be** — establishing the status quo, revealing a better way, and using the resulting gap to move an audience from complacency to action within Aristotle's three-part structure.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Persuasive Story Pattern**: Beginning (what is → what could be) → Middle (repeated oscillation between what is and what could be) → End (call to action → new bliss).
  - When to use: any presentation whose purpose is to change a mindset or a behavior — quarterly updates, strategy rollouts, pitches, change communications.
  - How:
    1. **Beginning — describe life as the audience knows it.** They should be nodding in recognition because you're articulating what they already understand. That recognition creates a bond and opens them to your ideas.
    2. **Beginning — introduce what could be.** The gap throws the audience slightly off balance, which is the point: it jars them out of complacency.
    3. **Middle — keep playing up the contrast.** Alternate what is / what could be repeatedly. With each swing, "what could be" grows more alluring.
    4. **End — call to action, then new bliss.** Include the ask, but make it inspiring rather than a burdensome list of to-dos, and describe how much better their world will be once they adopt your ideas.
  - Why it works: it borrows the storyteller's engine — a conflict that needs resolving — and Aristotle's beginning-middle-end shape makes the message easy to digest, remember, and retell. Failure mode: ending on a task list, which leaves the audience with obligation and no reward.
- **New Bliss**: The closing depiction of the future state. Defining future rewards shows people that getting on board is worth their effort — meeting *their* needs, not just yours.

## Key Concepts
- **What is** — the audience's current reality, stated so plainly they nod along.
- **What could be** — the alternative you're proposing; always stated as a contrast, never in isolation.
- **The gap** — the productive imbalance between the two, which the rest of the presentation exists to bridge.
- **Oscillation** — repeated movement between the two poles through the middle section; the source of sustained tension.
- **Call to action** — the explicit ask, framed to inspire rather than to burden.
- **Aristotle's three-part structure** — beginning, middle, end; chosen for memorability and retellability.

## Mental Models
- Think of the opening as earning the right to be heard: recognition first, disruption second.
- Use "never state a benefit without its contrasting reality" — a "what could be" with no "what is" beside it has no tension and no motive force.
- Treat audience discomfort as the mechanism, not a side effect: being thrown off balance is what breaks complacency.
- Close on reward, not on effort — the last thing they hear should be what they get.

## Worked Example
A Q3 update where revenues are down but employees must be motivated to make it up.

**Beginning**
- *What is*: We fell short of our Q3 financial goals partly because we're understaffed and everyone's spread too thin.
- *What could be*: But what if we could solve the worst of our problems by bringing in a couple of powerhouse clients? Well, we can.

**Middle** (oscillating)
- *What is*: We missed our Q3 forecast by 15%.
- *What could be*: Q4 numbers must be strong for us to pay out bonuses.
- *What is*: We have six new clients on our roster.
- *What could be*: Two of them have the potential to bring in more revenue than our best clients do now.
- *What is*: The new clients will require extensive retooling in manufacturing.
- *What could be*: We'll be bringing in experts from Germany to help.

**End**
- *Call to action*: It will take extra work from all departments to make Q4 numbers, but we can deliver products to our important new clients on time and with no errors.
- *New bliss*: I know everyone's running on fumes — but hang in there. This is our chance to pull together like a championship team, and things will get easier if we make this work. The reward if we meet our Q4 targets? Bonuses, plus days off at the end of the year.

Notice the structural discipline: every obstacle named in a "what is" line is answered by the immediately following "what could be," and the ending pairs an honest acknowledgment of exhaustion with a concrete, specific reward.

## Anti-patterns
- **Ending with a to-do list**: leaves the audience holding obligations with no vision of payoff.
- **Opening with your vision**: without the shared baseline of "what is," there's no bond and no gap.
- **A single contrast then a flat middle**: tension dissipates; the middle must keep oscillating.
- **A generic or abstract new bliss**: "we'll be well positioned" motivates no one — name bonuses, days off, specific relief.
- **Hiding the difficulties**: the retooling problem belongs in the talk, immediately followed by its answer.

## Key Takeaways
1. Open by articulating the audience's reality until they nod, then introduce the alternative.
2. Build the middle as an alternating sequence, not a linear argument.
3. Pair every problem you raise with the specific thing that resolves it.
4. Make the call to action ambitious but achievable, and state it plainly.
5. Close on new bliss with concrete rewards that meet the audience's needs.
6. Use the three-act shape deliberately — it's what makes the message retellable by people who weren't in the room.
