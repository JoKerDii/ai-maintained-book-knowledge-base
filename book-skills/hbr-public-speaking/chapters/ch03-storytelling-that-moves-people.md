# Chapter 3: Storytelling That Moves People: A Conversation with Screenwriting Coach Robert McKee
*Robert McKee, interviewed by Bronwyn Fryer*

## Core Idea
Rhetoric persuades only intellectually, and people are not moved to act by reason alone; story persuades by uniting an idea with an emotion. The engine of every story is the collision between subjective expectation and an uncooperative objective reality — so put your antagonists in the foreground, not under the carpet.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Story = How and Why Life Changes**: Balance → **inciting incident** → protagonist's subjective expectations crash into uncooperative objective reality → struggle against accumulating antagonists → truth discovered.
  - When to use: any pitch, all-hands, or investor presentation where you need people to *act*, not merely agree.
  - How: (1) Establish life in balance — the routine everyone expects to continue. (2) Introduce the inciting incident that throws it out of balance (a death, a new job, a customer threatening to leave). (3) Force the protagonist to dig deeper, work with scarce resources, decide under risk. (4) Accumulate antagonists so the audience genuinely doubts a happy ending. (5) Land the turn.
  - Why it works: cognitive psychologists find the mind assembles experience into exactly this shape — desire, then struggle against what blocks it. We remember stories and forget lists and bullet points.
- **The Four Storyteller's Questions**: The discovery interview a storyteller runs on their own material.
  - When to use: unearthing the real story hidden inside a company or a project.
  - How: (1) What does my protagonist want in order to restore balance? (Desire is a core need, not a shopping list — satisfy it and the story stops.) (2) What is keeping the protagonist from it? Antagonists come from people, society, time, space, nature, and every object in it. (3) How would the protagonist decide to act in the face of those forces? — the heart is revealed in choices made under pressure. (4) Lean back and ask: *Do I believe this? Is it neither exaggeration nor soft-soaping? Is this an honest telling, though heaven may fall?*
- **Text vs. Subtext (Skepticism)**: Believe nothing at face value; the real thoughts and feelings of individuals and institutions are unconscious and unexpressed. Look behind the mask — anyone working that hard to look fierce is a marshmallow; genuinely hard people make no effort.

## Key Concepts
- **Inciting incident** — the event that throws life out of balance and starts the story.
- **Protagonist / antagonist** — the actor pursuing desire, and every force blocking it: nature, institutions, rivals, time, money, self-doubt.
- **Desire** — the core need driving the protagonist; the "blood of a story."
- **Accumulation of antagonists** — stacking obstacles to build suspense and genuine doubt about the outcome.
- **Fear vs. dread** — fear is not knowing what will happen; dread is knowing and being unable to stop it.
- **The mask** — the persona individuals and institutions present; compelling stories live beneath it.
- **Companyspeak** — PowerPoint slides, dry memos, and hyperbolic corporate communications that draw cynicism and dismissal.
- **Self-knowledge** — the root of all great storytelling; characters are built by asking "if I were this person in these circumstances, what would I do?"

## Mental Models
- Use story instead of rhetoric when you need action: with rhetoric, listeners are arguing with you in their heads using their own authorities, statistics, and experience.
- Think of positive spin as *evidence of dishonesty* — the audience knows it's never that easy and knows your competitor doesn't wear a black hat, so boilerplate optimism foments distrust.
- Treat the dark side as your credibility engine: acknowledging real antagonism makes you truthful, and truthfulness generates positive, realistic energy in listeners.
- Use "leaders we follow are people we believe" — a CEO who looks the room in the eye and says "we'll be lucky as hell to get through this, but here's what I think we should do" gets listened to.

## Worked Example
**Chemcorp** — a biotech start-up whose CEO must persuade Wall Street bankers to invest.

*The rhetoric version*: We've discovered a compound that prevents heart attacks. Here is the market size, the business plan, the org chart. The bankers nod politely, stifle yawns, and think about better-positioned competitors.

*The story version*: It starts with the CEO's father, dead of a heart attack — **nature is the first antagonist**. In his grief he realizes that a chemical indicator could have saved his father. The company discovers a protein present in the blood just before heart attacks and builds a cheap, easy-to-administer test. **New antagonist: the FDA.** The first application is rejected. New research shows the test performs better than anyone expected, and a second application is approved. **Meanwhile Chemcorp is running out of money, and a key partner defects to found a rival** — now it's a fight-to-the-finish patent race. By this point the bankers are on the edge of their seats, genuinely unsure it ends well. Then: "We won the race, we got the patent, we're poised to go public and save a quarter-million lives a year." The bankers throw money at him. McKee reports that after he consulted with a dozen corporations whose principals told stories like this to Wall Street, all of them got their money.

*The untold story*: An immigrant assembly-line worker — the fastest window assembler on the line — suffered a severe head injury and was accused by the carmaker of fraud. Waiting for a titanium plate to be put in his head, all he wanted was to get back to work, and he still took pride in the company that had falsely accused him. Had that CEO told the tale of his managers recognizing their error and rewarding the man's dedication, every employee who heard it would have redoubled their effort.

## Anti-patterns
- **Beginning-to-end tales of results meeting expectations**: boring, banal, and nobody believes them.
- **Sweeping the dirty laundry under the carpet**: hiding difficulties and antagonists costs you both drama and trust.
- **Positive press releases and hypothetical rosy scenarios**: they work *against* you; if CEOs don't believe their own spin doctors, why should the public?
- **Statistics and accounting reports as proof of integrity**: statistics are used to tell lies, and accounting reports are often "BS in a ball gown" — witness Enron and WorldCom.
- **Confusing storytelling with exaggeration or manipulation**: the honest-telling test (question four) is the guard against it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Choose story over rhetoric when the goal is action; use rhetoric only when intellectual assent is enough.
2. Open by putting the problem in the foreground, then show how you overcame it — that positioning makes you look dynamic, not weak.
3. Build suspense by accumulating antagonists until the audience doubts the ending.
4. Run the four storyteller's questions on your own material before drafting anything.
5. Find the protagonist's true desire — a core need, not a wish list.
6. Reveal character (yours, your company's) through choices made under pressure.
7. Be a skeptic: hunt for the subtext behind every mask, including your own.
