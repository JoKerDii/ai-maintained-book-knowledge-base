# Patterns

## Frame the Talk as a Journey
**When to use**: At the very first outline, before any slide exists (Ch 1).
**How**: Decide where to start (calibrated to what the audience already knows and cares about) and where to end (a changed view of the world). Limit scope to what you can explain and illustrate with examples in the available time. Go deeper, not broader — your unique contribution, not your whole field.
**Trade-offs**: Narrow framing means abandoning material you're proud of; overcorrecting into overexplanation insults an intelligent audience.

## Detective-Story Structure
**When to use**: When the content is an insight or discovery (Ch 1, Ch 3).
**How**: Present a problem → describe the search for a solution → deliver the "aha" that shifts perspective.
**Trade-offs**: Requires you to genuinely have a problem worth solving; without one it becomes contrived suspense.

## Rehearse the Four Intents
**When to use**: Any talk where credibility and buy-in matter more than information transfer (Ch 2).
**How**: Practice the speech four times, once per intent. Open: imagine speaking to someone you're totally relaxed with. Connect: imagine capturing an inattentive child's attention, then a distractible teenager's. Passionate: identify what's at stake and let the underlying emotion into every word. Listen: imagine what the audience will be feeling and watch for their signals.
**Trade-offs**: Slower than gesture drills and feels indirect, but rehearsed gestures fire late and read as fake.

## Replace Fillers with Pauses
**When to use**: Whenever "um," "ah," "like," or "you know" recur (Ch 1).
**How**: Watch video or read a transcript of your last talk to identify your crutch words. Pair each with a physical action (tap your leg) or have a friend clap when you use one. Record yourself narrating your day using pauses instead of fillers. Prepare thoroughly — nervousness drives filler.
**Trade-offs**: Pauses feel far longer to the speaker than to the audience; great speakers pause two to three seconds.

## The 1:60 Preparation Ratio
**When to use**: Sizing rehearsal for an important talk (Ch 1).
**How**: Roughly one hour of practice per minute of performance; minimum three full trial runs. Finalize content a month before the event; rehearse alone and in front of an audience. Be choosy about feedback givers — prefer experienced presenters.
**Trade-offs**: Not every presentation is worth that investment; if you can't get past the valley of awkwardness, use bullet points on note cards instead of memorizing.

## Warmth Before Strength
**When to use**: First impressions, new teams, any stalled influence attempt (Ch 4).
**How**: Lower pitch and volume as if comforting a friend; validate what the room actually feels ("I know everybody's feeling a lot of uncertainty right now"); smile genuinely (eyes engaged, not just mouth); share one appropriately personal story. Then add strength: full-height posture, deliberate movement, stillness when finished.
**Trade-offs**: Amped-up enthusiasm reads as fawning; raised eyebrows while smiling signal anxiety and cost more strength than they gain warmth.

## Power Pose Warm-Up
**When to use**: Two minutes before a high-stakes pitch or speech (Ch 1, Ch 4).
**How**: Adopt an open, expansive, space-occupying posture — standing tall, hands on hips, feet apart. Add deep breathing before going onstage.
**Trade-offs**: Builds personal agency and self-regulation, not dominance over others; it's an inside-out preparation, not a performance move.

## Credibility Audit and Repair
**When to use**: Before beginning any persuasion campaign (Ch 5).
**How**: Score yourself separately on expertise (track record others know and respect) and relationships (seen as helpful, trustworthy, in sync). Test your self-assessment with trusted colleagues. Repair expertise by learning, hiring an outside expert, enlisting internal experts, citing independent research, or running a pilot. Repair relationships with one-on-one meetings that gather perspectives rather than pitch, and by involving allies who already have standing.
**Trade-offs**: Borrowed credibility works but takes time and can dilute your ownership of the idea.

## Vivid Analogy Substitution
**When to use**: When your case rests on abstract projections the audience can't feel (Ch 5).
**How**: Find a comparable situation the audience already understands and map your idea onto it (software as supermarket vs. restaurant; weekly sales meetings as early Christian gatherings). Lead or close a point with one relevant statistic; never recite strings of numbers.
**Trade-offs**: A strained analogy is worse than none; test it on someone outside the project.

## Emotional Matchmaking
**When to use**: Before any high-stakes internal presentation (Ch 5).
**How**: Canvass key individuals who have a pulse on the room's mood; ask how your proposal will land emotionally. Match your fervor to their receptivity. If they need recognition first, give it in a separate session before delivering bad news. End with hope rather than doom.
**Trade-offs**: Splitting recognition and warning across two meetings costs time and momentum, but combining them can produce open resentment.

## The Seven-Tactic Issue-Selling Campaign
**When to use**: Selling any idea upward over weeks or months (Ch 7).
**How**: Tailor the pitch to each decision maker's goals; frame in business benefits and opportunity; regulate your emotions and protect the boss's standing; time it to a trend or decision window; build a coalition including people the target trusts; follow house norms; always bring a solution or a process for finding one. Start informal to gauge interest, then go formal.
**Trade-offs**: All seven combined explain ~40% of the difference between success and failure, but campaigns cost social capital — choose battles by asking how important the issue is to the company and to you.

## Motivating Language Mix
**When to use**: Any pre-performance moment, one-on-one or to a crowd (Ch 6).
**How**: Include direction giving, empathy, and meaning making, then weight by need. Unknown challenge → more direction. Underdog or high stakes → more empathy and meaning. Obvious purpose → less meaning making. Sequence that works (McChrystal): direction → meaning → empathy → recap. Continue all three one-on-one after the speech.
**Trade-offs**: Veterans mid-operation want the plan, not reassurance; over-emphasized empathy reads as doubt in their capability.

## Organizational Apology Execution
**When to use**: After the four diagnostic questions indicate an apology is warranted (Ch 8).
**How**: Match apologizer seniority to violation severity; address the actual victims; include candor, remorse, and a concrete commitment to change; choose a setting proportional to the harm; move fast, with a placeholder if facts are pending; use plain, self-implicating language, preferably spoken.
**Trade-offs**: On-site, in-person apologies maximize sincerity signals but are uncontrolled — rejection becomes a public confrontation.

## Coherent Transition Story
**When to use**: Career change, interviews, networking, internal moves (Ch 9).
**How**: Ground the reasons in character ("I discovered I'm good at that"), cite multiple complementary reasons, extend the goal back in time and explain what blocked it, reframe past experience to surface the connecting pieces, and choose a familiar plot (maturation or education). Acknowledge the size of the jump explicitly.
**Trade-offs**: External causes ("I got fired") must be incorporated but never carry the justification; downplaying discontinuity to seem safe makes you dull and untrustworthy at once.

## Multiple Stories for Multiple Selves
**When to use**: Early transition, when several paths are live (Ch 9).
**How**: Develop a distinct coherent story per possible self and test each with the audience that relates to it. Let responses tell you which to pursue hardest.
**Trade-offs**: Not deception, but requires discipline — airing all options with one audience destroys coherence.

## The Story-Shaped Résumé
**When to use**: Whenever your target role differs from your last one (Ch 9).
**How**: Three parts, all pointing at one goal — (1) the position you want, (2) bulleted experience highlights proving you can do it, drawn from all experience including volunteer work, (3) a reverse-chronological professional summary phrased in the same terms, so every highlight claim is supported below. Build a separate version per target.
**Trade-offs**: A CV is exhaustive and neutral; a résumé is interpretive and must be rebuilt for each direction.

## The Dataviz Two Questions
**When to use**: Before opening any charting tool (Ch 10).
**How**: Ask whether the information is conceptual or data-driven, and whether you're declaring or exploring. The quadrant tells you which skills to bring: editing and design (idea illustration), facilitation (idea generation), BI and programming (visual discovery), design and storytelling (everyday dataviz).
**Trade-offs**: Slower than clicking a chart type, but convenience produces charts that visualize a spreadsheet rather than an idea.

## Subtractive Chart Design
**When to use**: Any chart destined for a live presentation (Ch 10).
**How**: One message, few variables. Cut historical context the decision doesn't need. If it takes more than a few seconds to digest, move it to paper or a personal screen instead of a slide.
**Trade-offs**: Requires courage — busy charts advertise your effort, clean ones advertise only the idea.

## What Is / What Could Be Oscillation
**When to use**: Structuring any presentation intended to change behavior (Ch 11).
**How**: Open by describing the audience's reality until they nod, then introduce the alternative. Alternate the two poles through the middle, pairing every obstacle with its answer. Close with a call to action, then new bliss — concrete future rewards.
**Trade-offs**: Requires naming problems out loud; hiding them collapses the tension the pattern runs on.
