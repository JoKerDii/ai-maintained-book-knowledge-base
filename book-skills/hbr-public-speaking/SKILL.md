## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The Five Keys to a Great Presentation** (Anderson, Ch 1) — Frame your story → Plan your delivery → Work on stage presence → Plan the multimedia → Put it together. Substance over style: you can *coach out* delivery problems but never *coach in* a missing idea. If the central theme isn't there, decline the invitation.

**The Talk as a Journey** (Ch 1) — The two biggest decisions are where to start and where to end. Start from what the audience actually knows and cares about; go deeper, not broader. Many of the best talks follow a **detective-story structure**: problem → search → "aha."

**The Valley of Awkwardness** (Ch 1) — The half-memorized stage where words sound recited and eyes drift. Rehearse past it or don't memorize; use bullet points on note cards and focus on transitions.

**The Four Aims / Intents** (Morgan, Ch 2) — Rehearse a talk four times, one intent per pass: **be open**, **connect**, **be passionate**, **listen**. Never rehearse specific gestures. Gesture fires before conscious thought, so coached gestures arrive late and audiences unconsciously read them as inauthentic. This is the **second conversation** — the nonverbal channel, which starts first.

**Story = How and Why Life Changes** (McKee, Ch 3) — Balance → **inciting incident** → subjective expectation collides with uncooperative objective reality → accumulating **antagonists** → truth. Rhetoric persuades intellectually; story unites an idea with an emotion. Put the problems in the foreground; rosy pictures foment distrust because the audience knows it's never that easy.

**The Four Storyteller's Questions** (Ch 3) — What does my protagonist want (a core need, not a shopping list)? What blocks it? How would they act under those forces? And: *Do I believe this — neither exaggerated nor soft-soaped?*

**The Warmth–Competence Matrix** (Cuddy, Kohut, Neffinger, Ch 4) — Two dimensions account for >90% of impressions. Competent-but-cold → **envy**; warm-but-incompetent → **pity**; both → trust and influence. **Connect, then lead**: warmth is the conduit of influence, and leading with strength elicits fear, which produces outward compliance without private conformity. Prepare **inside-out** (set your state) rather than outside-in (execute cues). The **Happy Warrior** combines both: faces troubles without being troubled.

**The Four Essential Steps of Persuasion** (Conger, Ch 5) — Establish credibility (from **expertise** + **relationships**; buy or borrow whichever is short) → frame for common ground (their tangible benefits; if none exist, change the plan) → provide **vivid evidence** (stories, metaphors, analogies — listeners absorb information in proportion to vividness) → connect emotionally (match your fervor to the room's actual state). Persuasion is a learning and negotiating process, not a hard sell; enter with judicious compromises prepared.

**Motivating Language Theory** (Mayfield, via McGinn, Ch 6) — Every effective pep talk mixes **direction giving** (uncertainty-reducing: exactly how, and how you'll be judged), **empathetic language** (praise, encouragement, acknowledgment of difficulty), and **meaning making** (why this matters — the hardest to deliver). Weight the mix by what the audience lacks.

**The Seven Issue-Selling Tactics** (Ashford & Detert, Ch 7) — Tailor your pitch (highest-yield) → frame the issue → manage emotions on both sides → get the timing right → involve others → adhere to norms → suggest solutions. Combined, they explain ~40% of the difference between success and failure. Business and opportunity framing beat threat framing; **moral framing is the weakest** because it reads as a judgment of character.

**The Apology Formula** (Schweitzer, Brooks, Galinsky, Ch 8) — Diagnose with four questions (Was there a violation, real *or perceived*? Core or noncore? How will the public react? Are we committed to change?), then execute **who / what / where / when / how**. The "what" always needs candor + remorse + a commitment to change. Speed signals sincerity; issue a **placeholder apology** rather than staying silent. Drop the legal lens — apologies address feelings, and evidence suggests they reduce litigation.

**Coherence = Continuity + Causality** (Ibarra & Lineback, Ch 9) — A transition story earns trust by linking past, present, and future. Ground the change in character, cite multiple reasons, extend the goal back in time, reframe your past, and use a familiar plot (**maturation** or **education**). Acknowledge the size of the jump — glossing over discontinuity destroys trust rather than protecting it. Develop **multiple stories for multiple possible selves**, each tested with its own audience.

**The Dataviz Two Questions** (Berinato, Ch 10) — Conceptual or data-driven? Declaring or exploring? The 2×2 gives four types: **idea illustration**, **idea generation**, **visual discovery**, **everyday dataviz** — each demanding different skills. Don't start with chart rules; that's packing without knowing the destination.

**The Persuasive Story Pattern** (Duarte, Ch 11) — Beginning: **what is** → **what could be**. Middle: oscillate between the two, pairing every obstacle with its answer. End: call to action → **new bliss** (concrete future rewards). The gap between what is and what could be jars the audience out of complacency.
