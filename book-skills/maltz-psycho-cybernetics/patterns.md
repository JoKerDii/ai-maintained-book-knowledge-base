# Patterns — Maltz's Runnable Techniques

The techniques are the point of this book. Each is stated so it can be run today.

---

## The Method (mental rehearsal in the theater of the mind)
**When to use**: To change any behavior, capability or reaction that has resisted effort. The core practice of the whole system.
**How**:
1. Ten minutes daily, alone, no distractions, eyes closed. Make the body still — "the greater energies of the mind are available only when the body is still and the door to the senses is closed."
2. Pick **one** situation you handle badly.
3. Build it in full sensory detail — the room, light, sounds, what you're wearing, who is present. Environmental detail is what makes the nervous system accept it.
4. **Be inside it as a participant, not an onlooker.** Think *from* the goal, not about it. A goal you think of is a possibility; a goal you think from is "overpoweringly real."
5. See yourself handling it as you intend. Past performance is explicitly irrelevant during these ten minutes.
6. Shift to the **feeling** of already being that person. "Begin by knowing you have arrived."
7. Mark off the day. **21 consecutive days**, then judge.
**Trade-offs**: Requires a real daily slot and a strong desire for the goal — the mechanism needs a defined objective, and a lukewarm one won't drive it. Watching yourself from outside produces a pleasant picture and no change. It also works in reverse: worry is this exact technique pointed at the failure.

## The daily relaxation practice (Quiet Place + daily vacation)
**When to use**: Every day as maintenance; immediately on agitation.
**How**:
1. *One-time build.* Sit undisturbed, close your eyes, and construct a private retreat in imagination — plan, paint, furnish and decorate it to your own taste until you genuinely feel at home. Beach house on an uncharted island, a cloud, deep in the earth, a distant planet; the constraint is comfort, not realism.
2. Set two rules: completely isolated from the outside world, and nobody knows where it is.
3. *Daily entry.* Close your eyes and go. It takes an instant — the same instant it takes to picture the Golden Gate Bridge.
4. Rest the nervous system there. Also **work** there: accept your insecurities, re-picture past successes, set today's goals.
5. Return and use the carry-over — the tranquility transfers into whatever action follows.
**Trade-offs**: Useless if built for the first time mid-crisis; build it in calm. Sharing its location converts it from a refuge into a topic. Maltz pre-empts the "escapism" objection and tells you to ignore it.

## The "act as if" drill
**When to use**: When you need a capability you don't believe you have — an interview, a difficult conversation, a performance.
**How**:
1. Take the method actor's stance: a man playing a king "tries to think *as if* he were that king," and projects it into living reality.
2. Decide who you are being for this specific occasion — not a fantasy self, the self you'd be on your best day.
3. Rehearse it in imagination first (The Method above).
4. Then act it in reality. "Act the way you want to be (in your imagination), and you'll be the way you act." Emerson's version: "Do the thing, and you will have the power."
**Trade-offs**: This is not the mask. The mask conceals a self-image you consider indefensible; "as if" rehearses one you intend to inhabit. If it's producing strain rather than release, you're wearing the mask.

## The forgiveness exercise (therapeutic forgiveness)
**When to use**: On any grudge held more than a few days — against another person or yourself.
**How**:
1. Name the specific item. Not "everything," not "the relationship" — one act.
2. Run the honesty check: *Is this an inconsiderate act that I, in my own imperfection, might have committed?*
3. Run the motive check: **what am I getting from the condemnation?** Maltz's answer — "As long as we condemn another, we can feel superior to him," and there is "a perverse sense of satisfaction in feeling sorry for yourself." Until you see the payoff you're taking, you won't release it.
4. Cancel the debt **with no reservations**. The surgical standard: "no partial, tentative, or halfway measures. The scar tissue is cut out completely."
5. The endpoint: recognizing "there is and was *nothing for us to forgive*" — the debt itself was not valid.
6. Then the self-directed pass. Mirror, write **FORGIVENESS**, say aloud: "I will try to forgive myself. I will try to forgive others. We are all human and imperfect."
**Trade-offs**: Maltz permits a few days of bitterness after real injury — "better to cleanse your system than to let inner hate eat away at you." What is forbidden is permanent residence. Verbal forgiveness ("I forgive him," then a catnap) does nothing.

## The Goal Builder protocol (generalized)
**When to use**: To convert any idea from the book into behavior.
**How**: One goal per week — **six days of application, one day of rest**. Each day: (1) a *seeing* component (an imagined scene, a walk, a sound); (2) a *mirror* component — write the goal word on the glass in erasable pencil or lipstick; (3) a *spoken* component — a first-person sentence said to your reflection; (4) *erase* the word, keep the feeling.
**Trade-offs**: Deliberately serialized — running all fourteen at once is the failure mode. The physicality (writing, speaking aloud) is not decoration; an internal resolution is not the same act.

## The anxiety audit
**When to use**: Chronic worry, or resentment you can't locate a cause for.
**How**: Write down today's worries. Then last week's. Then last month's. Then a year ago's, if you can recall them. Study the list and check each one: was it realistic? Did it make sense? Observe your own hit rate.
**Trade-offs**: The evidence is the argument — don't substitute reassurance for the written list. Maltz's prediction: most were unrealistic. Use the discovered overreaction rate on today's fears.

## The negative-feedback reflex
**When to use**: The instant you make an error or get rejected.
**How**: (1) **Be aware** of the negative — it's a warning light, and nobody gets upset at a warning light; (2) **evaluate** the outcome as unwanted and unacceptable — the *outcome*, not yourself; (3) **correct at once.** Then return attention to the objective: glance at the negatives, concentrate on the positives. Repeat until automatic.
**Trade-offs**: The "at once" is what installs the reflex; a period of review installs rumination instead. Excess attention to the indicator *is* the malfunction.

## Delay Your Response (the 12-count)
**When to use**: At the moment you recognize anger can no longer be checked — not at first irritation.
**How**: Identify your over-reaction domains in advance (money, status, criticism). When the flood arrives, count slowly to twelve. Then act.
**Trade-offs**: You will still be irritated at twelve. The success criterion is control of action, not absence of feeling.

## The daily recitation
**When to use**: As the standing 21-day practice; pairs with The Method.
**How**: Read Maltz's daily statement each morning ("This is the beginning of a new day… My thinking and my attitudes are calm and cheerful… I respond in a calm and intelligent manner, without alarm, no matter what the situation"). **Mark off 21 consecutive days.**
**Trade-offs**: Will not work if incompatible with your current self-image — "when it is incompatible with the self-image, it absolutely will not work." If it feels like lying, do The Method first.

## Mirror Magic (six-day micro-drills)
**When to use**: To attach practice to events that already happen daily.
**How**: pick one for six days. (a) *Morning:* breathing exercises, then the mirror question — "Am I a friend to myself?" (b) *Garbage:* as you throw out the household's accumulation, discard the mental accumulation too. (c) *Front door:* closing it symbolically closes the door on yesterday's mistake. (d) *Red light:* the red is your fear of failure holding you back; when it turns green, move forward as toward a useful goal, with the confidence of past successes.
**Trade-offs**: Deliberately small. They work by attachment to an existing trigger — don't try to schedule them as new appointments.

---

## The crisis protocol
**When to use**: Stage fright, high-stakes performance, pressure.
**How**: Set your goal → believe in the goal → **bring back your triumphs** (recall past successes in sensory detail) → **permit the nervousness** ("I will accept my fear. And my nervousness. And my panic.") → stop expecting yourself to fail → do your best.
**Trade-offs**: Intervene on the *expectation*, never on the symptom. Recalled successes serve double duty: they displace nervousness now, and if you do fail they make the failure easier to accept.
