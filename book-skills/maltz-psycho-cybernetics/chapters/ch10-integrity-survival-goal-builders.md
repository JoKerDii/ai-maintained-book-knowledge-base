# Chapter 10: Integrity / Survival With Self-Respect / Goal Builders (Book Two: The Search for Self-Respect, ch. 13–15)

## Core Idea
Book Two closes with its operational payload: **fourteen Goal Builders**, one per week, each converting a chapter's idea into a physical exercise. Maltz calls this "the key to this book" — because words left "passively on the paper" do nothing.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Goal Builder program**
  - Structure: **fourteen weeks. One goal builder per week. Six days of application, then one day of rest.**
  - Sequence: 1 Self-Respect · 2 Understanding · 3 Sincerity · 4 Defeating Uncertainty · 5 Overcoming Resentment · 6 Getting Rid of Emptiness · 7 Opportunity · 8 Forgiveness · 9 Creative Mirror Watching · 10 Tearing Down Prison Walls · 11 Tranquility · 12 No Retirement Clock · 13 Integrity · 14 Survival.
  - Common shape of each: a *seeing* component (an imagined scene, a walk, a sound) → a *mirror* component (write the word in erasable pencil or lipstick) → a *spoken* component (a first-person statement) → an *erasure* (keep the feeling, drop the prop).
  - The stated progression: **constructive thinking → constructive imaging → constructive action.**
- **Integrity as wholeness**: "The person with integrity is whole, honest, honorable. He feels self-respect." Maltz's pun is load-bearing — integrity is structural integrity, the absence of internal division between what you are and what you show.
- **Survival with self-respect**: surviving a crisis is not the achievement; surviving it without downgrading your opinion of yourself is.
  - How: (1) accept the fear, nervousness and panic rather than fighting them; (2) accept yourself regardless of what the audience thinks; (3) stop expecting yourself to fail; (4) bring images of past successes into mind — which both replaces nervousness *and* cushions an actual failure; (5) then do your best.
- **The invisible toolkit**: "understanding, forgiveness, success imaging, confidence building, goal orientation, and emotional readiness for constructive action."

## Key Concepts
- **The "grit" in integrity**: the abrasive, costly part — integrity is not comfort.
- **The tall little person / the "little self"**: the shrunken self-image that walks around inside a competent adult.
- **The anatomy of self-respect**: the components dissected across Book Two, reassembled here.
- **Deliberate exaggeration**: many Goal Builders are "purposely exaggerated and unreal so that when you come back to earth, you can cope with your more common problems still more effectively."
- **Your lifelong goal**: self-respect is never finished. "If you own a plant and love it, you keep watering it as long as it lives."

## Mental Models
- **Six days on, one day off.** Maltz never asks for continuous effort. The rest day is part of the design — this is training, not penance.
- **Write it, say it, erase it.** The physicality is the point. A statement spoken to your own face in a mirror is doing something an internal resolution isn't.
- **Overshoot the simulation.** By rehearsing a crisis far worse than yours, you return to your real problem with it correctly scaled. Maltz applies this to war, torture, and public humiliation scenarios.
- **Failure insurance via past successes.** Recalling past wins serves *two* purposes: it displaces nervousness now, and if you do fail, "these past successes will make acceptance of this failure easier for you." Rare in the genre — an explicit downside plan.

## Anti-patterns
- **Passive reading.** "My words will not help you enough if they remain passively on the paper."
- **Running all fourteen at once** — the program is deliberately serialized.
- **Expecting yourself to fail** as a form of humility; Maltz treats it as the core survival error.
- **Fighting the nervousness** instead of accepting it. The instruction is literally "I will accept my fear. And my nervousness. And my panic."
- **Treating self-respect as a milestone** rather than a plant you keep watering.

## Worked Example
**Goal Builder 14 — Survival**, in full, because it shows the exaggeration technique working:

Run this movie in your mind. You are scheduled to deliver a speech to an enormous crowd. Workmen have been *enlarging the stadium* so that 125,000 people can come hear you, if it doesn't rain. You are so nervous that when you look in the mirror you can't recognize yourself — you even forget your name. You pray for rain; the weather is ideal. Some stranger tells you it's time to get dressed. Oh — it's your spouse. Hurriedly you brush your teeth with soap and wash your face with toothpaste. You check yourself: thank God, a shoe on each foot, and you're wearing clothes. Then your hysteria mounts to super terror as you think of **250,000 eyes** watching you speak.

Maltz breaks frame: "Okay, you've got the picture. Obviously, it's a caricature." And then, disarmingly: "I am a veteran lecturer, and this picture scares me, too."

Now the actual protocol. Go to the mirror. Write **SURVIVAL**. Look at yourself and say:

> "I will accept my fear. And my nervousness. And my panic. My key to survival with self-respect is this: I will accept myself no matter what all these people think of me. I will stop expecting myself to fail no matter how imperfect I am. No matter how imperfect I am, I will accept myself."

Then bring images of past successes into mind — for the dual purpose above — and do your best. "Do your best in reality, too."

**The closing statement of the whole program**, said to the mirror: *"As long as I live, I will strive to make myself a complete person, in my own image, with a sense of mature responsibility, rising above my human limitations to realize in good faith and with constructive intentions toward other people my full potential for self-respect."*

## Key Takeaways
1. Run one Goal Builder a week, six days on and one off — serialization is the design.
2. Every exercise ends in a spoken first-person sentence to your own reflection; don't skip the speaking.
3. Simulate a crisis worse than yours to right-size the real one.
4. Accept the fear explicitly rather than suppressing it; then recall past successes.
5. Self-respect is a plant, not a summit.
