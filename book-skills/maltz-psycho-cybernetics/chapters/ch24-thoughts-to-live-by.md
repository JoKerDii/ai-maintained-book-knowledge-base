# Chapter 24: Thoughts To Live By (Book Five: Thoughts To Live By)

## Core Idea
Book Five is Maltz's late-life distillation: short essays that restate the whole system as two acronyms — the **Success Mechanism (S-U-C-C-E-S-S)** and the **Failure Mechanism (F-A-I-L-U-R-E)** — plus his sharpest definition of forgiveness and his most useful diagnosis of frustration.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Failure Mechanism — F-A-I-L-U-R-E** (verbatim; "We have a Failure Mechanism within us made up of past defeats, and the elements of this Failure Mechanism are"):
  **F**ear · **A**nger · **I**nferiority · **L**oneliness · **U**ncertainty · **R**esentment · **E**mptiness.
  - Consequence: "If we are overcome by these elements of the Failure Mechanism, we walk away from life because we neglect our assets, our built-in Success Mechanism. Failure leads to tension, corrosion, a lack of belief."
- **The Success Mechanism — S-U-C-C-E-S-S** ("Don't measure your success by counting prestige symbols and imitating other people, but by living the aspects of the Success Mechanism"):
  - **S: Sense of direction.** A goal within your capabilities.
  - **U: Understanding.** Understand your needs and the needs of others. "You may be an island within yourself, but you belong on the mainland with others."
  - **C: Courage.** To take your chances, to get your feet wet. "If you make a mistake, try again. Try. Try. Try!"
  - **C: Compassion.** For yourself as well as others — the cure for loneliness. (Schopenhauer: "Compassion is the basis of all morality.")
  - **E: Esteem.** "If you have no respect for yourself, no one will give it to you." When you contribute, you enlarge your sense of worth.
  - **S: Self-Acceptance.** Never try to be someone else. Adapting Shaw: **"Better keep your Self-Image clean and bright. It is the window through which you see the world."**
  - **S: Self-Confidence.** Remember the confidence of past successes in your present undertaking. "Concentrate on success like professional players in sports. They forget the times they lost… They are out to win Now" — while remembering "you cannot be a champion 100 percent of the time."
- **Therapeutic forgiveness = cancelling a debt.** The most precise statement in the omnibus:
  - "Therapeutic forgiveness is not difficult. **The only difficulty is to secure your own willingness to cast aside a feeling of condemnation — your willingness to cancel out the debt with no reservations.**"
  - Why we resist: "We find it difficult to forgive only because we take comfort in a sense of condemnation. We get a perverse and morbid enjoyment out of nursing our wounds. **As long as we condemn another, we can feel superior to him.**"
  - The endpoint: "We cancel the debt because we come to recognize that **the debt itself is not valid.** True forgiveness comes only when we are able to see, and instantly accept, that there is and was *nothing for us to forgive.*"
  - The surgical model: "In facial surgery, there can be no partial, tentative, or halfway measures. The scar tissue is cut out completely and entirely. The wound is allowed to heal cleanly."
  - Six points to remember: use foresight and clear thinking (live in the present, look toward your goal) · seize opportunities to improve yourself now · develop insight to check negative feelings · concentrate on compassion for yourself and others · forget yesterday's trials, remember the good things · try to relax.
- **The Five Roadblocks of Frustration** — with cures:
  1. **We worry not only before making a decision but after** — "this extra fifty pounds of worry on our minds all day." *Cure:* express anxiety *before* deciding. Anxiety is creative while you choose among the five roads; once you choose, stop worrying and call on the confidence of past successes. "If we call upon the failures of the past to guide us in the present, we create immediate frustration."
  2. **We worry about yesterday and tomorrow as well as today.** *Cure:* think only of today. "Every day is a complete lifetime. Forget yesterday; lose it in the vacuum of time. Tomorrow doesn't exist; when it comes it is another day. Let your servomechanism do what it can do well; respond to the present."
  3. **We try to do too many things at one time** — "tension of tone, spasm instead of comfort." *Cure:* "Don't fight relaxation. Join it. Learn to do one thing at a time."
  4. **We wrestle with our problems twenty-four hours a day without letup.** *Cure:* **"Sleep on your problem if you are unable to solve it. Sleep on it, not with it."**
  5. **We refuse to relax.** *Cure:* "You sit in a room of your mind, and you relax there to cut the electric circuit of distress."

## Key Concepts
- **The Yesterday Tomorrow Complex**: "Sixty percent of what we see deals with yesterday. Twenty percent deals with tomorrow. But where is today?" There are 1,440 minutes in a day and we live very few of them in the present.
- **Jamming the creative mechanism**: "too many of us jam our creative mechanism with worry, anxiety, and fear, trying to force a solution with the forebrain, the seat of our thinking but *not* the seat of doing."
- **Simplicity**: one goal at a time; stop criticizing yourself and others; do what you can rather than imitating; see life as it is, not as it should be. (Thoreau: "Our life is frittered away by detail. Simplify, simplify.")
- **The Daily Requirements of Man** — Maltz's other "daily dozen": Love · Security · Self-expression · Recognition · New creative experiences · Self-respect · Getting more living out of life · Sharing happiness with others · Involvement · The art of relaxation · Reaching goals · Rising above a mistake.
- **The greatest failure**: "to be afraid to make a mistake, to be afraid to take the calculated risk of living."

## Mental Models
- **The hurdler who became a salesman.** A college hurdle champion, later a mediocre insurance salesman — likable, easy with people, but selling little. Fear of not being a perfect salesman distorted his self-image; a single rejection frustrated him. At a class reunion, slightly drunk, he borrowed sneakers to jump the hurdles again — and broke his leg. A month in a cast gave him time to think. He recalled that he had become a champion *through constant practice, by overcoming failures*; that he had had self-confidence, understanding of his skill, courage, self-respect, self-acceptance, and a sense of direction. **"Why couldn't he jump the hurdles of his job?"** He returned to selling with the same tenacity, practicing in his mind how to approach a customer and overcome obstacles. Within a year, top-flight salesman. The moral Maltz draws: "Failure teaches success."
- **Live today, this very minute.** Casals at ninety-three: "Every day I am reborn, every day is a new lifetime for me."
- **Maltz's own failure.** As a premed at Columbia he failed organic chemistry and feared he'd never be a doctor. Desire was greater; he took the course over the summer, studied hard, and passed with an A. **"I rose above failure because I had a goal, a goal important to me."**

## Anti-patterns
- **Nursing wounds for the superiority they confer** — the real reason forgiveness is hard, named exactly.
- **Partial forgiveness.** Halfway measures fail in facial surgery and in this.
- **Forcing a solution with the forebrain** rather than letting the servo-mechanism work.
- **Worrying after the decision** — pure cost, no information.
- **Being afraid to make a mistake**: "the greatest failure of all."

## Key Takeaways
1. Learn both acronyms. F-A-I-L-U-R-E names what you're running; S-U-C-C-E-S-S names what to run instead.
2. Forgiveness is cancelling a debt you come to see was never valid — and no reservations are permitted.
3. Spend anxiety before the decision; afterwards, call only on past successes.
4. Sleep *on* the problem, not with it; one thing at a time; today only.
5. The fear of making a mistake is itself the greatest failure — the calculated risk is the price of living.
