# Chapter 5: Your Great Need — Self-Respect / Understanding / Sincerity (Book Two: The Search for Self-Respect, ch. 1–3)

## Core Idea
Self-respect is a *need*, not a luxury, and it cannot be imported from other people's opinions. It is assembled from three components you can practice: understanding (that you have rights), sincerity (that you can face reality without evasion), and the daily decision to reach toward the best in yourself.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Self-respect as a lifetime job, not a state.** "When you feel self-respect, you still seek more, and when you find more, you keep your momentum alive and give your self-respect to others."
  - When to use: whenever you catch yourself waiting to be *given* standing by a boss, partner, or audience.
  - How: source it internally, then spend it outward. Sharing is what keeps it replenishing.
- **Understanding as the key to human dignity** — and Maltz means a specific understanding: *that you have the right to live happily, pleasurably, and with self-respect.*
  - How: observe how differently people carry this conviction. Some slouch, avert their eyes, seem "empty of any feeling but fear." Others walk erect without swaggering, meet eyes with friendliness. The first group habitually short-changes itself.
  - Then apply the same question to yourself, out loud, in a mirror: *Do I have rights? Yes. Yes, I do.*
- **The Active Power of Sincerity**: sincerity is not niceness; it is the capacity to face reality without evasion, and it therefore **requires confidence** as a precondition. The sincere person "gives substance to human relations" — enriching himself and others simultaneously.
- **Understanding others**: an obligation that follows automatically once you can communicate with yourself. It is "the beginning of friendship — the growth of your self-image."

## Key Concepts
- **Reaching out toward the best in yourself**: the daily orientation, as opposed to defending the worst.
- **The right to be happy**: something you grant yourself, subject only to the restrictions of civilized law.
- **Misunderstanding as a way of life**: the default setting when people are running on negative feeling; each party reads the other's fear as hostility.
- **Finding the self you like**: the target of the search — not a perfect self, a *likeable* one.
- **Funhouse mirrors**: physical distortion at an amusement park is harmless and amusing; emotional self-distortion is crippling, and it is the same operation.

## Mental Models
- **You are neither superior nor inferior.** Maltz returns to this constantly. Both poles are distortions of the same mirror; self-respect lives at true scale.
- Think of self-respect like **savings**: built week after week, month after month. "Self-respect is even more basic than money. If you are frugal, you can survive with just a little money and live a good life — but life without self-respect is a wasteland."
- **You cannot respect others unless you respect yourself; you cannot admire others unless you admire yourself; you cannot help others unless you help yourself.** Treat outward generosity that isn't grounded this way as unstable.
- **Envy is the tell.** If you downgrade yourself, you can still admire other people — "but your respect will be tainted with envy," and others sense it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Sourcing self-respect from others' opinions.** "If you don't have self-respect, no one will give it to you." Waiting for the promotion, the compliment, or the applause to confer standing is a permanent wait.
- **Habitual self-short-changing**: the walk-down-the-street diagnosis — carrying yourself as someone with no rights, and thereby confirming it.
- **Sincerity without confidence**, which collapses into either evasion or brutality. Maltz says sincerity requires "enough belief in yourself so that you can face up to reality without evasion."
- **Treating self-respect as an achievement to be completed** rather than a plant you keep watering as long as it lives.

## Worked Example
**Goal Builder 2 — Understanding** (Maltz's own exercise, run over six days):

1. Take a walk down a street you know well — New York, St. Louis, wherever. You have walked it before for physical exercise; today it is mental exercise.
2. Look at the people you pass and hold one question in mind: *Does he (or she) believe he has the right to be happy?*
3. Accept that in many cases you cannot tell. Some are in "up" moods, some "down," many successfully hide their feelings, and plenty register neutral or mixed. Do not force a verdict — the ambiguity is part of the exercise.
4. Notice the clear cases at both ends: the ones who slouch, stumble, and avert their eyes in an excess of timidity; and the ones who walk proud and erect, without swaggering or overcompensating, looking others in the eye with a smile of confidence.
5. Walk home. Go to your mirror. With an erasable pencil or lipstick, write on the glass in bold capitals: **UNDERSTANDING**.
6. Say aloud: "I have seen people who feel they have rights and other people who feel they have no rights. But how about me? Do I have rights? Yes. Yes, I do."
7. Erase the word — but keep the feeling. The erasure matters: the mirror is a working surface, not a shrine.

Maltz's framing of the whole fourteen-part Goal Builder program: **one full week per goal builder — six days of application, then one day of rest.** Fourteen chapters, fourteen goal builders, fourteen weeks. "You move from constructive thinking to constructive imaging, and on to constructive action."

## Key Takeaways
1. Self-respect is generated internally and *spent* outward; that circulation is what maintains it.
2. Practice the specific understanding that you have the right to a good day — then verify it aloud in the mirror.
3. Sincerity is downstream of confidence; build the confidence and the sincerity becomes available.
4. Run one Goal Builder per week, six days on and one off — not all fourteen at once.
5. Envy in your admiration of others is a reliable signal that self-respect is low.
