# Chapter 21: How To Be An Individual in An Age of Conformity / How To Function Successfully Under Pressure / Winning Friends Can Be Easy for You (Book Four: The Magic Power of Self-Image Psychology, ch. 12–14)

## Core Idea
Under pressure, the people who stay calm are not the ones with fewer problems — **"their self-image holds up."** And friendship starts from the same source: you cannot be a friend to others until you are a friend to yourself.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Secret of Winning under pressure**: "This ability to remain calm when others feel harassed and confused is their big secret. They can feel secure regardless of pressures; **their self-image holds up.**"
  - Companion requirement: adaptability. "You must also be able to adapt yourself to changing conditions. Sometimes, to succeed, you must change your plans."
- **The crisis protocol** (from "Living With Crises"):
  1. **Set your goals.** A crisis without a goal is only weather.
  2. **Believe in your goals.**
  3. **Bring back your triumphs** — deliberately recall past successes into the theater of the mind.
  4. **Don't be afraid to be nervous.** Nervousness is not disqualifying; expecting yourself not to be nervous is.
  5. Recognize **your power in a crisis** — the capacity is already installed.
- **Five Rules for Winning Friends** ("Apply these concepts, and you will never lack friends"):
  1. **Be a friend to yourself.** If you're not, you can't be a friend to others. "If you downgrade yourself, you can still admire other people, but your respect will be tainted with envy. Others will sense the impurity of your friendship." Pity, he adds, "is not a strong foundation for friendship."
  2. **Reach out to people.** Express yourself as uninhibitedly as the situation permits. Don't tell yourself you're silly for cracking a joke or unstable for being nervous and wanting to be liked. Look for the other person's positive qualities and try to bring them out; **watch for overcritical thoughts and stamp them out.**
  3. **Imagine you're the other person.** Reconstruct their total life situation as accurately as you can — then you can sense their needs, understand their responses, and avoid stepping on the toes you now know are sore.
  4. **Accept the other fellow's individuality.** "People are different, especially when they're genuine. Don't try to alter this fact." Forcing someone to conform to your preconceived ideas produces an enemy, not a friend.
  5. **Try to meet others' needs.** "Many people talk *at* people; they deliver lectures, and the other fellow is just an ear. Never do this to a friend; talk *to* him! And make sure to listen."
- **Individuality without contrarianism**: "We must conform to certain rules" — Maltz concedes the civilised minimum immediately — but over-conforming distorts the self-image. Keep your identity in a crowd; have a mind of your own.

## Key Concepts
- **Pressure-toleration quotient**: Maltz's informal measure — how much load your self-image carries before it starts revising itself downward.
- **Life is a series of crises**: stated as the normal condition, not the exception.
- **Life is people.**
- **Why be shy?** / **coming out of your shell**: shyness treated as an inhibition of the self-image, not a fixed trait.
- **You can put others at ease** — and doing so is the most reliable route to being sought out.
- **Friends can make you a bigger person.**

## Mental Models
- **Bing Crosby drops the script.** Maltz quotes Donald Curtis: a live radio broadcast, ten seconds to air, Crosby drops his script. Panic breaks loose in everyone but Bing. While actors, agency men, stagehands, and musicians scramble after the fluttering pages, "Bing nonchalantly bent down, picked up the elusive first page, cocked his hat on the back of his head, and came in exactly on cue." A tremendous performance, no fuss. The scarcity in the room was not competence — it was an intact self-image.
- **Whistler and the silicon.** In 1854 Cadet Whistler at West Point was asked to discuss silicon. He rose and began, "Silicon is a gas." "That will do, Mr. Whistler." Weeks later he was discharged. Years later, famous as a painter, he liked to say: **"If silicon had been a gas, I would have been a general."** Maltz's point is adaptation — the plan changed and the man didn't stop.
- **Envy is a friendship leak.** Rule 1 is not sentiment; it is a claim that other people can detect the substitution and respond to it.
- **The self-image is what's under load.** In any pressure situation, ask not "can I handle this?" but "is my picture of myself holding?"

## Anti-patterns
- **Over-conforming**, which "distorts your self-image" — the social equivalent of imitation.
- **Trying to alter another person's individuality**: domineering tactics manufacture enemies.
- **Talking at people.** The lecture-and-ear pattern, which Maltz forbids specifically among friends.
- **Being afraid to be nervous** — the second-order fear that does the actual damage under pressure.
- **Friendship as a search for validation**: if you're downgrading yourself, the admiration comes back tainted and everyone can tell.

## Worked Example
**Overcoming stage fright — how Maltz actually did it.**

Maltz reports helping an actress conquer stage fright, and the method is the crisis protocol applied step by step. It is worth noticing what he *doesn't* do: he does not reassure her that the audience is friendly, he does not tell her to calm down, and he does not attack the nervousness at all.

1. **Set the goal.** Attention is redirected from the audience's judgment onto a defined objective — the performance itself. This is Ch 15's principle in a room: the golfer does not stare at the water hazard.
2. **Believe in the goal.** A goal you privately consider unachievable feeds the failure mechanism just as efficiently as no goal.
3. **Bring back the triumphs.** Recall, in full sensory detail, an occasion of doing this well — not an argument that you *can* do it, but the reinstalled *feeling* that you have. This is The Method (Ch 14) applied acutely.
4. **Permit the nervousness.** "Don't be afraid to be nervous!" Maltz treats the arousal as normal equipment. The catastrophic move is expecting to be free of it, because then every symptom becomes evidence of impending failure — and the self-image, which was the only thing under load, buckles.
5. **Go.**

Compare Goal Builder 14 (Ch 10), which arrives at the same instruction from Book Two: *"I will accept my fear. And my nervousness. And my panic… I will stop expecting myself to fail no matter how imperfect I am."* Two volumes, same protocol: **the intervention is on the expectation, not on the symptom.**

Maltz also grounds this in his own history — "The Story of My First Patient," which he calls one of the turning points of his life, and his standing claim that during sixty-odd years he has had his share of crises. The framework is not offered from a position of never having been frightened.

## Key Takeaways
1. Under pressure the variable is whether your self-image holds — treat that as the thing to reinforce.
2. Run the crisis protocol: set goal → believe it → recall triumphs → permit the nervousness → act.
3. Intervene on the expectation of failure, never on the symptom of fear.
4. Be a friend to yourself first; envy contaminates admiration and others detect it.
5. Reconstruct the other person's whole situation before deciding what their behavior meant.
