# Chapter 18: The Secret Strength in Relaxation / Why Not Be a Winner? / You Can Master Your Habits (Book Four: The Magic Power of Self-Image Psychology, ch. 4–6)

## Core Idea
**"Easy does it."** The characteristic modern failure is not laziness but strain — people "try too hard, push themselves too hard." Relaxation is not the reward for success; it is the precondition for it, and the two roadblocks to it are refusing your limitations and holding grudges.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Do not try too hard**
  - The diagnosis: excess tension is "a disease more common than the so-called common cold. It is a rare person who is calm." Maltz claims hypertension afflicts more people and is more destructive than the diseases we lie awake worrying about.
  - The trade-off he presses: "What price material success? Is it worth hypertension? Is it worth the sacrifice of one's health?"
  - Note the concealment case: "Others seem relaxed but are merely masters of the art of concealment."
- **The two roadblocks to relaxation** — the operative pair:
  1. **Accept your limitations.** "Understand that you are just one person and that you can do only so much." If you find yourself rushing at a frantic pace, tell yourself to stop and **ask what you are accomplishing.** "Would you rather drive yourself into a heart attack or relax with your human limitations? Ask yourself this question over and over."
  2. **Learn the healing power of forgiveness.** Stop holding grudges against others *and against yourself.* "You cannot afford this burden of resentment; you cannot have peace of mind while you hate. **Compassion and love are parents of relaxation.**"
- **See yourself as a success / take dead aim on the target.** Success must be defined by you first: "Once you have defined success for yourself, you have taken your first stumbling, awkward baby steps. No easy road lies ahead… **You must be your own navigator, and your compass is in your mind.**"
- **How habits are born, and how to change them.** Habits are learned responses that became automatic; they can be discarded and rebuilt with deliberate work. Applied by Maltz to drinking, weight, smoking, overwork, and dress ("dress as if you think you're worth it").
- **Three winning habits** (the practice exercise): (1) do the practice exercises regularly — "just like washing your hands or combing your hair"; (2) understand the importance of your mind — the ability to produce and sustain a single mental picture can matter more than monetary success; (3) realize the power of your self-image — "you rise or fall, succeed or fail, depending on your self-image."

## Key Concepts
- **You can't push yourself into sleep** — Maltz's cleanest demonstration that effort is counterproductive in exactly the domains people apply most of it.
- **You can only do so much**: a limit, stated as a fact rather than a failing.
- **Let bygones be bygones** — the relaxation chapter's forgiveness heading.
- **To err is only human / no one is perfect**: the permission that makes relaxation structurally possible.
- **You have as many rights as the next person.**
- **Rationalizing away defeat, then crawling into a shell of resentment at the world's failure to recognize you** — the habit Maltz singles out for demolition. "First, you have to recognize the good in yourself."

## Mental Models
- **Tension is an occupational spasm you carry home.** The man in the worked example brings "from his office the spasms of his occupation" into a house that has nothing wrong with it.
- **Relaxation is a *capacity*, not a break.** Maltz quotes Franklin: "He that can take rest is greater than he that can take cities" — taking cities involves superior firepower; taking rest involves a deep spiritual capacity.
- **Concealment is not relaxation.** Many apparently calm people are simply better at hiding the same strain.
- **Kennedy at the podium.** Maltz's example of visible relaxation under maximum stakes: "Only a man who sees himself favorably can be so comfortable when the eyes of millions are on him." Relaxation is a *readout* of the self-image, not a technique layered on top.

## Anti-patterns
- **Willpower strain / driving yourself.** "Don't drive yourself!" The chapter's own heading, and the book's most repeated warning.
- **Trading health for material success** — and then discovering the wealth cannot buy back the relaxation.
- **Masking tension** rather than resolving it, which costs the same and teaches you nothing.
- **Trying to force sleep** — the reductio that proves the general rule.
- **Holding a grudge while seeking peace of mind.** Structurally impossible: "you cannot have peace of mind while you hate."

## Worked Example
**The tense millionaire — Maltz's set-piece, staged in the playhouse of your mind.**

He asks you to watch it as a scene, deliberately, in detail — because the exercise is to *see* the man, not to think about him.

The man has made a fortune, saved a good deal of it, and Maltz has watched him do the following hundreds of times. He comes home from work and steps into his dining room — rich mahogany, a long table, six chairs. He doesn't notice it. He sits, is restless, paces the room, bangs the table absently, almost trips over a chair. His wife sits down; he says hello and drums the table until the chef brings the meal. **He gobbles the food "as if his hands were two shovels hurling it down a hole."**

Dinner over, he gets up quickly, walks to the living room — long sofa, plush chairs, wall-to-wall carpet, good paintings. He hurls himself into a chair, picking up a newspaper in almost the same motion. Ruffles a few pages, scans the headlines, throws it to the ground. Picks up a cigar, bites off the tip, lights it, puffs twice, puts it out. Springs up, turns on the television, changes channels, turns it off impatiently. Grabs his hat and coat from the hall closet and goes out for a walk.

"He just can't find himself." No economic problems. Two cars. Staff attending his every command. **"But he cannot relax. Not only that, he has even forgotten who he is."**

Maltz then brings him back on stage for an interview. A friend asks: *Who are you?* He answers: "You know who I am. I'm Mr. X." — a name, a title, and nothing underneath.

**The exercise:** bring your mental camera in for a closeup — the worried lines on the forehead, the fist-clenching, the stiffness of movement. Maltz is explicit that thinking about him is insufficient: "you must **see** him in your mind. For once you do, you will do everything in your power to avoid being such a personal failure." Then run the beach scene (Ch 22's relaxation picture) and work the two roadblocks: accept your limitations, forgive.

## Key Takeaways
1. Do not try too hard — strain jams the mechanism it is trying to drive.
2. Accept your limitations out loud, repeatedly: "What am I actually accomplishing at this pace?"
3. Compassion and forgiveness are the *parents* of relaxation; a grudge and peace of mind cannot coexist.
4. Relaxation is a readout of the self-image, not a technique bolted onto a tense one.
5. Make the practice exercises a habit at the level of washing your hands — regularity, not intensity.
