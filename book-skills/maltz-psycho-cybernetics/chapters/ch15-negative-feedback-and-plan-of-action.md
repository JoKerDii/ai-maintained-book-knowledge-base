# Chapter 15: How To Make Use of Negative Feedback / Attitudes Can Be Changed / Plan of Action / Tired People (Book Three: Live and Be Free Thru Psycho-Cybernetics, ch. 21–24)

## Core Idea
Mistakes are **steering corrections, not verdicts**. In a feedback system a negative signal means "adjust heading" — it is never a STOP sign. Reading errors any other way is what converts a correctable course into a settled failure identity.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Negative feedback as course correction**
  - Definition: "In a feedback system, a negative signal is the same as disapproval, telling you that you're going in the wrong direction… **The main concern of negative feedback is to alter and adjust the direction, not to halt it. Negative feedback is never a STOP sign.**"
  - When to use: the moment you make an error, get rejected, or miss a target.
  - How — Maltz's three steps: (1) **be aware** of the negative so it can warn you of danger; (2) **evaluate** it as unwanted, objectionable, unacceptable, something that cannot result in happiness; (3) **take whatever measures are necessary to correct the situation at once.**
  - Result: repeated, this becomes an **automatic reflex** stored as data, and negative feedback starts steering you clear of failure without conscious effort.
- **Glance at negatives, concentrate on positives**
  - The instrument-panel model: a modern feedback system has warning lights. When one illuminates it does not mean the system is defective — it means take corrective steps. **"When one of the panels lights up, no one becomes upset."**
  - But: "it is not necessary to give the instrument panel one's undivided attention. An occasional glance at the negative indicators is all that is required." Major attention stays on the positive aspects.
- **How to be effective with other people** — three prohibitions:
  1. Avoid trying too hard at the conscious level to impress them.
  2. Avoid doing or saying anything for the effect you believe it will have on them.
  3. Avoid thinking about what they think or feel about you.
- **Your Own Plan of Action** — the five-part goal procedure:
  1. **Crystallize your thinking** — decide precisely what you want.
  2. **Develop a written plan and a deadline for its attainment** (note: deadlines belong on *plans*, never on affirmations — see Ch 14).
  3. **Develop a real desire** — tested by the question "Is it really worth it to me?"
  4. **Develop self-confidence** — from recalled past successes.
  5. **Develop determination** — the follow-through.
- **The Personal Balance Sheet**: a self-audit across Know-how · Energy · Time · Imagination · People · Research and Development · Leadership · Intellectual Efficiency · Ambition · Responsibility · Conviction of Success. Plus a six-area life review: Social · Mental · Family Life · Spiritual · Physical · Financial.

## Key Concepts
- **Automatic reflex**: the goal of practicing steps 1–3 — correction that no longer requires deliberation.
- **The inhibited personality**: someone "always weighing everything he says and does in an effort to please other people," too easily offended, whose feelings are hurt by what someone thinks — or by **what he thinks they think.** Maltz's diagnosis: this is behaving badly "merely because of excessive negative feedback."
- **Tired people**: fatigue that is not physical. Maltz's chapter on why so many people are exhausted — the drain is unresolved negative feeling and goalless effort, not workload.
- **Attitudes can be changed** — stated flatly against the fixed-personality assumption.

## Mental Models
- **The tennis player and the net.** "A tennis player does not need to be aware consciously of the net; a golfer, of the sand traps and water hazards; a bowler, of the channels; or a high jumper or pole-vaulter, of the bar. They all concentrate on their objectives or positive goals." The hazard exists; it just isn't where your attention goes.
- **The warning light is information, not judgment.** Nobody gets upset at a dashboard light. The upset is a separate, optional, and expensive addition you make.
- **Excessive feedback jams the system.** Too much attention to the negative indicator is itself the malfunction — the mechanism was designed for an occasional glance.
- **Trying too hard is a feedback disorder.** Straining to impress is over-monitoring the audience's warning lights instead of playing your shot.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading a mistake as a STOP sign** — the anti-pattern the chapter exists to kill.
- **Concentrating on the negative indicator**: valid signal, catastrophic allocation of attention. This is the mechanism by which one error becomes a paralysis.
- **Trying too hard to impress** — Maltz's three prohibitions above. All three describe the same error: attention pointed at the observer rather than the task.
- **Being hurt by what you *think* others think** — a feedback loop with no external input at all.
- **Reviewing the past to decide the future.** "Our future and present are not decided by constantly turning over and reviewing the past." Doing so leaves you unable to regulate present thought.

## Worked Example
**Turning an error into a correction, using the three-step reflex.**

Suppose a proposal is rejected. Compare the two readings:

*Failure-mechanism reading:* the rejection is a verdict about your capability. You review it repeatedly. You conclude something about yourself. Attention pins to the warning light; the vehicle stops. Maltz's language: you have made negative feedback into a STOP sign, and you now steer using past failures as your guide to the present — which he elsewhere calls the mechanism of "immediate frustration."

*Servo-mechanism reading:*
1. **Aware.** The light is on. Registered. This is the system working correctly — you were off heading and you have been told.
2. **Evaluate.** This outcome is unwanted and cannot result in what I want. No commentary about my worth is contained in that sentence; the evaluation is of the *outcome*, not the person.
3. **Correct at once.** Adjust the approach and move — immediately, not after a period of review. The immediacy is the part people skip, and it is what installs the reflex.
4. **Return attention to the objective.** Glance at the indicator; concentrate on the goal. The golfer knows where the water is and looks at the flag.
5. **Repeat until automatic.** Maltz's claim is that these correct reactions become stored data, so the correction eventually happens without deliberation — the same way an experienced driver corrects a drift without noticing.

The parallel diagnosis he supplies for social life: if you are inhibited in a meeting, the cause is *excessive* negative feedback — too much monitoring of how you are landing. The remedy is the three prohibitions: stop trying to impress, stop calculating effect, stop thinking about what they think.

## Key Takeaways
1. Negative feedback is a steering signal. It is never a stop sign.
2. Aware → evaluate as unwanted → correct at once. The "at once" is what makes it a reflex.
3. Glance at the negatives; concentrate on the positives. Attention allocation is the whole game.
4. Inhibition is over-monitoring; the cure is the three prohibitions, not more self-scrutiny.
5. Put deadlines on written plans, never on your self-image work.
