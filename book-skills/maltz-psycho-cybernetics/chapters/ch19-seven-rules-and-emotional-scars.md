# Chapter 19: Seven Rules for Happy Living / Cast Off Your Emotional Scars (Book Four: The Magic Power of Self-Image Psychology, ch. 7–8)

## Core Idea
**Emotional scars are deeper than physical ones and harder to remove** — and unlike physical scars they keep generating new injury until you decide to heal them. Maltz supplies seven rules for happy living and four "miracle drugs" for the scars.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Seven Rules for Happy Living**
  1. **Get the happiness habit.** Smile inside and make the feeling part of you. "Even if some shadows blot out the sunshine, there is always something to feel good about."
  2. **Declare war on negative feelings.** Don't let unrealistic worries eat at you. Ask yourself why you, "who have every natural right to be happy, must spend your waking hours wrestling with fear, worry, and hate."
  3. **Strengthen your self-image.** See yourself as you've been in your best moments. Visualize happy times and the pride you felt. Imagine future joyful experiences. "Stop beating your brains in!"
  4. **Learn how to laugh.** Not a grin or a chuckle — "a real belly laugh that gives one a sense of release and freedom." Laughing, when genuine, is purifying: **"It is part of your success mechanism, jet-propelling you to victories in life."** If you haven't laughed since you were ten, go back into the school of your mind and re-learn it.
  5. **Dig out your buried treasures.** "Don't let your talents and resources just die inside you; give them a chance to meet the test of life!"
  6. **Help others.** Understand that many people who seem unpleasant or hostile are wearing facades they think will protect them. "Some people who seem the hardest are really soft and vulnerable. You'll feel great when you can give without thought of profit."
  7. **Seek activities that will make you happy.** Golf, tennis, painting, singing, sewing — Maltz explicitly refuses to choose for you. "An active life is a happy life *if you're doing what's right for you.*"
- **The Four Miracle Drugs for Emotional Scars**
  1. **Accept your limitations.** "If you stretch even a rubber band beyond its limits, it will break."
  2. **Forget your mistakes.** "We've all done things we'd like to undo, but it can't be done. Stop torturing yourself with self-blame!"
  3. **Forgive others.** "They're just as fallible as you are. They're not gods, and they're not machines; they're just human beings."
  4. **See yourself at your best.** Picture the situations you relished when things fit into place. Recapture the feeling, visualize it in glowing technicolor, hold onto it even if its impact shocks you. Keep these images alive and **reject the "failure" images and the sinking feeling in your stomach that goes with them. Feed your success mechanism!**

## Key Concepts
- **Emotional scars**: invisible, unfeelable — "but they can make the stomach rumble and the heart palpitate," and cause dizziness, nausea, insomnia, indigestion.
- **The vicious cycle**: emotional scars "bring on a vicious cycle of negative feelings, which never ends until you resolve to heal the wound." The resolve is the necessary trigger.
- **To others, your scar might seem a scratch** — which is why external validation of the injury is not the route to healing it.
- **You're tougher than you think.**
- **Don't hold a grudge against yourself** — the chapter's most important heading. "You cannot be happy and successful if you hold a grudge against yourself."

## Mental Models
- **The ointment of kindness.** Maltz's instruction is physical and gentle: "Remove your emotional scars with the ointment of kindness. **Apply it gently in the areas which hurt.**" Then he names the wounds specifically — a lifelong fear of romantic entanglement that kept you from marriage; an inability to channel aggression that cost you vocational achievement; something you blurted out once that hurt a dear friend. For each: be your own gentle doctor, apply the soothing salve of kind thinking, "rub gently till it no longer hurts."
- **A brief period of resentment is legitimate.** Maltz is unusually permissive: after an unfair blow, being bitter and short-tempered "for a few days" is understandable, "maybe even desirable, for it is often better to cleanse your system than to let inner hate eat away at you." What is forbidden is letting the scar **permanently dominate** your thoughts.
- **Resentment radiates outward and returns.** The wounded man vows to refuse anyone any favor — fifty cents, a push for a stalled car. Failing to get even with the one who hurt him, he takes it out on his sister, the postman, maybe the cat. "Bristling with hostility, you flaunt your emotional scar at the world, and the world reacting with resentment sets up a nightmarish crosscurrent that seems unending."
- **Forgive because you're not always fair either.**

## Anti-patterns
- **Carrying resentment as identity** — the chapter's central target.
- **Holding a grudge against yourself**: the version people don't recognize as a grudge at all.
- **Waiting for the injury to be acknowledged** before healing it. To others your scar may look like a scratch; healing cannot be contingent on their assessment.
- **Demanding that the world be fair before you re-engage.** "There is a place for you in society if you will meet life halfway."
- **Forcing an activity on yourself** to satisfy someone else's idea of a happy life (Rule 7's failure mode).

## Worked Example
**The unfair firing — Maltz's case, worked to a conclusion.**

You are in your late twenties. Three or four jobs, none of which suited you; each time you gave notice and looked for greener pastures, mostly finding muddy fields. Then a large publishing company hires you as assistant personnel director. Good salary, promising prospects. The director who hired you was skeptical of your background — most of your experience was clerical work in banks — but thought you might produce what was needed.

The catch: you needed three or four months to adjust. Given leniency for that period, you were confident you could make the grade.

The director was an impatient man, afraid of your inexperience, and watched you from day one. You made mistakes; he was critical; you became nervous and committed several more. One day he called you in and said he was sorry — you just weren't suitable.

**Since then you have felt a failure. An emotional scar has taken root.**

Maltz's ruling: "You have been treated shabbily and, since this job was important to you, a certain amount of resentment is natural." A few days of bitterness is fine — cleansing, even. But the scar must not take permanent hold.

The intervention, in order:
1. **Widen the field.** "There are good things in life; there are other avenues down which you can drive, there are other jobs, other fields."
2. **Forgive the director** — and here is the crucial move: *"You don't know what problems are making him insecure so that he couldn't give you a fair chance. It took courage for him to hire someone inexperienced in this area. When you made mistakes, he might have been fearful of his responsibility for them."* Maltz does not ask you to decide he was right. He asks you to recognize you were reading his fear as malice.
3. **Forgive on grounds of symmetry.** "Forgive because you are a human being too, and you're not always fair yourself."
4. **Remove and redirect.** "Remove the emotional scar and apply yourself to positive goals once again. Get your old perspective back; oil up the old machine, and get back on the ball."

## Key Takeaways
1. Emotional scars generate new injury continuously; the cycle ends only when you resolve to heal it.
2. A few days of resentment is healthy cleansing; permanent residence is the disease.
3. Apply the four drugs by name: accept limitations, forget mistakes, forgive others, see yourself at your best.
4. Forgive the person who hurt you by considering what fear was driving them — not by deciding they were right.
5. Learn to laugh properly; Maltz classifies genuine laughter as part of the success mechanism.
