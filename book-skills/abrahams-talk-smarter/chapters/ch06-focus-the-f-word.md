# Chapter 6: Focus — The F-Word of Spontaneous Speaking

## Core Idea
The most powerful communication conveys everything the audience needs — and only that. "Focus" is the F-word of spontaneous speaking: strip away the fuzzy, irrelevant, dense, jargon-laden, and long-winded so you draw attention to what matters most. Focus has four dimensions to practice one at a time.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Dimensions of Focus**: precision, relevance, accessibility, concision. Improve each with the "Bottom Line Up Front" summary below.
- **Dimension #1 — Precision (Know, Feel, Do)**: have a clear, complete goal.
  - When to use: before any spontaneous moment, to decide what to say *and what not to*.
  - How: ask three questions — what do you want the audience to **know** (content), **feel** (emotion), and **do** (action)? People usually nail "know," ignore "feel," and stay vague on "do." Define measures of success and evaluate afterward.
- **Dimension #2 — Relevance ("Why should I care?")**: focus on importance and salience *for the audience*.
  - How: in the moment ask "Who is this? What do they need to hear? How do I frame this for them?"; in advance analyze audience knowledge, impressions, resistance, and motivations; **create curiosity or tension** (raise an unexpected point/question first, then answer) to make the topic feel urgent; **turn resistance into curiosity** by lowering defensiveness with open, inclusive, cooperative framing (Tormala). Koch's **"Golden Rule of Selling"**: never ask customers to do something not in their long-term best interest.
- **Dimension #3 — Accessibility ("Why is this so complicated?")**: make content understandable; kill jargon and acronyms.
  - How: apply the **grandmother test** (would my grandmother understand?); be a **translator** of complex ideas; **chunk** into a few simple steps (creates multiple "starting moments" that aid memory); lead with **BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front)** / "don't bury the lead"; prep analogies or a simple whiteboard visual in advance.
- **Dimension #4 — Concision ("Why so long-winded?")**: be crisp.
  - How: "minimal words"; leverage context so you don't state the obvious; aim for a concision-clarity sweet spot (Hilary Price's "49% of the information," letting the audience fill in the rest); run **brevity challenges** (say it as a tweet/140 chars, a haiku, a six-word story, or PechaKucha); analyze transcripts/texts for verbosity patterns.

## Key Concepts
- **Focus**: conveying exactly the desired message and only that — not distracting, boring, or wasting time.
- **Know–Feel–Do**: a goal is not just intended meaning but the broader impact (information, emotion, action) you intend it to have.
- **Curse of knowledge**: you know too much and assume/use language others find impenetrable.
- **Curse of passion**: you ramble on because *you* care, assuming the audience does too.
- **The GOAT anecdote**: a military team so jargon-laden it needed a "Glossary of Acronyms and Terms" — itself an acronym — instead of just paring back language.
- **Selfless/selling relevance (Koch)**: understand what customers perceive their need to be before trying to change beliefs.
- **Chunking**: breaking a message into simple pieces creates multiple starting moments and boosts recall.
- **BLUF / don't bury the lead**: state the main point first, then add detail.
- **Overcorrection risk**: each dimension can be pushed too far — too goal-directed = rigid; too tailored = narrow; too accessible = dumbed-down; too concise = confusing.

## Mental Models
- Before speaking, run Know–Feel–Do like a targeting system — vague goals produce distracting, incoherent answers.
- Apply the grandmother test as a jargon filter even when your grandmother isn't the audience.
- Think of yourself as a translator (LitCharts/SparkNotes) turning complexity into plain terms, not a status-signaling expert.
- Use "49% of the information" (Price) as a concision target — give enough context, then trust the audience to close the gap.
- Improve one dimension at a time; don't try to max all four at once.

## Anti-patterns
- **Answering only the rational mind with data**: the boss burying a simple opportunity under charts until the room tunes out (Abrahams even catches himself doing this mid-chapter).
- **Twenty-minute technical answer relevant to a handful of people**: the gaming founder who missed the primary audience; "Yes, that feature ships in the next release" would have sufficed.
- **Broadcasting expertise via jargon/acronyms**: creates distance and boredom (the GOAT).
- **Over-explaining requests**: Abrahams' over-rationalizing with his kids vs. his wife's "minimal words," which resolves conflict faster.
- **Pushing any dimension to extreme**: rigidity, over-narrow tailoring, dumbing down, or confusing terseness.

## Worked Example
Two contrasting demonstrations of accessibility and concision. **Steve Jobs** could have listed the iPod's design, weight, screen, and storage; instead he introduced it with five words — **"1,000 songs in your pocket."** In one focused phrase he evoked the old pain (lugging CDs, limited MP3 storage), distinguished the product, and conveyed its practical value. **Hans Rosling**, in "the shortest TED Talk ever," largely improvised: holding seven stones (one billion people each), he laid them out by affluence — one could fly, one owned a car, three saved for a bike, two only for shoes — then rearranged and added stones to show population rising to ten billion and everyone climbing the economic ladder, ending on the question of whether the rich are prepared to be integrated with the formerly poor in a world of ten billion. He skipped economists, jargon ("crude birth rate," "doubling time"), and data dumps — keeping it relevant, accessible, and stunningly concise. Both show: focus, not word count, is what makes a message land.

## Key Takeaways
1. Focus is the F-word of spontaneous speaking: say what the audience needs, and only that.
2. Precision = Know–Feel–Do; define all three, especially the usually-ignored feel and do.
3. Relevance answers "Why should I care?" — frame for the audience and manufacture curiosity to cut resistance.
4. Accessibility beats jargon: use the grandmother test, translate, chunk, and lead with BLUF.
5. Concision means minimal words and leveraging context; run brevity challenges — but don't push any dimension so far it backfires. Work on one at a time.
