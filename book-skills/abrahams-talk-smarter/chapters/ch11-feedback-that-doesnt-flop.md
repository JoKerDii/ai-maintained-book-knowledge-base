# Chapter 11: Feedback That Doesn't Flop

## Core Idea
Feedback framed as judgment from on high triggers defensiveness; feedback framed as an invitation to solve a problem together builds shared ownership and strengthens the relationship. A four-part structure keeps in-the-moment feedback clear, constructive, and collaborative.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 4 I's**: A structure for delivering feedback that is inviting and collaborative.
  - When to use: giving feedback in the moment — to a report, peer, boss, student, or child.
  - How:
    - **I — Information**: Present concrete, specific, verifiable observations; leave out emotion. Also delineate what you're *not* discussing, to scope the conversation.
    - **I — Impact**: Explain the effect on you using first-person singular ("I think," "I feel"); owning your reaction reduces the other's defensiveness and shows why it matters.
    - **I — Invitation**: Make a specific request to address the issue — phrased as a question ("How can we…?") to invite participation, or as a declarative for directness/urgency.
    - **I — Implications**: Detail the positive and/or negative consequences of adopting or rejecting the change.

## Key Concepts
- **Feedback as invitation, not judgment**: The reframe that replaces a power dynamic with shared ownership and accountability.
- **Scoping ("what I'm not discussing")**: Explicitly bounding the topic so both parties focus (e.g., timeliness, not quality).
- **"We" and questions**: Word choices that put both parties on the same level and grant the recipient agency.
- **Nonteachable moment (Ruben Nieves)**: A time/place where the recipient can't absorb feedback — distracted, public, having a bad day.
- **Acknowledge emotions without naming them**: Recognizing intensity ("I can hear how important this is") without labeling it, to avoid debating the emotion instead of the problem.
- **Ask what kind of feedback is wanted**: Checking whether the person wants support/catharsis or advice/solutions before diving in.
- **Less is more**: Limiting yourself to the one or two most important changes.

## Mental Models
- Use "invitation, not judgment" whenever you feel the urge to tell someone what to do.
- Use question-framed invitations ("How might we…?") to grant agency; switch to declarative when you've raised it before or are pressed for time.
- Use tone deliberately — the same 4 I's can be collaborative or stern depending on delivery and which implications you stress.
- Use "acknowledge without naming" when a conversation turns emotionally charged.
- Ask what type of feedback is wanted before offering solutions, especially with people who want to be heard, not fixed.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging from on high**: Positions you as authority and the recipient as passive, breeding defensiveness.
- **Emotion in the Information step**: Muddying facts with feeling invites argument; keep observations verifiable.
- **Feedback at a nonteachable moment**: Wrong place/time (crowded venue, bad day) means it won't land.
- **Naming the recipient's emotion**: "You seem upset" invites "No, I'm frustrated," derailing into a debate about the feeling.
- **Praising something trivial before criticizing**: Complimenting an outfit then panning the work feels forced and disingenuous — praise real contributions.
- **Drowning them in feedback**: Too many points means none get absorbed.

## Worked Example
Your boss consistently calls on men and overlooks women raising their hands, hurting team morale — feedback given upward, using the 4 I's: "When you asked for input today, you only called on men and didn't let the women with raised hands speak [Information]. I fear this signals to the women on my team that you don't value their work as much as the men's [Impact]. Are there things I can do to help you involve the women on my team more [Invitation]? If we address this, I think you'll find all my team members have insights that help solve the challenges you bring us [Implication]." Note the "we/I can help" framing that makes even upward feedback collaborative rather than accusatory.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reframe feedback as a joint problem-solving invitation, not a verdict.
2. 4 I's = Information (facts, scoped) / Impact (your first-person reaction) / Invitation (specific request) / Implications (consequences).
3. Own your reaction with "I" and share the level with "we" to reduce defensiveness.
4. Prepare (why they act this way, what you want to see) and ask what kind of feedback they want.
5. Be timely but respect context — deliver in person, at a teachable moment, once emotions have settled.
6. Tone is a lever: the same structure can be gentle or stern; stay balanced with genuine praise.
7. Monitor emotions (acknowledge without naming) and stay focused on one or two changes.
