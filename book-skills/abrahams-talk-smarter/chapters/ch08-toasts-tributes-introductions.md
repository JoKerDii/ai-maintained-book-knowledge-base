# Chapter 8: Toasts That Tantalize (and Tributes and Introductions, Too)

## Core Idea
Toasts, tributes, and introductions are not about you — they are gifts you bestow on the audience and the person being honored. Reframing them as gifts prompts you to package them well, and a simple four-part structure keeps them focused, concise, and memorable.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **WHAT formula**: A four-part structure for celebratory or commemorative remarks.
  - When to use: weddings, funerals, product launches, panels, work anniversaries, introducing a speaker — any moment you must say something meaningful about a person, team, or organization.
  - How:
    - **W — Why are we here**: Identify the context/purpose of the gathering; this focuses the audience and lets you convey emotion and importance.
    - **H — How are you connected**: Tell the audience who you are and why you're the one speaking; a place to slip in background or light humor.
    - **A — Anecdotes or learnings**: Share the bulk of engaging content — relatable, appropriate, concise stories or lessons. Think a few minutes, not tens of minutes.
    - **T — Thank**: Express gratitude and well-wishes to those being commemorated (and the audience).

## Key Concepts
- **Remarks as a gift**: The mental reframe that shifts focus from your self-image to the recipient's experience, naturally driving you toward structure.
- **Clearing the runway**: Setting up whoever follows you (next speaker, honoree) for a "smooth takeoff" with logistics, agenda, and a positive handoff.
- **The "I" audit**: Noticing overuse of the word "I" as a signal you've drifted the spotlight onto yourself.
- **Staying in bounds**: Calibrating emotional expression to what your role and audience expect (a project manager may show more emotion than a senior leader).
- **Striving for unity**: Choosing common ground everyone can support without compromising your values, especially in polarized settings.

## Mental Models
- Use the "gift" frame when nerves pull your attention onto how you're being perceived.
- Use the "I" audit mid-remarks when you suspect you're centering yourself instead of the honoree.
- Use "clearing the runway" when you are the opening act for another speaker.
- Use "if you can't stay in bounds, hand it off" when your honest remarks would require airing division you can't set aside — suggest someone else speak.

## Anti-patterns
- **Going long / covering too many topics**: Dilutes impact; no one ever complains a tribute was too short.
- **Reading from notes when highly emotional**: Distracts you and disconnects you from the audience — plan a closing line or a backup person instead.
- **Spotlight-hogging**: Excessive "I" and personal detail steals focus from the subject.
- **Inside jokes / unexplained jargon**: Leaves most of the audience out; explain acronyms briefly.
- **Needling or salty humor that divides**: Misreads the audience's emotional state and the occasion.

## Worked Example
Colleagues gather on Zoom to remember Edwina, a mentor who died of cancer; remarks are kept to about a minute each. Using WHAT: "Edwina was an amazing person whose wise counsel was sought after far and wide [W]. I was fortunate enough to follow her in two leadership roles she held [H]. I clearly remember heated decision-making sessions where I — as many of you still do — thought, 'What would Edwina do?' Reflecting on her approach helped me contribute usefully [A]. I invite all of us to remember Edwina for how she touched our lives and so many others [T]." Brief, sincere, appropriately emotional, and it sets others up by inviting them to share their own memories.

## Key Takeaways
1. These moments are gifts for others, not performances about you.
2. WHAT = Why are we here / How are you connected / Anecdotes or learnings / Thank.
3. Be brief and to the point — a good tribute does one thing: memorably honor its subject.
4. Prepare for emotion; have a closing line or a stand-in ready, and avoid leaning on notes.
5. Keep anecdotes accessible and appropriate; audit your "I" count to keep the spotlight off yourself.
6. Seek unity and common ground; if you truly can't, let someone else give the remarks.
7. Clear the runway — end positively and hand off logistics so the next speaker succeeds.
