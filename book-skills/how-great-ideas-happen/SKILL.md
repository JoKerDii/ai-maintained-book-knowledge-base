## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The thesis — creativity is discovery, not conjuring.** Replace the genius myth with the **creative explorer**: someone who searches for ideas that already exist in the landscape around them. Evidence: multiple discovery (Bell and Gray filed the same day; nine independent telescope inventors), and a ~30,000-career analysis where >90% of creators show a *hot streak* triggered by one fertile idea. Practical consequence: when stuck, **change your inputs, not your effort**.

### The Dig — four stages, in order
Treat any creative project like an archaeological dig. Doing them out of order is the single most common failure.

1. **Surveying** (Part I) — decide *where* to search. Read the terrain before digging.
2. **Gridding** (Part II) — section the space so you know where you looked *and where you didn't*.
3. **Digging** (Part III) — generate volume inside the chosen cell.
4. **Sifting** (Part IV) — choose what to pursue, mostly by subtraction.

**Mode-switching**: Surveying is deep→surface, Gridding is deep, Digging is surface, Sifting is deep. Know which mode you owe the work right now.

### Surveying
- **Explore, then Exploit** — decide explicitly which mode you're in and for how long. Brainstorming first is digging at random.
- **The 5% Novelty Rule** — hits cluster at ~90–95% conventional / 5–10% novel (Uzzi, 18M papers). Prefer a sharp small departure from a celebrated concept over wholesale originality. Virgil Abloh's version: alter a design by **3%** — "it's sampling."
- **Don't be an Originality Ostrich** — creators assume originality only helps; audiences penalize it, invisibly to the creator. On *Top Chef*, contestants who explicitly chased originality were >2× more likely to have the worst dish. Novelty means new *to you*; "it's been done before" is a creativity killer.
- **Second-mover advantage** — the tweak, not the original, breaks through (CouchSurfing → Airbnb; Rhapsody/Last.fm → Spotify).
- **Problem finding over problem solving** — let the problem emerge from **close looking** (30–60 min on one object) instead of importing a predefined one. Fight the **inner explainer**, the voice that labels a scene and thereby ends observation. Use **bottom-up processing**; distrust top-down expectation.
- **Knowledge funnel** — deep in one or two domains, shallow in many, skilled at translating between them.

### Gridding
- **The Guiding Question** — the compass fusing *what* you're making with *why* it matters. Template: *"What is a [project type] that [provides unique value/meaning] for [audience] in [context], despite [obstacle]?"* Gate it on four criteria: **specific, open-ended, motivating, measurable**. Questions are dynamic — sharpen them with data.
- **Prefer outside-in over inside-out** — start from the audience's end experience ("what will they think about on the drive home?"), not from what you want to say. If you bore yourself giving the one-minute elevator pitch, the *idea* is wrong.
- **The What and Why Grid** — (1) list existing successes, (2) derive *what* and *why* categories from that list, (3) grid them (what = columns, why = rows), (4) fill every cell weighting toward your expertise and drop barren rows/columns, (5) enter the cell with the most branching options. The grid's real value: it tells you where you *haven't* looked, which free brainstorming never can.
- **Constraints Are Creativity's Engine** — the more rigid the box, the better you think inside it. In Moreau & Dahl's toy study the *most*-constrained group produced the highest-rated designs. Convert each constraint into an advantage for one named audience persona.
- **Transplanting — The Three R's**: **Recognition** (spot the cross-domain connection) → **Relevance** (map element-to-element what actually overlaps) → **Refinement** (prototype in the new context with experts). Stopping after Recognition is the default failure. Look for **brokers** spanning **structural holes**, and for **sleeping beauties** — dormant ideas a new domain can awaken.

### Digging
- **The Creative Cliff Illusion** — the felt collapse in idea quality after ~2 minutes never happens; late-session ideas rate *higher*. So: **double or triple** whatever target seems reasonable, and run deliberate second and third rounds past the point you'd normally stop. The first idea decreases creativity; the ninety-seventh may enhance it.
- **Look for a *set* of ideas, not the right idea** — mine a localized region for near-variants and compare.
- **Narrative Reversals** — list your assumptions, ask "what if the opposite were true?"
- **Group Digging protocol** — declare an **evaluation-free zone**; separate Digging from Sifting into different sessions; formalize turn-taking; take anonymous submissions; generate deliberately bad ideas; let someone junior lead; enforce **"yes, and."** Collective intelligence tracks emotional intelligence and turn-taking, not member IQ.
- **Early diversification** and **weak ties** beat depth-only networks. The anti-pattern is **turtling-up** — retreating into strong ties under pressure.
- **The Spark** — a kernel idea is worth elaborating if it cues something *specific and complex*: a core idea plus satellites, mood, energy, flow. More time searching means less time overall.
- **AI as excavator** — guide it to an already-gridded region, solicit many, then **curate with intent**. Keep the deep work (Surveying, Gridding, Sifting) human; beware the homogenization effect.

### Sifting
- **The Creative Endowment Effect** — you overvalue an idea because you authored it. Warnings and examples do *not* debias it. Only **psychological distance** works: hand the list to someone else, or shelve it for months.
- **Float a Raft, don't Build a Tower** — the Tower posture crams everything in and defends it; the Raft posture asks what an audience inspecting for holes will find. Write out your guiding question, state what each element contributes, cut what doesn't serve it. Counter **subtraction neglect**: people improve things only by adding unless explicitly prompted.
- **Great ideas feel uncomfortable** — curiosity peaks at an *information gap*, for questions you sort of already know. Rank ideas most→least comfortable and develop the **most uncomfortable** for 10–15 minutes before discarding. **Be wary of brilliance**: praise the idea, not the genius — brilliance-focused cultures drive talent out.
- **Wright's law** — effort per unit falls ~**10–15%** with each doubling of cumulative output. So **tweak and ship again; never restart**. Watch the **identity trap** (refusing a change because the current version feels like you).
- **Feedback**: state your guiding question as context first; ask "what specific aspects are strong / could be improved?" not "do you like it?"; weight the **tails** of the response distribution over the agreeable middle, then triage the middle by **frequency**.
- **Schedule a Mid-Stakes Event** — a dated exposure to a real but non-gatekeeping audience. Predict the reactions beforehand, then audit your predictions. The audit, not the feedback, is the durable gain.

### The Three Roadblocks (Conclusion: Getting Unstuck)
- **Stuck with nothing** → you haven't done enough work up front (no *why*, no guiding question, no grid).
- **Stuck with something mediocre** → you settled too early. "Ideas are cheap" is false; breakthrough ideas are rare.
- **Stuck defending** → you fell in love with an idea rather than the process (including attachment to *finishing*).
