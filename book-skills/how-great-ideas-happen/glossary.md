# Glossary — *How Great Ideas Happen* (George Newman)

**5% Novelty Rule** — Hit ideas sit at roughly 90–95% conventional and 5–10% novel; make a small, sharp departure from an established concept rather than a wholesale one (Ch 2).

**Adjacent possible** — The set of moves reachable from where you currently stand; ideas arrive only when their neighboring components already exist (Ch 6).

**Analogical reasoning** — Using a familiar concrete case to unlock the abstract relational structure of a new problem; Hofstadter's "engine of cognition" (Ch 6).

**Attending to negative space** — Deliberately examining what is absent or unmarked in a scene, rather than the labeled objects (Ch 3).

**Bottom-up processing** — Building a representation from the sensory data actually present, rather than from expectation (Ch 3).

**Brick-counting fallacy** — Mistaking accumulation of facts for progress toward an idea; volume of detail is not directed thinking (Ch 11).

**Brilliance-focused culture** — An environment that prizes innate genius; signals belonging to a narrow group and drives talent out (Ch 11).

**Broker** — A person who connects otherwise disconnected clusters of a network, and therefore sees combinations no one inside either cluster can (Ch 6, Burt).

**Building a Tower** — The creator's default posture: cram in every element you built, defending the whole (Ch 10). Contrast *Floating a Raft*.

**Close looking** — Sustained 30–60 minute examination of a single object or scene, past the point where the inner explainer stops you (Ch 3).

**Collective intelligence** — A team's general problem-solving capacity; driven by emotional intelligence and turn-taking, not average member IQ (Ch 8).

**Creative Cliff Illusion** — The false belief that idea quality collapses after the first couple of minutes; in fact late-session ideas rate *higher* (Ch 7, Lucas & Nordgren).

**Creative Endowment Effect** — Overvaluing an idea because you authored it, which wrecks your ability to sift your own list (Ch 10).

**Creative explorer** — Newman's replacement for the genius myth: someone who searches for ideas that already exist in the landscape (Introduction).

**Curation** — Selecting with intent from a large generated set; the human half of working with AI (Ch 9).

**Digging** — Stage 3: generating many candidate ideas within the region you have gridded (Part III).

**Directed thinking** — Reasoning aimed by a structural question rather than by accumulating facts (Ch 11, Wertheimer on Einstein).

**Early diversification** — Sampling widely across styles/domains early in a career; predicts later hits via skill portfolio, transferable expertise, and better talent-matching (Ch 8, Berg).

**Evaluation-free zone** — A declared period in which no idea may be judged, protecting the Digging stage from premature Sifting (Ch 7, Ch 8).

**Floating a Raft** — Reframing your idea as something an audience will inspect for holes, which puts you in subtraction mode (Ch 10).

**Gridding** — Stage 2: sectioning the conceptual space so every combination gets examined, and you know where you *haven't* looked (Part II).

**Guiding Question** — The compass question fusing what you are making with why it matters; must be specific, open-ended, motivating, and measurable (Ch 4).

**Identity trap** — Refusing a needed change because the current version feels like *you*; blocks tweaks (Ch 12).

**Information-gap theory of curiosity** — Curiosity peaks for questions you *sort of* already know the answer to; discomfort marks the gap worth digging into (Ch 11, Loewenstein).

**Inner explainer** — The internal voice that labels a scene and thereby ends observation (Ch 3).

**Inside-out method** — Starting a project from your own goals and interests; Newman's own failure mode. Contrast *outside-in method* (Ch 4).

**Kernel idea / the spark** — The seed that cues something specific and complex — core idea, satellites, mood, energy, flow — and is therefore worth elaborating (Ch 9).

**King of the Mountain** — Tournament-style group selection: each person brings their top two ideas, and rounds narrow to a top three (Ch 8, Ex. 25).

**Knowledge funnel** — Deep expertise in one or two domains, shallow familiarity with many, plus skill at translating between them (Ch 3).

**Low-resolution surveying** — Knowing a little about a lot, deliberately; the input side of transplanting (Ch 2).

**Mid-Stakes Event** — A scheduled, dated exposure of work to a real but non-gatekeeping audience, used to force selection and test predictions (Ch 10, Ex. 32).

**Multiple discovery** — The same idea reached independently by several parties, often simultaneously; evidence that ideas are found, not conjured (Ch 1).

**Narrative Reversal** — Listing your assumptions and asking "what if the opposite were true?" (Ch 7, Knight).

**Originality Ostrich** — A creator who assumes originality only helps, blind to the fact that audiences penalize excess novelty (Ch 2).

**Outside-in method** — Starting from the audience's anticipated end experience and working backward (Ch 4).

**Problem finding** — Letting the problem emerge from close observation instead of importing a predefined one (Ch 3, Csikszentmihalyi & Getzels).

**Psychological distance** — Separation from your own work via elapsed time or other people's eyes; the prerequisite for subtraction (Ch 10).

**Refinement** — Third R: prototyping and iterating a transplanted concept in its new context, usually with other experts (Ch 6).

**Recognition** — First R: spotting a candidate cross-domain connection (Ch 6).

**Relevance** — Second R: mapping element-by-element what actually overlaps between the two domains (Ch 6).

**Second-mover advantage** — The tweak, not the original, is usually what breaks through (Ch 2).

**Set of ideas** — What to hunt for instead of "the right idea": a localized cluster of near-variants to compare (Ch 7).

**Sifting** — Stage 4: choosing which ideas to pursue, mostly by subtraction (Part IV).

**Sleeping Beauty** — A dormant idea that lies unused for years until a new domain awakens it (Ch 6).

**Structural hole** — The gap between disconnected network clusters that a broker spans (Ch 6, Burt).

**Subtraction neglect** — The robust human tendency to improve things only by adding, never by removing (Ch 10, Adams).

**Surveying** — Stage 1: reading the conceptual terrain to decide where to search (Part I).

**The Three R's of Transplanting** — Recognition, Relevance, and Refinement; skipping past Recognition is the standard failure (Ch 6).

**The Unconscious Grid** — The implicit constraint structure that produces the sensation that "the story writes itself" (Ch 5).

**The What and Why Grid** — A two-axis grid (what you make × why it matters) whose cells you fill to find unoccupied combinations (Ch 5).

**Top-down processing** — Perception driven by prior expectation; the source of seeing what you expected rather than what is there (Ch 3).

**Transplanting** — Taking an existing concept from one domain and applying it somewhere new and unexpected (Ch 6).

**Turtling-up** — Retreating into your existing strong-tie network when threatened, which starves you of new information (Ch 8).

**Wright's law** — Cost/effort per unit falls a predictable ~10–15% with each doubling of cumulative output; the most successful entrepreneurs are identifiable by adherence to it (Ch 12).

**"Yes, and"** — The improv rule adopted as a group Digging protocol: build on contributions rather than evaluating them (Ch 8).
