# Patterns — techniques from *How Great Ideas Happen*

## The 5% Workshop
**When to use**: You have a domain and want a viable idea fast, without the originality penalty.
**How**: Copy a celebrated existing work verbatim (score it, rebuild it, retype it). Brainstorm ≥10 distinct modifications that alter roughly 5% of it. Implement one at a time and compare.
**Trade-offs**: Feels like cheating and produces no origin story. But it targets the 90–95% conventional / 5–10% novel band where hits actually cluster; pure originality reliably underperforms with audiences.

## Close Looking
**When to use**: You need a *problem* worth solving and don't have one — or your read of a situation is running on assumption.
**How**: Pick one mundane scene from your actual work (people reacting to an idea, customers browsing, your own routine). Observe 10–60 minutes without labeling. When the inner explainer names the thing, keep going. Record what is absent, not just what's present.
**Trade-offs**: Slow and feels unproductive; there is no deliverable. It's the only reliable route from top-down assumption to bottom-up data.

## The Guiding Question Template
**When to use**: Start of any project, and again whenever you feel directionless mid-project.
**How**: Fill in — "What is a [type of project] that [provides a unique form of value or meaning] for [a particular audience] in [a specific context/environment], despite [potential obstacles]?" Test it against four criteria: specific, open-ended, motivating, measurable. If you bore yourself delivering it as a one-minute elevator pitch, the idea needs work, not the pitch.
**Trade-offs**: A too-specific question forecloses search; too broad and it fails as a compass. Expect to sharpen it with data as you go — it's meant to be dynamic.

## The Outside-In Flip
**When to use**: Your work is well-crafted but isn't landing.
**How**: Replace "What do I want to say?" with "What does my audience need to hear?" and "What will they be thinking about on the drive home?" Design backward from that end experience.
**Trade-offs**: Can slide into pandering if you skip the *why*. Pair with the guiding question so audience-fit doesn't erase point of view.

## The What and Why Grid
**When to use**: You have a guiding question and need to map the search space before generating.
**How**: (1) Gather data — list existing successes in the space. (2) Derive *what* categories and *why* categories from that list. (3) Build the grid: what = columns, why = rows. (4) Fill every cell, weighting toward your expertise; delete barren rows/columns. (5) Pick the cell with the most branching options and narrow in.
**Trade-offs**: Overhead before any ideation, and the axes you choose bound what you can find. Its payoff is knowing where you *didn't* look — free brainstorming can't tell you that.

## Constraints into Opportunities
**When to use**: The brief feels impossibly restrictive (budget, format, tech, time).
**How**: List every constraint. Invent one named audience persona with a history and goals. Convert each constraint into an advantage *for that person*. Repeat with a different persona.
**Trade-offs**: Requires taking the constraints as fixed, which is wrong sometimes. But the more rigid the box, the better people think inside it — the most-constrained groups produce the highest-rated work.

## The Three R's of Transplanting
**When to use**: You've noticed a concept in another domain that might apply to yours.
**How**: **Recognition** — name the candidate connection. **Relevance** — map element to element what actually overlaps; be explicit, not metaphorical. **Refinement** — prototype in the new context with domain experts. Different people are often good at different R's; staff each stage separately.
**Trade-offs**: Stopping after Recognition is the default failure — a pleasing analogy that never gets tested. Relevance is where most transplants die, and should.

## Push Past the Creative Cliff
**When to use**: Any Digging session, at the exact moment you feel tapped out.
**How**: Set a target and then double or triple it. Run a deliberate second and third round after the point where you'd normally stop. Sort output into at least 3 categories to expose thin regions.
**Trade-offs**: Uncomfortable and feels wasteful. The felt cliff is an illusion — later ideas rate *higher* in quality, and the first idea suppresses creativity while the ninety-seventh may enhance it.

## Narrative Reversal Sweep
**When to use**: The idea set is competent but predictable.
**How**: List the assumptions your idea rests on. For each, ask "what if the opposite were true?" Keep the ones that are still coherent.
**Trade-offs**: Generates a lot of nonsense per usable reversal. Cheap enough that the ratio doesn't matter.

## Group Digging Protocol
**When to use**: Ideation with more than one person.
**How**: Declare an evaluation-free zone. Separate Digging from Sifting into different sessions. Formalize turn-taking (randomize with cards or an RNG). Accept anonymous submissions. Purposely generate bad ideas. Let someone other than the senior person lead. Enforce "yes, and."
**Trade-offs**: Feels bureaucratic. Without it, the highest-status voice sets the frame and collective intelligence collapses.

## Engaging Your Weak Ties
**When to use**: Your idea set is converging and everyone around you agrees with you.
**How**: Consult acquaintances outside your cluster — unprimed, without pre-explaining what you want to hear. Reach for people whose domain doesn't overlap yours.
**Trade-offs**: Weak ties give less committed help. But strong ties mostly know what you already know; turtling-up under pressure is the anti-pattern.

## Sifting with Others
**When to use**: You must select from your own list — i.e., always, because the creative endowment effect makes self-selection unreliable.
**How**: Hand your list to someone else and compare their picks with yours. Or shelve the list for weeks-to-months and re-select with time-created distance. Warning yourself about the bias does not work; only distance does.
**Trade-offs**: Costs calendar time or social capital. Being told about the bias, or shown examples, demonstrably fails to correct it.

## Floating a Raft (not Building a Tower)
**When to use**: Cutting an over-built piece of work.
**How**: Write out your guiding question. Go element by element and state what each contributes to it. Cut anything that doesn't serve it — including material you love. Ask what an audience inspecting this for holes would find.
**Trade-offs**: Painful; the tangent material is often the prettiest. People almost never edit by subtraction unless explicitly prompted to.

## The Mid-Stakes Event
**When to use**: The work could drift indefinitely, and low-stakes feedback isn't forcing decisions.
**How**: Schedule a dated exposure to a real but non-gatekeeping audience. Before it, write down predicted reactions. After, audit your predictions against what happened.
**Trade-offs**: Manufactured deadlines can force premature closure. The prediction audit — not the feedback — is the durable payoff: it calibrates your taste.

## Develop the Uncomfortable Idea
**When to use**: Sifting a list where one idea keeps making you uneasy.
**How**: Rank ideas most to least comfortable. Take the *most* uncomfortable and develop it for 10–15 minutes before discarding. Treat discomfort as an information gap, not a verdict.
**Trade-offs**: Most uncomfortable ideas are just bad. Cheap to test, and the ones that survive are disproportionately the interesting ones.

## Planning for Feedback
**When to use**: Before every feedback conversation.
**How**: Decide the purpose of the feedback. Provide context — state your guiding question first. Ask better questions: replace "Do you like this?" with "What specific aspects are strong / could be improved?" and "Does this remind you of other things you've seen?" Pre-declare what you will not change.
**Trade-offs**: Framing the ask constrains what you'll hear. Unframed feedback is mostly noise about taste.

## The Bell Curve of Feedback
**When to use**: Triaging a pile of responses.
**How**: Weight the tails — the outlier comments — over the agreeable middle. Then triage the middle by frequency: if many people trip on the same thing, it's real regardless of how they explain it.
**Trade-offs**: Tails include cranks. Frequency alone would have you optimize for the median and sand off everything distinctive.

## Tweak, Don't Restart (Wright's law)
**When to use**: The work is underperforming and starting over feels attractive.
**How**: Pivot adjacent and ship again. Volume of iterations, not restarts, drives the improvement curve — roughly 10–15% better per doubling of cumulative output.
**Trade-offs**: Locks you into the current region of the space, so it only works after Surveying and Gridding were done honestly. Watch for the identity trap: refusing a tweak because the current version feels like you.

## AI as Excavator
**When to use**: Digging within a region you have already mapped.
**How**: Guide it to the specific gridded region, solicit many candidates, then curate with intent. Keep the human on Surveying, Gridding, and Sifting.
**Trade-offs**: Powerful for volume, but produces a homogenization effect across users. Use it for surface work (Digging); keep deep work — deciding where to search and what to keep — yours.
