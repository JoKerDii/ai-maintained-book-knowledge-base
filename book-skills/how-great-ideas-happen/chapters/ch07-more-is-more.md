# Chapter 7: More Is More

## Core Idea
The single most reliable predictor of creative success is volume: generate far more ideas than feels reasonable, and keep going past the point where you feel tapped out. You are not hunting *the* right idea — you are mining a promising region for a *set* of ideas, one of which will stick.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Creative Cliff Illusion** (Lucas & Nordgren): People predict their idea output will nosedive after ~2 minutes; in fact later minutes produce *more* ideas, and independent raters judge second-half ideas as *higher quality* than first-half ideas.
  - When to use: Any time you feel "I've exhausted this." Treat that feeling as a timestamp, not a verdict.
  - How: When the impulse to quit arrives, run a second round, then a third. Whatever number of ideas or amount of time seems reasonable, double or triple it.
  - Why it works: Two memory systems. Long-term memory is the library; working memory is your desk inside it. Your first ideas are just what you happened to grab off the nearest shelf — the most obvious, best-associated references. Judging "how many ideas exist" by glancing at that small stack systematically underestimates what you know. Deeper retrieval requires patience and systematic movement from one section to the next.
- **Look for a Set of Ideas (not the right idea)**: Creative search means finding a promising area and thoroughly mining its localized possibilities, hoping one variant "sticks."
  - When to use: The moment you find an idea you like — that's the signal to drill *down*, not to stop.
  - How: Take the promising idea and generate several near-neighbor variants of it. The winner is often adjacent to, not identical with, your first good idea.
  - Failure mode: Falling in love with the first workable solution and rushing to implement. You then select from a pool that is less innovative than the one you'd have had by continuing.
- **Narrative Reversals** (Samsun Knight): Points where a story's direction suddenly shifts. Analysis of ~30,000 shows/films/novels found more and larger reversals predicted better audience reception (IMDB ratings, book sales); in 1,000+ GoFundMe pitches, reversals raised funding probability by nearly 40%.
  - When to use: When ideas feel flat or expected — hunt for tension and counterintuition.
  - How: List your assumptions about the problem, then systematically ask "What if the opposite were true?"
- **Checklist for Effective Digging**: Prep Work (research, high-resolution Surveying, guiding question, constraints already done) · Dedicate Time and Space · Get Help (people and AI) · Evaluation-Free Zone · Don't Rush (days/weeks/months, not 15 minutes) · Record Everything (frictionlessly; no idea is bad) · Just Do It (you don't have to be at your best — tiredness encourages creative leaps).

## Key Concepts
- **Creative cliff illusion**: The false belief that idea productivity drops sharply after a short initial burst.
- **Long-term memory / working memory**: The library vs. your desk within it; ideas must be retrieved before they can be used.
- **Set of ideas**: A constellation of near-variants in one promising region, rather than a single candidate.
- **Narrative reversal**: A sudden shift in direction or fortune that captures attention by defying expectation.
- **Early-stopping / settling**: Choosing from an artificially small pool because search was abandoned prematurely.
- **The archaeological dig**: Idea generation as excavation — the first find is usually the surface of a larger deposit.
- **Age–creativity null result**: Across dozens of studies, no reliable decline in creativity with age; some show gains. Among 6,000+ film directors, breakthrough films were equally likely at the start, middle, or end of a career.

## Mental Models
- **Think of the urge to stop as the heating-up signal**: "Just when you think you've exhausted every idea, that's probably when your process is really heating up."
- **Use the excavation metaphor when planning a session**: constantly change the angle of your shovel — different people, group sizes, physical locations, personas ("what would my creative hero think of?").
- **Think of your first ideas as the nearest shelf**, not the whole library.
- **Use "it's never too late" as a working assumption**: whether within one session or across a lifetime, the greatest discovery may still be ahead.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rushing to implement the first workable solution**: Most people underinvest in ideation; you end up executing an average idea very well.
- **Evaluating while generating**: Criticism during Digging collapses volume and drives people to share only polished work.
- **Booking a single 15-minute brainstorm**: Digging unfolds over multiple sessions across days; one session never reaches past the cliff.
- **Stopping at the first find**: The initial discovery may be the top layer of a much larger deposit.

## Worked Example
Spencer Silver, a 3M chemist in 1968, was trying to make a superstrong aircraft adhesive and accidentally produced the opposite: a low-tack, residue-free, reusable adhesive. No existing product category fit it, and Silver couldn't name an application. For five years he brainstormed variants, running informal lunchtime talks pitching the adhesive across departments. A manager eventually backed one idea — an adhesive bulletin board where papers could be affixed and reaffixed. It failed. But at one of those talks, colleague Art Fry — who kept losing hymn notes in his church songbook — flipped the configuration: put the adhesive on the *paper*, not the board. That became "Press'n Peel" bookmarks. Those also failed. Silver and Fry kept digging through office-product variants and landed on Post-it Notes.

Newman's reading: this is not a grit story. Bulletin board, repositionable bookmark, and sticky note are all the same idea — paper + Silver's adhesive — explored as a *set*. The order was accidental; Silver could plausibly have hit the Post-it first if he'd stuck two papers together on day one. What Digging accomplished was exhausting a localized region of possibility until one variant resonated.

## Key Takeaways
1. Don't trust the felt sense of exhaustion — the creative cliff is an illusion, and late ideas rate higher than early ones.
2. Digging is a numbers game: whatever seems reasonable, double or triple it. 100–500 candidate names; a full stack of Post-its; multiple sessions over days.
3. When you find something you like, generate several tweaks of it rather than stopping — the winner is often the neighbor.
4. Systematically invert your assumptions; surprise and reversal are measurable drivers of audience response.
5. Separate generation from evaluation absolutely; make recording effortless and treat every idea as progress.
6. Change the shovel angle: new locations, new personas, new collaborators, new group sizes.
7. Creativity does not decline with age — never treat a late start as disqualifying.

## Exercises
- **Exercise 20: Post-it Note Challenge** — Write the guiding question; fill an entire 100-note book with ideas (no evaluation); break; categorize into at least three themes; then fill a *second* book over multiple days, adding to existing categories and inventing new ones. 200 ideas is the point: the high bar forces you past the obvious.
- **Exercise 21: Idea Tweaks** — Pick one promising idea and generate a constellation via: add an element, remove an element, swap it, expand it 10x, make it niche, invert it, shrink/blow up, make it for the "wrong" audience, time-travel it, break a rule, give it a personality, force a clash with something unrelated.
- **Exercise 22: Ideas from the Future** — Imagine you've already solved it; note how it feels and what it took; generate ideas from your future self's perspective; reflect on what the backward view surfaced.
- **Exercise 23: Bad Ideas** — Deliberately generate the worst possible ideas (to shorten meetings, first brainstorm how to lengthen them); examine why each fails; flip the worst ones into productive versions; harvest anything interesting.
