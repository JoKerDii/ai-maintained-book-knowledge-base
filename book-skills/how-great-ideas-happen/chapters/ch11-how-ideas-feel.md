# Chapter 11: How Ideas 'Feel'

## Core Idea
Breakthrough ideas feel unfamiliar and uncomfortable at first, so even expert creators discard their best material; meanwhile confidence and praise feel good and quietly push us to settle early. Discomfort is a signal to lean in, not a verdict.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Information Gap theory of curiosity** (George Loewenstein, applied to idea selection): Curiosity is not cognitive — it's a visceral state of discomfort arising from a gap between what you know and a glimmer of something you don't yet know. The urge to close that gap *is* curiosity.
  - When to use: When an idea feels vague, abstract, or "off" and you're tempted to drop it.
  - How: Treat the unease as evidence that you have a partial glimpse of an answer. Name what you sort-of-know, name the gap, and traverse it rather than abandoning the thread.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Curiosity is highest for questions you *sort of* know the answer to — near-zero for things you're certain about and near-zero for things you have no clue about. Failure mode: creators mistake the discomfort of a partially-understood good idea for the discomfort of a bad idea, and quit at the exact moment the signal is strongest (Justin Berg's finding: successful ideas initially feel foreign and abstract, so their originators systematically underrate them).
- **Great Ideas Feel Uncomfortable**: Familiarity breeds liking, so by definition a genuinely novel idea feels weird when it first occurs to you — including to Einstein.
  - When to use: As a triage rule during Sifting.
  - How: Rank your ideas by comfort. Deliberately develop the most uncomfortable one first.
  - Why it works: The recoil is a measure of distance from convention, not a measure of quality.
- **Five practical strategies for navigating idea-feelings**: *Give Every Idea a Shot* (develop before judging; dead ends still spawn better ideas) · *Attend to the Awkward Ones* (treat strangeness as opportunity) · *Be Wary of Brilliance* (praise the idea, never the genius) · *Embrace Curiosity, Not Closure* (ask "what if?" instead of rushing to solve; reopen settled questions late) · *Build a Feedback Network* (a few people who judge ideas on merit, separate from the person presenting).

## Key Concepts
- **The gap in understanding**: The space between current knowledge and a hunch of the answer; the engine of directed creative search.
- **Directed thinking**: Wertheimer's insight that Einstein's steps had a *direction* — an answer pulling him forward, not random steps pushing him.
- **The brick-counting fallacy**: Describing creation as an aggregate of logical operations tells you where every brick sits and nothing about how the building went up.
- **Getting warmer**: The felt sense that a marking, phrase, or arrangement is "right" — evidence that ideas are being found, not conjured.
- **Power as a creativity killer**: Feeling powerful raises certainty that your idea is best; you talk over people and lobby instead of listening (Hildreth & Anderson). Lower-power people take turns, consider perspectives, and select better ideas.
- **Praise as a creativity killer**: Praise attributed to talent/genius makes people avoid risk to protect the label — "if you're thought a genius, better to stay silent than speak and cast doubt."
- **Brilliance-focused culture**: Organizations that say they value genius signal a masculine culture (don't show emotion, don't ask for advice, don't admit mistakes), driving talent away.
- **Greater Male Variability Hypothesis (GMVH)**: The claim that men vary more in creative ability, hence more male extremes — minimal support in the creativity literature.

## Mental Models
- Think of discomfort as a compass, not a verdict: recoil marks the frontier of the familiar.
- Use lower-power framing when you want a group to pick the best idea; power buys advocacy, not accuracy.
- Think of praise for *talent* as a lock on risk-taking; praise the work, the choice, the effort.
- Use "what if?" as the default question in late-stage review — closure feels like progress and is often premature.

## Anti-patterns
- **Discarding an idea because it feels strange**: Sheeran nearly gave "Shape of You" to Rihanna; Screamin' Jay Hawkins forgot recording "I Put a Spell on You"; the White Stripes thought little of "Seven Nation Army."
- **Letting the most confident person drive selection**: Power produces jockeying and talking over others, not better picks.
- **Telling people (or yourself) "you're brilliant"**: Dweck's children praised as smart were *less than half as likely* to choose harder puzzles next round and *over three times as likely* to lie about their performance.
- **Framing team creative goals around genius/brilliance**: Excludes perspectives and deters people who won't work in a high-masculinity culture.
- **Treating market outcomes as proof of quality**: Attribution bias distorts perceived skill — identical paintings and identical business plans are rated more skillful/innovative when believed to be made by a man.

## Worked Example
Kara Walker, commissioned to fill the 100,000+ sq ft Domino Sugar Refinery in Brooklyn before its conversion to condos. She had a cloud of associations — sugar, brown sugar, Black female sexuality, the slave trade, ruins, a refinery explosion she'd witnessed as a child — that refused to cohere. That non-coherence is the information gap: enough of a glimpse to feel the pull, not enough to resolve it. She stayed in it and kept *researching* rather than switching projects. On the Q train, reading Sidney Mintz's history of sugar, she hit a passage on "subtleties" — ornamental sugar sculptures made for kings. The gap closed in one move: it's a sugar sculpture, and the Sphinx is the perfect ruin. The result was *A Subtlety*, a 75-foot-long, 35-foot-high sphinx made entirely of sugar. Adam Moss's observation is the point: Walker approached it "like a research project" — methodical, not pure instinct.

Same shape, different domain: Einstein's route to Special Relativity ran chase-a-light-beam → how would anyone know they're at rest? → a puzzling experimental result → *what does it mean to say two events happened at the same time?* (a semantic question that plagued him for weeks) → relativity, invariant light speed, space-time. Wertheimer's question — why *that* next step each time? — implies an answer drawing him forward through sustained discomfort.

## Key Takeaways
1. Rank ideas by how uncomfortable they make you and develop the most uncomfortable one first.
2. Read vagueness and abstraction as an information gap to close, not as a defect to escape.
3. Give every idea enough development to see its logical conclusions before judging it.
4. Praise ideas, never inner genius — yours or anyone's; genius framing suppresses risk-taking and diversity.
5. Watch for power and praise as the failure mode on the confident side: they make you settle at "good."
6. Run **Exercise 33 (Discomfort into Curiosity)**: identify discomfort sources → describe why and where you feel them → convert each into a question or story → repeat.
7. Run **Exercise 34 (Embrace the Discomfort)**: rank Digging-phase ideas from most to least comfortable → spend 10–15 minutes developing the most uncomfortable → discuss with a friend and watch their initial reaction → repeat with the next one.
