# Chapter 12: The Learning Curve

## Core Idea
Breakthroughs come from carefully calibrated tweaks to a preserved idea, not from dramatic restarts — and you only learn from failure if enough stays constant between attempts. That requires deliberately engineered feedback: a plan, better questions, and a rule for which criticism to act on.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Wright's Law (learning by doing)**: Theodore Wright, 1930s aerospace — for every doubling of the number of airplanes produced, labor per airplane falls roughly **10–15%**. Graph effort against repetitions and you get the downward-sloping learning curve.
  - When to use: Deciding whether to pivot or restart after a failure.
  - How: Keep the substrate constant and change one layer. A study of tens of thousands of start-ups found the most successful entrepreneurs are readily identified by adherence to Wright's law — incremental "learning from mistakes" improvement rather than serial abandonment.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Learning requires continuity; if you change everything after each critique you reset the curve to zero and merely roll the dice again. Newman's own diagnosis of why he improved at running (clear goal, obvious levers, low identity stakes, visible what-worked) but not at art (vague goal, holistic feedback, identity fused to output, so total pivots felt safer than investigation).
- **The Power of Tweaks**: In the face of rejection, pivot to something *adjacent* — preserve the accumulated work, change the specific thing that failed.
  - When to use: After a hard "no" that seems to invalidate the whole project.
  - How: Extract from the feedback precisely what wasn't working; hold everything else.
- **The Feedback Plan (four moves)**: 1) **Purpose of the Feedback** — decide what you actually want (kill/continue verdict, encouragement, targeted critique) *before* asking, and choose the person to match; don't ask a known critic for reassurance. 2) **Provide Context** — state your guiding question, what the idea is, why it should exist, and which areas you need help with. 3) **Ask Better Questions** — replace "Do you like this?" with "What specific aspects are strong, and which could be improved?", "What is one thing that could make this idea even better?", "Does this remind you of other things you've seen?" 4) **Decide in advance what you'll do with it** — including what you will *not* change no matter what.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Vague questions get "Oh, fine" (the restaurant manager problem). Failure mode: responding to every comment creates confusion, not clarity, and zaps motivation — you cannot listen to everyone.
- **The Bell Curve of Feedback**: Reactions are normally distributed — a few love it, a few hate it, most are in the middle.
  - When to use: Planning who to ask, when to ask, and what to act on.
  - How: (a) Weight the extremes — middle responders have weak opinions and social pressure to soften, so the best information often comes from the tails. (b) Expect negative feedback as inevitable, not as evidence of error. (c) Don't seek feedback too early — the haters exist regardless, and a tenuous project can't absorb them. (d) Widening to a group widens the curve: more people generate *entirely new* critiques, not more of the same.

## Key Concepts
- **Learning vs. rolling the dice**: Incremental refinement of a stable idea vs. wholesale substitution after each failure.
- **The identity trap**: When your ego is fused to the work, abandoning it feels safer than investigating what failed.
- **The cilantro frame**: Some people have a natural aversion; that says nothing about the thing's quality. Removes blame and depersonalizes criticism.
- **Frequency rule**: The critiques that recur across independent people are the ones to fix.
- **Outlier-comment capture**: Getting numb to the common complaint while obsessing over one memorable oddball ("your protagonist should be a talking penguin").
- **Rejection as information**: Learning where good ideas are *not* is as useful as learning where they are.
- **The archaeology metaphor** (Andrew Stanton, via Catmull): You're digging blind for an unknown dinosaur; once you glimpse part of it, you know better how to dig.
- **Blind spots**: Criticisms you're dismissing specifically because they challenge your assumptions.

## Mental Models
- Think of your idea like cilantro: a hater is a taste distribution, not a verdict on you.
- Use tweaks when the feedback names a specific failing part; use a restart only when the guiding question itself is wrong.
- Think of group feedback as widening the variance, not deepening the consensus — plan for breadth.
- Use frequency as your triage signal: fix the undercooked pasta fifty people mentioned, not the loud music one person mentioned.

## Anti-patterns
- **Abandoning the whole idea at each failed critique**: Newman's art practice — every pivot produced an entirely new set of problems and zero cumulative learning.
- **Asking "How was everything?"**: Guarantees "Oh, fine." Ask specific, comparative, or forced-choice questions instead.
- **Seeking feedback before the idea can stand**: The bell curve's tail exists at every stage; premature exposure just demoralizes.
- **Trying to satisfy every critique**: Fix the big issues, then stop — new fixes just breed new haters. Past that point, strengthen what's working.
- **Over-weighting the memorable outlier**: Common critiques feel threatening precisely because they read as definitively true; that's why they matter most.
- **Asking for feedback when you're not willing to change anything**: Then feedback isn't what you want — be honest about that.

## Worked Example
Pixar, 1993. Riding on shorts like *Luxo Jr.* but not profitable, the company lands a Disney development deal for the first fully computer-animated feature. Disney executives push for "edgier" to differentiate it from traditional animation. Pixar duly pitches *Toy Story* told from the perspective of Woody as a sarcastic, tyrannical ventriloquist's-dummy character who throws the other toys around and calls them names. Disney hates it, doubts computer animation can carry a feature at all, and shuts the project down — the "Black Friday" incident. Catmull describes it as terrifying; the company's future was in question.

The decision point: scramble for something entirely new (roll the dice) or tweak (stay on the curve). Pixar read the feedback narrowly — the *tone and Woody's character* were the failing part, not the medium, the premise, or the toy-world architecture. They kept everything else and pivoted to an adjacent idea: a buddy movie between a warm, charismatic cowboy doll and Buzz Lightyear, lighter in tone, which as a bonus opened up the aging/irrelevance themes. Disney greenlit it; *Toy Story* broke box office records and proved CG could carry narrative. All the up-front work survived because only one layer changed.

Scale check on how much gets cut in normal practice: Dua Lipa wrote 97 songs for *Radical Optimism*; 11 made the album.

## Key Takeaways
1. Default to a tweak; a total restart erases your learning curve and your accumulated work.
2. Keep something constant between attempts, or you aren't learning — you're sampling.
3. Decide the *purpose* of feedback before requesting it, and pick the person to match that purpose.
4. Give context — guiding question, what the idea is, why it should exist — or you'll get reactions to the wrong thing.
5. Replace liking questions with specific, comparative, and constructive ones.
6. Act on the most *frequent* criticism; discount the memorable outlier.
