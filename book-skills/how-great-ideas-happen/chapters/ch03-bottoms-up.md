# Chapter 3: Bottoms Up!

## Core Idea
When people search for ideas, their prior expectations (top-down processing) quietly dictate where they look, and they end up with "ferrets on steroids" — something they've mislabeled because they expected it. Breakthroughs come from letting raw observation guide you bottom-up: finding problems rather than solving given ones, looking closely, and attending to what isn't there.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Problem finding**: Treating the *identification* of a creative problem, not its solution, as the decisive skill — approaching a task without preconceived notions and letting the problem emerge from the situation.
  - When to use: At the outset of any creative venture, especially when a brief already seems well-defined. If you can already tell whether you're getting closer to a solution, the problem was handed to you and the leverage is gone.
  - How: Delay committing to an approach. Physically explore and manipulate the available material. Attend to what emerges (including negative space) rather than executing a preformed image. Only then let a problem crystallize.
  - Why it works: Csikszentmihalyi and Getzels found problem finding at the *very beginning* of a task predicted artistic career success 7 and 18 years later — while drive, ambition, confidence, philosophical sophistication, and appeals to "beauty/order/harmony" predicted nothing.
- **Close looking**: The art-historical practice of deliberate, sustained, detailed examination of one object — thirty minutes to an hour on a single painting — until first-glance labels dissolve and real detail emerges.
  - When to use: Any time your "inner explainer" has already summarized a situation ("there's a house, there's a woman, let's move on"). Transfers directly to meetings, customer behavior, team dynamics, and your own routines.
  - How: Pick one thing. Stay past the boredom, the way you would in meditation. Let new details surface, draw connections between elements, and form theories about why the choices were made. Start at ten minutes on something mundane.
  - Why it works: It defeats the simplified representation. Newman's executives, after an hour of museum close looking, began scrutinizing their own meetings and behaviors and saw what their actions actually communicated — the museum wasn't the point; the transfer was.
- **Knowledge funnel**: Know a lot about one or two things and a little about a lot of things — and be highly adept at translating the far-afield ideas back into the language of your own expertise.
  - When to use: As the standing structure of your learning; it resolves the apparent conflict between expertise (needed to recognize a promising idea) and fresh eyes (needed to see what's really there).
  - How: Dive deep enough in your domain to know what a successful idea looks like there. Keep peripheral vision wide — philosophy for the artist, other genres for the musician, distant fields for the scientist. Then do the actual work: ask what a foreign concept implies for a problem you're already holding.
- **Attending to negative space**: Deliberately examining absences, gaps, and silences rather than objects.
  - When to use: When a domain looks exhausted. Opportunity often sits in the negative space between existing ideas.
  - How: Exercises 9 and 11 — track pauses and what goes unsaid in a conversation, then form and test a theory about them; photograph one static scene from ten vantage points, then isolate and compare the shapes made by the negative spaces.

## Key Concepts
- **Bottom-up processing**: Building a representation from the sensory data actually arriving in the moment.
- **Top-down processing**: Prior knowledge, context, and expectation shaping what you perceive.
- **Ferrets on steroids**: Newman's label for what you produce when top-down beliefs dictate your search — the buyers in Buenos Aires (2012) took home groomed, steroid-fed ferrets sold in dog cages and advertised as toy poodles, and perceived poodles.
- **Digital anthropology**: Joanna Griffiths' term for immersing in online forums and chat rooms and listening until people feel safe discussing intimate needs — bottom-up market research.
- **The inner explainer**: The voice that labels a scene and declares it understood, terminating observation prematurely.
- **Low- vs. high-resolution knowledge**: The two arms of the knowledge funnel — breadth for far-afield material, depth for judging promise.
- **Drive over talent**: Alice Flaherty's conclusion — the neurological correlates of creativity (temporal lobe epilepsy, bipolar disorder) affect emotion and drive, not some sealed wellspring of ideas; they raise the search intensity, which is what produces output.
- **Hypergraphia**: The overwhelming compulsion to write; Flaherty's own four-month episode after the death of her twin sons became the seed of *The Midnight Disease*.

## Mental Models
- Think of top-down expectation as the thing that sells you a ferret — assume it's active, and design against it.
- Use close looking when a situation feels already-understood; the feeling of "I get it" is the signal to slow down, not move on.
- Think of a domain's unmet needs as negative space: your brain tracks the flying ball, not the air around it, so you must look for absence deliberately.
- Use expertise for *judging* ideas and fresh eyes for *finding* them — learn extensively, then set the inner explainer aside when you're in the field.

## Anti-patterns
- **Arriving with a preconceived approach**: the low-scoring artist who said "I knew right away I was going to make a drawing of a startling vanishing point" — the fast commitment is what forecloses discovery.
- **Letting others' expectations define the market**: Griffiths was told leakproof underwear was "too niche" for a need half the population has.
- **Treating expertise as pure asset**: unmanaged prior knowledge seeps in and distorts perception at exactly the moment you're searching.
- **Confusing drive/ambition talk with creative promise**: it predicted nothing about which artists succeeded.

## Worked Example
The Csikszentmihalyi and Getzels study, run in the 1960s, is the chapter's spine. Thirty-one visual artists from the Art Institute of Chicago each entered a studio with two tables — one holding still-life objects (a leather-bound book, a car gearshift, a velvet hat, a prism), the other holding paper, pencils, and charcoal. Instructions were minimal: choose objects, arrange them however you like, make a drawing. Unlimited time, permission to restart, stop when satisfied. Most artists reported it felt like an ordinary working session.

Researchers logged everything: how many and which objects were selected, how unusual they were, time spent inspecting before choosing, time spent arranging. Afterward, interviews of up to four hours reconstructed the artists' thinking — what they attended to, what they ignored, how they judged the result. Responses were coded onto a quantitative problem-finding continuum: low scores for artists who arrived with a fixed image ("a startling vanishing point"), high scores for those who played with the objects and let a problem emerge (one noticed the shapes formed by negative spaces *between* objects and worked from there).

Then they waited. In 1970 — seven years out — they interviewed critics, gallery owners, and artists to score each participant's success on a 10-point scale. About half had stopped making art or were untraceable; seven were working with some success; nine were represented by leading Chicago and New York galleries, one with a museum sale. They repeated the assessment in 1981, eighteen years out, by which point some were internationally recognized stars.

Problem finding at the very beginning of the task was the predictor. Not ambition, not confidence, not articulated philosophy, not invocations of beauty or harmony. And the behavioral record backed the interviews: successful artists had physically explored and manipulated the objects far more. Notably, no visual property of the drawings — skill, composition — was factored in at all. How a student approached one drawing session forecast a career two decades out.

## Key Takeaways
1. Assume your search is being steered top-down. The correction is deliberate bottom-up observation, not more effort.
2. Spend your leverage on finding the problem; a well-defined problem has already given away most of its value.
3. Don't commit to an approach at the start of a creative task — manipulate the material and let the problem emerge.
4. Practice close looking on something mundane for ten minutes: how your team reacts to a new idea, how customers move through a store, your own morning routine.
5. Hunt negative space — the gap, the silence, the absence. Fleming's non-growing spot became penicillin; Cage's 4'33" made silence audible.
6. Do digital anthropology: go where your users already talk to each other and listen until they say something intimate.
7. Build a knowledge funnel — deep in one or two areas, wide elsewhere, and practiced at translating foreign concepts into your own domain's language.
8. Carry a notebook for a week logging every friction and inefficiency you hit (Exercise 10), then mine it for recurring themes.
