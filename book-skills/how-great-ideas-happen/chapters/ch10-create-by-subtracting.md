# Chapter 10: Create by Subtracting

## Core Idea
At the Sifting stage the gains come from what you remove, not what you add — but people reliably "improve" work by adding, and they overvalue ideas merely because they authored them. Effective sifting means engineering psychological distance so you can throw your own material away.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Creative Endowment Effect**: We overvalue our own ideas simply because we thought of them; the same idea proposed by someone else would be discarded without hesitation.
  - When to use: Any moment you're culling a list of your own ideas, drafts, slides, features, or scenes.
  - How: (a) hand your raw list to someone else and have them pick the keepers; (b) shelve the list for weeks-to-months and re-select with fresh eyes; (c) compare their picks vs. yours and interrogate the deltas.
  - Why it works / failure mode: It's an ownership bias, not a knowledge deficit — de-biasing by *information* fails. Newman found that showing people better examples, or explicitly telling them the effect exists, does not improve calibration. Only distance (other people, or time) moves the needle.
- **Building a Tower vs. Floating a Raft**: Creators default to tower mode — cram in every study, anecdote, and flourish to display total effort. Audiences are permanently in raft-inspection mode — hunting the holes that will sink it.
  - When to use: Preparing any talk, pitch, exhibition, manuscript, or proposal for an audience.
  - How: Evaluate your own work as a raft-inspector would. Ask of each element: does this keep the thing watertight, or is it ballast added to look impressive?
  - Why it works / failure mode: The same person flips modes depending on ownership — analytical when judging others, accumulative when judging themselves. Failure mode: "all the flash in the world" cannot offset one under-thought section; the weakest element sets the perceived quality.
- **Write Out Your Guiding Question** (the real-time sifting test): Distance and a critical eye are useless unless you know what you're looking for.
  - When to use: Whenever you can't get outside input or wait months.
  - How: 1) Write out your guiding question (Ch 4). 2) Write a summary of each element of your idea and its contribution — how does it add meaning, what problem does it solve, what question does it answer? 3) Compare the two documents. 4) Cut every element that doesn't serve the guiding question; save it for another project.
- **The Mid-Stakes Event**: A scheduled presentation/performance with a real audience and a real date, pitched between a low-stakes chat (too soft to force scrutiny) and a high-stakes gatekeeper moment (too late — sifting must already be done).
  - When to use: When you need pressure to force selection but can't afford the outcome to matter.
  - How: Pick the event (guest lecture, group show, internal cross-department pitch, reading, bigger-than-usual gig). Set a challenging date. Define an audience whose feedback is valuable but who are not career gatekeepers. Anticipate their reactions while preparing. Afterward, log where your predictions were right and wrong.
  - Why it works: The value is entirely in the *preparation*, not the outcome — a deadline forces deeper digging, and a known audience pulls you into raft-inspection mode.

## Key Concepts
- **Subtraction neglect**: The documented tendency (Gabrielle Adams et al., UVA) to edit text, designs, and Lego structures only by adding, almost never by removing.
- **Psychological distance**: Space from your own work — created by time or by other people — that restores the ability to judge it.
- **Sifting with others**: Handing ideas off, which raises scrutiny, exposes weak points, and surfaces subtraction opportunities.
- **The time-delay effect**: Newman's participants revisiting their own idea lists months later became significantly more selective.
- **Grumpy Yoda**: An imagined high-standards expert you know personally, mentally set to maximum grumpiness, used to generate the criticisms you can't generate yourself.
- **Low- / mid- / high-stakes events**: The three pressure levels of showing work; only the middle one reliably improves the work.
- **Tangent inertia**: Beautiful material disconnected from the core thesis pulls the project toward dead ends because it has its own momentum.

## Mental Models
- Use raft-inspection mode when reviewing your own work; tower mode is what your ego wants and what your audience punishes.
- Think of your idea list as someone else's property — that's the only frame in which you'll cut properly.
- Use a scheduled mid-stakes event as a forcing function whenever an idea is drifting without a deadline.
- Think of a subtraction as a save, not a loss: removed pieces go into inventory for another project.

## Anti-patterns
- **Trying to de-bias yourself with facts**: Knowing about the creative endowment effect doesn't cure it — only distance does.
- **Submitting everything good you have**: In Newman's competition, *not one* participant won money; good ideas were consistently dragged under by the subpar ones submitted alongside.
- **Building everything around one great line**: David Sedaris's warning — protecting a favorite joke or sentence leads to a dead end; go find another laugh.
- **Sifting during a high-stakes event**: By then you should be polishing, not still discovering what the idea is.
- **Assuming improvement means addition**: Most editing instincts add material to work that needed cutting.

## Worked Example
Paul Simon, 1984: post-divorce, two commercial flops, stuck. A friend hands him an unmarked cassette of South African street music (a broker moment). He drives it on loop all summer, then goes to Johannesburg, is connected by a local producer to the musicians, and books two weeks of essentially improvisational studio jamming — pure Digging, inverting his usual method (tracks preceded the songs rather than songs preceding tracks). He returns with troves of raw material and spends a full year editing it — so extensively that the team had to pioneer a new digital audio workstation. The decisive move: throughout that year Simon was not asking what he could add, but what he could take away. The result was *Graceland*.

Newman's contrasting lab case: participants generated charitable-fundraising ideas, then selected only their best for a competition paying cash for good ideas and penalizing bad ones. Zero participants won. They submitted genuinely good ideas plus enough mediocre ones to wipe out the gains. A second group, handed the *first group's* lists, submitted significantly fewer ideas, rated significantly better. Same material, different owner — that's the creative endowment effect isolated.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask "what can I remove?" before "what can I add?" — subtraction is the underused lever.
2. You cannot reason your way out of over-valuing your own ideas; outsource the cut or delay it.
3. Run **Exercise 29 (Overcoming the Creative Endowment Effect)**: list ideas → have a friend pick → compare and discuss → revisit months later → merge both insights.
4. Run **Exercise 30 (Floating a Raft)**: state the core idea → exhaustively list elements → test each against "does this directly support the core idea?" → cut → present the stripped version.
5. Run **Exercise 31 (Grumpy Yoda's Critique)**: pick a real high-standards expert, imagine them grumpy, list every criticism, revise against each. Tough on ideas, easy on yourself.
6. Run **Exercise 32 (A Mid-Stakes Event)**: choose event → set date and audience → predict reactions while preparing → afterward audit prediction accuracy. The prediction audit is the gold.
7. One under-supported element sinks the whole raft — quality is set by the weakest visible part, not the total volume of work shown.
