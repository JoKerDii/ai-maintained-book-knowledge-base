# Chapter 8: Search Far and Wide

## Core Idea
Sustained creative success is predicted less by what you do after a breakthrough than by how broadly you experimented — in styles and in social connections — *before* it. Range and weak ties expand the terrain you can dig in; retreating into your close circle when things get hard is exactly backwards.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Early Diversification** (Justin Berg): Experimenting across many styles/domains *before* your breakthrough is the key predictor of having more than one hit.
  - When to use: Early career, between projects, or whenever you're tempted to specialize prematurely.
  - How: Deliberately work in adjacent genres, disciplines, formats, or problem types before committing. Treat variety as investment, not distraction.
  - Why it works: Three mechanisms — (1) a Swiss Army knife portfolio of skills you can pivot to when your original style falls out of favor; (2) general, transferable expertise (writing in multiple genres teaches songwriting *as such*); (3) better matching — you're more likely to find the thing that clicks with your actual talents.
- **The Strength of Weak Ties** (Mark Granovetter): Strong ties = frequent interaction, high trust, reciprocity, and high similarity to you. Weak ties = acquaintances and friends of friends, who hold different information and networks.
  - When to use: Whenever you need new *directions*, not new *hands* — idea generation, unsticking, finding non-obvious framings.
  - How: Inventory your network, classify strong vs. weak, and deliberately route early-stage project conversations through weak ties.
  - Why it works: A strong tie digs beside you in the same hole — same experiences, same media, same schools, same reactions. The point of Digging is ground covered, so partner with someone who sees differently. Validated at scale: teams connected by weak ties produced more impactful work across 5 million scientific papers; breakthrough ideas came more often from teams spanning diverse departments.
- **Opening Your Network Early** (Lee & Gargiulo, K-pop songwriter dataset): Hitmakers "opened" their networks faster than peers — aggressively connecting to distant songwriters *before* their breakthrough, so it wasn't a byproduct of fame.
- **Collective Intelligence** (Carnegie Mellon / MIT): A team's creative performance is not the sum of member IQs; a single factor — the group's ability to work together — explained nearly half of team success. Its two drivers: emotional intelligence (reading and responding to social cues) and turn-taking (actively soliciting from everyone rather than being dominated by one or two voices). A third predictor, number of women in the group, was explained by those two factors.
- **Practical Digging Protocols for Groups**: Make Digging evaluation-free (say it out loud before starting, reinforce it throughout) · Separate Digging from Sifting (target quantity, vary location and grouping) · Formalize turn-taking (randomize order with cards or an RNG) · Make submissions anonymous · Purposely generate bad ideas · Let someone else lead (junior or quieter people run the session) · Use "yes, and" (everyone must build on someone else's contribution before pitching their own).

## Key Concepts
- **Early diversification**: Broad experimentation across styles/domains before a breakthrough, which predicts sustained rather than one-hit success.
- **One-hit wonder curse**: Caused by narrow pre-breakthrough experimentation, not post-fame pressure or luck.
- **Strong tie / weak tie**: Frequent, trusted, similar contacts vs. acquaintances who carry non-redundant information.
- **Turtling-up**: The documented tendency (from millions of LinkedIn messages during downturns) to message only your immediate network in times of distress — emotionally sensible, creatively counterproductive.
- **Collective intelligence**: The group-level factor, driven by emotional intelligence and turn-taking, that predicts team creative performance.
- **"Yes, and"**: Improv rule requiring each contribution to accept and extend a prior one.
- **Visual drifting**: Letting the eyes wander to environmental stimuli — the mechanism behind in-person creativity advantage.
- **Evaluation-free zone**: An explicitly declared period in which no idea may be judged.

## Mental Models
- **Think of a strong tie as an extra set of hands in your hole, and a weak tie as a second hole.** Speed vs. ground covered.
- **Use early diversification as career portfolio theory**: range bought early is optionality you can exercise when tastes shift.
- **When blocked, face outward, not inward**: a close friend offers a sympathetic shoulder; the next breakthrough comes from a friend of a friend.
- **Think of virtual meetings as impoverished, not doomed**: the deficit is missing peripheral stimuli, and it's fixable.

## Anti-patterns
- **Turtling-up under stress**: Shrinking your network when blocked cuts off exactly the non-redundant information you need.
- **Senior people critiquing during a brainstorm**: In Newman's lab meeting, professors' inability to suspend judgment stopped students from sharing anything unpolished — they resorted to a squirt gun. Critique is invaluable at Sifting, corrosive at Digging.
- **Letting the smartest person carry the room**: Even an exceptionally intelligent member didn't guarantee better team output.
- **Unstructured turn-taking**: Introverts burn mental energy computing when to speak instead of engaging with ideas.
- **Staring fixedly at the screen on video calls**: Politeness locks your gaze, which locks your brainstorming.

## Worked Example
Björk's *Debut* (1993) reads as a breakout: chart success, a million copies, then nine more studio albums with eight consecutively in the Billboard Top 20. But it wasn't a debut. She recorded her first album at eleven, formed the punk group Spit and Snot as a teenager, played jazz fusion in Exodus and Jam-80 at fifteen, drummed in art-rock band Rokka Rokka Drum, fronted post-punk acts Tappi Tíkarrass and Kukl, then broke internationally with the Sugarcubes — and several *Debut* songs were written during those earlier bands. Janet Jackson shows the same shape: *Control* was her third album, preceded by experiments across pop, dance, R&B, soul, and funk, giving her the versatility behind 42 subsequent Top 100 hits.

Berg's data confirms the pattern isn't anecdotal: analyzing 3 million songs by ~70,000 artists, the predictor of multiple hits wasn't timing, genre, or label — it was stylistic variety *before* the breakthrough. And the pattern generalizes: Olympic medalists played more sports as children and specialized less than non-medaling elite athletes; Nobel laureates were more likely than comparably accomplished researchers to have worked across disciplines early. Critically, diversification is social — nobody wakes up deciding to try jazz fusion; it comes from crossing paths with other musicians, a coffee conversation, a workshop. Breadth of experimentation tracks breadth of connections.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diversify early and broadly; the payoff is sustained success, not just the first hit.
2. Open your network *before* you need it — hitmakers connected to distant collaborators pre-breakthrough.
3. Route early-stage thinking through weak ties; strong ties duplicate your information.
4. When blocked, expand your network rather than shrinking it — resist turtling-up.
5. Engineer collective intelligence directly: formalize turn-taking, randomize speaking order, go anonymous, hand leadership to quieter people.
6. Declare the evaluation-free zone explicitly and enforce it; critique belongs to Sifting.
7. On video calls, deliberately let your eyes wander and put something strange in your field of view.

## Exercises
- **Exercise 24: King of the Mountain** — Team of 2–4, ideally outside your immediate circle. Post the guiding question visibly. Round 1: everyone generates independently on Post-its, then each posts their *top two*; remove duplicates; discuss and **anonymously vote** three ideas to the top of the mountain (no single person decides). Round 2: repeat, voting across ideas from both rounds. Photograph every stage, keep all ideas, and leave time to discuss the ones that were generated but never posted.
- **Exercise 25: Engaging Your Weak Ties** — List everyone in your professional and personal networks; classify each as strong or weak; scan the weak-tie list for anyone with interesting connections to your project; then set up an informal conversation where you explain only the project and guiding question, say nothing more, and wait for their unprimed first thoughts.
