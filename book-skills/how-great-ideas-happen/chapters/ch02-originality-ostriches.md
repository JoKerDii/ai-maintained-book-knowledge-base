# Chapter 2: Originality Ostriches

## Core Idea
Creators systematically overweight novelty and are blind to the cost — the more original they try to be, the less anyone wants what they made. The reliable Surveying move is the opposite: start where success has already clustered, imitate deeply, and add only a thin layer of novelty.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 5% Novelty Rule**: The highest-impact work sits at roughly 90–95% conventional and 5–10% novel. Breakthroughs come from starting with a proven idea and carefully reworking a small part of it — remixing a song, not composing from scratch.
  - When to use: Whenever you're scoping a project and feel pressure to do something never done before; also when evaluating whether an idea is too far out to land.
  - How: (1) Pick an existing, celebrated work or approach in your domain. (2) Reproduce it faithfully enough to understand its mechanics. (3) Enumerate many candidate ~5% modifications. (4) Implement one at a time and observe how the character of the whole shifts. (Exercise 7, "The 5% Workshop," is exactly this: copy the work verbatim, brainstorm ten 5% changes, try your favorite, then try others.)
  - Why it works: Uzzi's analysis of 18 million scientific papers scored each paper's novelty by how far its references spanned fields. The biggest hits were neither the most conventional nor the most novel — they clustered tightly in the 5–10% novel band. Failure mode: mistaking process for outcome. The *outcome* can be epic (Abloh, Dylan); the *process* is a slow, incremental crawl out of the known.
- **High-resolution Surveying**: Gathering exhaustive, detailed information about a very narrow topic — one person, or one stretch of one person's career — by trying to re-create their work.
  - When to use: When you want to actually learn from a hero rather than admire them; when your work feels shapeless and you need a structural theory to steal.
  - How: Choose someone who makes you think *I wish I had done that*. Retrace their path chronologically. Attempt reproduction and notice the decisions you're forced to make (line weight, sequence, proportion). Log points of friction — the places your copy refuses to match are where your own voice is surfacing.
  - Why it works: Imitation is a sense-making act, not mechanical copying. Lyons and Keil's toddler experiments showed children copy even unnecessary steps of a demonstrated procedure, but when offered a cumbersome vs. a direct route they choose efficiency — they're reconstructing *why*, not just *what*. Imitating a creator forces you to build a theory of their decisions.
- **Interview Your Hero** (Exercise 8): Research a hero via interviews and biographies, prepare questions on career choices, failures, and how they judged an idea worth pursuing, then write out their answers in their voice as a documented transcript.

## Key Concepts
- **Originality Ostrich effect**: Creators believe originality can only help their work while audiences penalize it — a blind spot, not a tradeoff they've chosen.
- **Utility**: The value side of the novelty/value pair — why anyone is drawn to a work at all (nourishes, entertains, expands knowledge, stirs emotion, connects people, solves a practical need). Not money.
- **Second-mover advantage**: The breakthrough usually goes to a slight tweak of an existing idea, not the first version (CouchSurfing 2004 → Airbnb 2008; Rhapsody 2001 / Last.fm 2002 → Spotify 2006; wallpaper putty → Play-Doh).
- **The 3% approach**: Virgil Abloh's "cheat code" — take a celebrated existing design and change it by 3%. "It's like hip-hop. It's sampling."
- **Imitatio**: The Renaissance practice, per Raphael Falco, of finding external models, imitating them, and transforming the imitation — the bee digesting a field of flowers into honey that is part flower, part bee. Dylan built "A Hard Rain's A-Gonna Fall" on the Old English ballad "Lord Randal."
- **Low-resolution Surveying**: Surface-level knowledge of a wide range of topics — useful, but not what unlocks imitation's learning value.
- **Our wonderful flaw** (Kleon): Humans cannot make perfect copies; the failure to copy your heroes is precisely where your own thing lives.
- **Finds cluster**: As in archaeology, past success is one of the strongest signals about where other promising ideas are buried.

## Mental Models
- Use "where have successful ideas been found before?" as your search prompt, instead of "what has never been done?"
- Think of imitation as a mechanic disassembling a car — the point is comprehension, not the copy.
- Use friction while copying as your originality detector: where the imitation resists you is where your perspective is emerging.
- Think of novelty as garnish on a proven dish — indispensable, but not the meal.

## Anti-patterns
- **Optimizing for "unlike anything anyone has seen"**: produces the Bros. restaurant — twenty-seven courses including an oyster loaf tasting of Newark airport, twelve foams, flavored paper, and dessert licked from a plaster cast of the chef's mouth. Novelty purchased at the cost of value.
- **Solving a problem no one has**: Homer Simpson's Everything's Okay Alarm — genuinely novel, zero utility.
- **Announcing your originality**: on *Top Chef*, contestants who explicitly said they aimed to make something original and different were more than twice as likely to have the worst-rated dish and be eliminated.
- **Avoiding influence to stay "pure"**: forecloses the only reliable learning mechanism and leaves you with no springboard.

## Worked Example
Newman's own study with graduate student Jin Kim. Participants — "chefs" — were asked to invent a novel sandwich recipe, a domain where nearly everyone considers themselves competent. Cash prizes went to the most-liked recipes, so everyone was incentivized toward appeal. Each chef reported how much they prioritized originality versus taste. Chefs uniformly believed originality could only help: the more novel, the more attractive.

A second, separate group — "customers" — then rated how likely they'd be to try each recipe. The result inverted the chefs' prediction: willingness to buy fell as chef-side originality focus rose. Because the chefs were blind to the damage, Newman named it the Originality Ostrich effect.

The replication in the wild: research assistants coded multiple seasons of *Top Chef*, marking for each contestant whether they described sticking to an established recipe or explicitly reaching for creativity/originality. Same pattern, sharper — explicit originality focus more than doubled the odds of the worst dish and elimination. Amateurs and professionals both.

The constructive mirror is Mondrian. Over forty years his work splits into two visible halves: two decades of exploration — emulating Dutch masters with representational landscapes, then Post-Impressionism via Seurat and Cézanne, then treelike forms and geometric grids, each style bleeding into the next — and then, around 1920, convergence into the signature primary-color grid, which he iterated obsessively for the rest of his life. It wasn't money or fame (his first US solo show came in 1942, at nearly seventy). Later computational analysis found true Mondrian grids differ from lookalike replicas by a mathematically precise balancing of visual weight: small patches of dark blue and black offset by larger patches of light red and yellow. Years of imitation had let him excavate a real principle about how brains process visual balance.

## Key Takeaways
1. Assume you are an Originality Ostrich — your originality bias is invisible to you and visible to your audience.
2. Aim for 90–95% conventional, 5–10% novel. Abloh's version is 3%.
3. Search where success has already clustered; past success is your best signal of a rich site.
4. Pick a hero, copy the work exactly, then brainstorm ten 5% changes and try them one at a time.
5. Do high-resolution Surveying — exhaustive depth on one narrow subject — rather than skimming widely, when the goal is learning.
6. Judge every idea on utility as well as novelty: who does it nourish, entertain, move, connect, or serve?
7. Don't fear derivativeness. You are physically incapable of a perfect copy, and the gap is your voice.
