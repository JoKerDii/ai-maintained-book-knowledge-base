# Patterns — Techniques from The Certainty Illusion

## The Certainty Six
**When to use**: As a standing checklist for any consequential claim you encounter or are about to share.
**How**: (1) Pause and recognize the information ecosystem isn't rigged for accuracy — ask "is this accurate?" before sharing. (2) Watch for scienceploitation. (3) Resist the allure of science hype. (4) Take a closer look when goodness is used as a marketing strategy. (5) Always consider the existing body of evidence. (6) Like what you like.
**Trade-offs**: Deliberately low-effort by design, so it will not catch sophisticated fraud. It is a filter for the 90% of everyday claims, not an audit.

## The Accuracy Pause
**When to use**: The instant before sharing, forwarding, or acting on any headline, meme, or post.
**How**: Take a beat and ask yourself whether this is accurate before you hit share (David Rand's phrasing). No fact-checking required — the attentional shift alone does the work.
**Trade-offs**: Highest return per unit of effort in the book, but it only counters the impulsive channel; motivated, deliberate belief is untouched by it.

## Scienceploitation Detection
**When to use**: Any claim leaning on scientific vocabulary to persuade.
**How**: (1) Is the claim riding a *hot topic* — stem cells, regenerative medicine, genomics, nanotech, microbiome, quantum, AI? (2) Does actual research support it, or is the buzzword itself doing the persuading? Does the mechanism make conceptual sense, or is it science-y word salad? (3) Are the scientific trappings appearing inside a field already associated with pseudoscience? (4) Are risks and limitations stated — absence is diagnostic. (5) Check what the field's own governing body says is actually clinic-ready.
**Trade-offs**: Produces false positives on genuinely novel legitimate work, which also uses new vocabulary. Resolve with the range-of-sources test.

## The Quantum Test
**When to use**: A health product or procedure is explained using the word *quantum*.
**How**: Stop evaluating. Assume pseudoscience and a fraudster nearby. Physicist Mark Freeman has never seen *quantum* used accurately in a health product claim.
**Trade-offs**: A blunt heuristic with essentially no false-negative cost in the consumer-health domain; do not export it to physics contexts.

## The Sagan Standard
**When to use**: Evaluating any contrarian, fringe, or "they don't want you to know" claim.
**How**: Assess plausibility against the existing knowledge base; assess the claimant (relevant expertise? selling something? claiming to know what thousands of researchers and mountains of data missed?); scale the required proof to the improbability. Onus stays with the claimant.
**Trade-offs**: Conservative by construction — it will slow acceptance of the rare valid revolution. Caulfield accepts this: real revolutions survive testing.

## Dismantling the Galileo Gambit
**When to use**: Someone answers evidence with a persecution narrative.
**How**: (1) Galileo faced a religious orthodoxy, not a scientific consensus. (2) Sagan's rebuttal — they laughed at Bozo the Clown too. (3) Real revolutionaries produced data tested against the conventions of their time. (4) Classify it as a defence of last resort, not an argument, and return to the body of evidence.
**Trade-offs**: Rhetorically satisfying but rarely persuades the claimant; its value is protecting the audience.

## The Humility Inventory
**When to use**: Before forming or publicly defending a position on contested evidence.
**How**: List areas in your personal and professional life where evidence evolved and you changed position. Separately list the grey areas where you are still gathering information. An empty list is itself the finding.
**Trade-offs**: Self-report, so it is gameable — but the exercise is diagnostic precisely because most people find it harder than expected.

## The Red-Flag Audit for a Single Study
**When to use**: Someone waves one paper at you as proof.
**How**: Check (1) the publisher — known predatory operator? (2) author affiliation — vague or mimicking a real institution? (3) submission-to-acceptance interval — biomedical papers average ~100 days; two weeks means no real review. (4) ethics review conducted? (5) methods — control group, blinding, standardization, sample size. (6) the author's regulatory history. (7) retraction status. (8) citation pattern — do a breadth of other academics cite it?
**Trade-offs**: Time-consuming and requires some literacy in academic publishing. Reserve it for claims that actually matter to a decision.

## The Body-of-Evidence Test
**When to use**: Any extraordinary, contrarian, or personally flattering claim.
**How**: (1) Refuse single study syndrome. (2) Name your own bias on the topic first. (3) Check venue and retraction status. (4) Ask what the scientific consensus is and find it via responsible aggregators — good science communicators, science journalists, university resources, credible professional organizations, competent governments, mainstream media.
**Trade-offs**: Consensus is slow-moving and genuinely lags on emerging topics; for those, seek independent expert advice rather than a settled answer.

## The Range-of-Sources Breakthrough Test
**When to use**: A dramatic scientific result is being reported.
**How**: Ask whether you are hearing it from a range of independent reputable sources or from exactly one commercially interested channel — a clinic's website, a wellness influencer, a podcaster. Real breakthroughs propagate broadly.
**Trade-offs**: Introduces lag; you will be late to genuine news. That lag is the point.

## Health Halo Deconstruction
**When to use**: Any product label or wellness claim leaning on a positive-sounding adjective.
**How**: (1) Ask what the word actually means — is it defined by any regulator? (2) Ask what it implies — usually "healthier/safer." (3) Check whether the implied claim is supported. (4) The gap between (1) and (3) is the halo. For food: assume health-halo terms are meaningless or misleading, read the ingredient list, and default to real food.
**Trade-offs**: Some halo terms encode real production facts (organic means something about farming) — the error is inferring nutrition or safety from them.

## The Evil-Spirits-Free Substitution
**When to use**: A product claims to be "toxin-free," "chemical-free," or similar.
**How**: Substitute "evil-spirits-free" and re-read. If the claim still feels persuasive, you are responding to vibe rather than content.
**Trade-offs**: A rhetorical check, not an ingredient assessment; it will not tell you whether a specific ingredient is a problem.

## Separating the Goal from the Intervention
**When to use**: Any pitch framing a product as serving an obviously virtuous end — safety, sustainability, masculinity, doing right by your kids.
**How**: (1) Isolate the goal. (2) Ask whether the goal was argued for or merely assumed. (3) Ask what evidence supports *this intervention* for that goal. (4) Check whether the pitch first manufactured insecurity in order to sell the fix.
**Trade-offs**: Watch for the motte-and-bailey retreat — challenge the specific claim, and expect a fallback to the unarguable truism.

## The Emotional-Signal Check
**When to use**: Before sharing anything politically or morally charged.
**How**: Kate Starbird's rule — when content makes you feel politically self-righteous, that is precisely the moment to be extra careful about sharing. Misinformation is measurably easier to process, more emotional, scarier, and more focused on morality than accurate content.
**Trade-offs**: Requires honest introspection in exactly the moment when you least want to slow down.

## The Review Escape Plan
**When to use**: Any purchase, booking, or professional choice driven by ratings.
**How**: (1) Be very skeptical of online reviews. (2) Seek side-by-side comparisons under standardized conditions. (3) Go to independent expert outlets — Consumer Reports, Wirecutter, professional video reviews. (4) Use multiple comparative sources, since experts can also be incentivized. (5) Ignore star ratings and aggregate scores. (6) Assume you cannot detect fakes; watch legacy red flags but never conclude the rest are genuine. (7) For professionals and physicians, know the profound limitations and use with extreme caution. (8) Ask who is doing the reviewing and whether their tastes are relevant to yours. (9) Talk to actual humans, face to face. (10) Keep an open mind, try things, and like what you like.
**Trade-offs**: Expert comparatives don't exist for every category, and don't apply to genuinely subjective goods — for those, find critics whose taste consistently matches yours.

## The Four Poll Questions
**When to use**: A survey or poll result is being cited at you.
**How**: (1) How big is the sample — ~1,000 respondents is reasonable for a national survey. (2) Is it representative — random selection, or a self-selecting open internet poll? (3) Who did the research — survey experts or an influencer? (4) Any obvious biases, such as a sponsor with a vested interest? Plus: check question wording for leading phrasing.
**Trade-offs**: You often cannot get answers to all four; inability to answer them is itself a verdict. Standard: sound survey research should mirror public opinion, not shape it.

## Bridge-Building via Incidental Similarities
**When to use**: Trying to reach someone across a political or ideological divide.
**How**: Establish non-political common ground first — shared interests, experiences, tastes. Incidental similarities increase felt closeness and, in turn, openness to opposing political views.
**Trade-offs**: Slow, individual-scale, and not a substitute for correcting the record; but it works where argument does not.
