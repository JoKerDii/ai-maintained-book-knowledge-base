# Cheatsheet — The Certainty Illusion

## Triage: which illusion is this?
- Claim leans on **research, mechanism, or "studies show"** → Science Illusion → run Scienceploitation Detection + Body-of-Evidence Test (ch01–ch05)
- Claim leans on **a virtuous adjective** (natural, clean, safe, sustainable, manly) → Goodness Illusion → run Health Halo Deconstruction + separate goal from intervention (ch06–ch10)
- Claim leans on **what other people think** (stars, rankings, polls, "everyone says") → Opinion Illusion → run the Review Escape Plan + Four Poll Questions (ch11–ch13)

## Core decision rules
- **When a guidepost feels unusually trustworthy, raise suspicion, because distortion follows value.** Trust level is a risk indicator, not a safety indicator.
- **When you feel the urge to share, pause and ask "is this accurate?"** — the single highest-return habit in the book.
- **When content makes you feel politically self-righteous, do not share yet.** Misinformation is measurably easier to process, more emotional, scarier, and more moralized than accurate content.
- **When a claim rests on one study, refuse it.** One study rarely changes anything — most of all when it flatters your priors or offers a contrarian thrill.
- **When a claim is extraordinary, scale the proof bar to the improbability** and keep the onus on the claimant (Sagan standard).
- **When someone invokes Galileo or persecution, treat it as a last-resort defence,** not an argument, and return to the body of evidence.
- **When jargon appears with no plain-language mechanism, the language is there to impress, not inform.**
- **When a label carries a virtuous adjective, read the ingredient list instead.** Assume health-halo terms are meaningless or misleading.
- **When you can't answer who reviewed, how many, and how selected — the number is not evidence.**

## Thresholds & defaults
| Signal | Threshold / default |
|---|---|
| Clinical trials reaching clinical application | ~10% — calibrate "breakthrough" headlines down hard |
| Submission-to-acceptance for real biomedical papers | 91–639 days, ~100 avg. Two weeks = no real peer review |
| National survey sample size | ~1,000 respondents is reasonable; self-selecting internet polls are worthless |
| Unreliable share of online reviews | Assume 25–30%; no platform is at zero |
| Fake-review target band | 4.2–4.5 stars with minor complaints — a manipulation zone, not a credibility signal |
| Incentivized reviews | Inflate ratings 40–70% |
| Your ability to detect fake reviews | Assume ~zero; AI-generated fakes are detected at chance |
| Biomedical abstracts containing spin | ~40% |
| Post-retraction citations acknowledging retraction | 5.4% — retraction status will not reach you passively |
| Predatory journals in existence | 11,000–16,000; 750,000+ articles/year |
| Default food strategy | Real food — fruits, veggies, nuts, legumes, whole grains |

## Tells & smells — fast red flags
**Science-y bunk**
- The word *quantum* attached to a health product → stop, walk away
- Riding a hot topic: stem cells, regenerative medicine, genomics, nanotech, microbiome, quantum, AI
- No stated risks or limitations (only 19% of stem-cell clinic sites mentioned any)
- Science-y word salad in place of a mechanism; "scientifically proven" with no relevant research
- Scientific trappings inside a field already tied to pseudoscience (quantum + astrology)
- "Immune-boosting" — a near-reliable scam marker
- Vagueness sold as profundity: consciousness, resonance, paradigms, optimal levels, transformational
- Fear + jargon together (the science-y/scary dyad) — strip each and test alone; neither usually survives

**Bad publication**
- Vague or institution-mimicking author affiliations; two-week acceptance; no ethics review
- Editorial board publishing itself; journal built to support one predetermined position
- Nobody else cites it (check PubMed / Google Scholar)
- One weak paper carrying an entire commercial industry's proof of efficacy

**Goodness marketing**
- The Devious Dozen: natural, holistic, healthy, organic, non-GMO, gluten-free, chemical-free, toxin-free, locally grown, the colour green, immune-boosting, personalized
- Halo stacking, or halos fused with science jargon — the incoherence is the tell
- Halo appearing where the claim is logically inapplicable (gluten-free water)
- The pitch first manufactured the insecurity it now sells the fix for
- Challenge met with retreat to an unarguable truism (motte-and-bailey)
- "Toxin-free" → substitute "evil-spirits-free" and re-read

**Opinion**
- All five stars (depresses sales — real profiles aren't that clean); balanced-sounding minor gripes (the faker's credibility texture)
- Aggregates you can't decompose: who reviewed, how many, how selected
- Physician/professional ratings — knowledge asymmetry means they track experience, not clinical quality
- Poll with no named methodologist, no sample size, or a sponsor with a stake

## Evidence-source preference order
1. Scientific consensus via responsible aggregators (science journalists, university and government resources, credible professional bodies)
2. Independent expert review with side-by-side comparative testing under standardized conditions (Consumer Reports, Wirecutter)
3. Multiple independent expert sources cross-checked (experts can also be incentivized)
4. Actual humans you know, face to face
5. — hard stop —
6. Consumer star ratings and aggregate scores (ignore for anything with objective quality)

## When consensus is genuinely unsettled
- Consensus well known (smoking, exercise, vaccines, climate, flat Earth) → act on it
- Consensus findable but less known (GMO safety, most supplements, homeopathy) → look it up via aggregators
- Consensus in flux (psychedelics for mental health, probiotics, parabens) → seek independent expert advice; do not let a single study or a confident podcaster settle it
- Assume you *underestimate* how strong consensus is — the public reliably does, because contrarians are overrepresented

## Judging a contrarian claim
- Was it raised and tested in a scientific venue, or proclaimed on podcasts and cable news? → venue is the discriminator
- Plate tectonics and bacterial ulcers won because data eventually supported them, not because the proponents were disbelieved
- Does the claimant have relevant expertise, something to sell, and a story about what thousands of researchers missed?
