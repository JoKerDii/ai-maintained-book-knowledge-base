# Chapter 11: That Earthy Taste! The Opinion Economy and Biases in the Machine

## Core Idea
Online ratings and reviews feel like authentic, crowd-sourced certainty, which is exactly why they have been captured by a trillion-dollar **opinion economy** that manufactures, incentivizes, and distorts them. Even the reviews that are genuine are systematically skewed by who writes them and by biases that have nothing to do with product quality.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The opinion economy**: Caulfield's name for the vast, lightly regulated industry that monetizes our belief in the authentic opinions of strangers — and, in doing so, destroys the authenticity that made those opinions valuable.
  - When to use: Any time a purchase, booking, or professional choice is being driven by a star rating.
  - How: Ask three questions before trusting an aggregate — (1) Who profits if this rating moves up half a star? (2) Is this rating plausibly manipulated, incentivized, or herded? (3) Would an independent comparative expert agree?
  - Why it works: It reframes reviews from "data" to "a market in perceived authenticity," where the exploited attribute is precisely the one you were relying on.
- **The five reasons we over-trust reviews**: (1) "a person like me" trust; (2) the persuasive force of anecdote and testimonial; (3) the "feels real" perceived authenticity; (4) wisdom-of-the-crowd consensus; (5) desire for quick, easy, certain answers.
  - When to use: As a self-diagnostic when a review feels unusually convincing.
  - How: Name which of the five is doing the work on you. If it is (1) or (2) — a story from someone like you — discount hardest; those are the most exploitable.
  - Failure mode: Wisdom of the crowd only works if each judgment is independent. Online reviews are not independent, so the mechanism you are trusting is broken.
- **The fake review cycle** (Curtis Boyd, Transparency Company): Business buys fake reviews to sell more, spends the extra revenue on ads with the platform hosting the fakes, so the platform, the advertiser, the business, and the fake reviewer all profit — only the customer loses.
  - When to use: When evaluating whether a platform's fraud-removal claims mean anything.
  - How: Assume enforcement is misaligned with incentives; treat platform "we removed millions of fakes" numbers as evidence of scale, not of cleanliness.
- **The Goldilocks zone (4.2–4.5 stars)**: All-five-star ratings actually reduce sales; mixed good-and-bad reads as credible. Sophisticated fakers deliberately include small complaints.
  - When to use: Reading a page that seems believably good.
  - How: Treat a plausible balance of praise and minor gripes as neutral evidence, not as a credibility signal — it is a manipulation target.

## Key Concepts
- **Anchoring effect**: A high (possibly fake-inflated) average becomes the reference point that pulls honest reviewers' own ratings upward.
- **Social influence bias**: Seeing prior positive reviews raises your own rating — one experiment found +32% likelihood of a positive rating and +25% in final ratings via accumulating "positive herding."
- **Choice supportive bias (post-purchase rationalization)**: Verified reviewers already paid, so they are motivated to say they liked it.
- **Marketing placebo effect**: Higher price and recognizable brand raise perceived quality — expensive wine tastes better; discounted energy drink measurably works less well.
- **Precarious manhood principle**: Men devalue products with feminine-coded brand names to defend their sense of masculinity ("Real Men Don't Buy 'Mrs. Clean'").
- **Incentivized review**: A review paid for or discounted into existence — not technically fake, but rating-inflating by 40–70% and requiring FTC disclosure.
- **Super contributor**: A high-volume reviewer for whom reviewing is identity and brand-building; disproportionately influential because of an authoritative voice.
- **Illusory consensus**: The manufactured impression that everyone agrees, delivered by a star rating.
- **Rating filter effect**: ~70% of shoppers filter by rating, most commonly at four stars — below four, you are invisible.

## Mental Models
- Think of a star rating as **a price signal in a manipulation market**, not a measurement of quality.
- Use the "ox-weight" test: wisdom of the crowd requires independent guesses; **when reviewers can see each other, the crowd gets less wise, not more**.
- Think of reviewers as **an extreme-value sample, not a random sample** — an MIT Sloan study found only 1.5% of an apparel company's customers wrote reviews. You are reading an average of raves and rants.
- Use expert comparative evaluation when the product has objective properties; use reviews only for taste-like attributes where there is no expert answer anyway.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating volume as a fix**: "Thousands of reviews will average out the bias" fails — garbage in, garbage out scales with the garbage, since ~25–30% may be unreliable.
- **Reading a balanced-sounding review as authentic**: The stained-blanket detail is the exact texture fakers add.
- **Trusting reviews more than experts or friends**: 88% do, despite reviewers being non-random, non-independent, and often paid.
- **Ignoring context effects**: Weather, travel companions, culture, and gender bias move ratings — rainy-day reviews are measurably lower; NYC hotel ratings ranked couples 8.19 highest, business travellers 7.61 lowest.

## Worked Example
Oobah Butler's The Shed at Dulwich. He bought a $13 burner phone to get TripAdvisor-verified, built an appointment-only website with a menu of *moods* rather than dishes, and photographed food styled from shaving cream and toilet-bowl bleach cakes. He and recruited friends posted florid fake reviews from different computers — praising the "earthy taste," with a small complaint about a stained blanket for credibility. Starting at rank 18,149 of 18,149 restaurants in London, the Shed reached 156 within months and eventually number one, with 89,000 page views in a single day. Booking requests poured in worldwide; Butler refused them all, because top-rated restaurants do not need your business — and the refusals amplified the exclusivity. No food was ever served. The lesson: rank was produced entirely by narrative and scarcity signals, with zero underlying product.

## Key Takeaways
1. Assume roughly a quarter to a third of reviews on any platform are unreliable; no platform is at zero.
2. Discount the star rating and read *why* a rating was given and *who* gave it — but know that "someone like me" is the most exploited channel.
3. Never read a 4.2–4.5 average with light criticism as a credibility guarantee; it is the fakers' target zone.
4. Assume herding: your own rating and everyone else's is anchored to whatever is already on the board.
5. One extra Yelp star can move restaurant revenue 5–9%; the money explains the fraud.
6. Fake reviews influence roughly $791 billion in annual US online spending, directly affecting about $152 billion (World Economic Forum estimate).
7. Prefer independent comparative testing (Consumer Reports-style) over aggregated consumer opinion for anything with objective quality.
