# Chapter 4: The Predator Problem: Predatory Journals, Hijacked Titles, and Fake Peer Review

## Core Idea
An industry of pay-to-publish journals with little or no peer review, plus criminally cloned journal identities and purpose-built advocacy "journals," has flooded the literature with real-looking papers — and those papers reach clinical guidelines, government policy, media coverage, and marketing. Publication itself is no longer a credibility signal; only the specific venue and citation pattern are.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Red-flag audit for a single supporting study**: Caulfield's diagnostic set, applied when someone waves one paper at you.
  - When to use: A product, therapy, or contrarian claim rests on "just some of the research."
  - How: (1) Publisher — known predatory operator? (2) Author affiliation — vague or mimicking a real institution ("Medical School Birmingham")? (3) Submission-to-acceptance interval — a 2021 study found biomedical papers take 91–639 days, averaging around 100 days to acceptance; two weeks means no real review. (4) Ethics review — was it done? Legitimate biomedical journals require proof. (5) Methods — control group, blinding, standardization, sample size. (6) Author's regulatory history.
  - Why it works: predatory publication leaves a consistent forensic signature, and one flag rarely appears alone.
- **Source-legitimacy check for controversial claims**: If a claim runs counter to conventional wisdom *and* appears to support a specific controversial agenda, double-check the legitimacy of the source before anything else. Controversial research does and should get published in credible journals — but it must survive genuinely independent peer review.
- **Citation-pattern test**: Look at who cites the work and how.
  - When to use: Assessing whether a paper is real science or propaganda.
  - How: If the broader academic community isn't picking it up — even to contest it — be suspicious. Caulfield's citation analysis of the *International Journal of Vaccine Theory, Practice, and Research* found nearly all citations came from the same journal, from its own editorial board (often in predatory venues), or from known anti-vaccine advocates. Echo-chambering is diagnostic.
- **Defunding the industry (policy fix)**: Prohibit the use of public research funds to pay predatory publication fees. Most academics would not pay out of pocket; no funds, no industry. Predatory journals need the academic community to survive.

## Key Concepts
- **Predatory journals**: Publications that charge authors a fee while applying lax or nonexistent peer review, with editorial boards padded by questionable "experts." Estimated 11,000–16,000 of them; likely over 750,000 predatory articles per year; roughly $400 million spent annually (some estimates run to billions).
- **Hijacked journals**: Criminal cloning of a legitimate journal's name, domain, and imagery to harvest publication fees — "the publication equivalent of buying a fake Gucci handbag when you think you are getting the real thing." Straight-up fraud, distinct from predatory publishing.
- **Vanity / agenda-driven journals**: Purpose-built pretend journals created to legitimize a predetermined position (anti-vaccine, climate denial, alternative medicine, the defunct Holocaust-denier *Journal of Historical Review*), typically publishing their own editorial board.
- **Publish-or-perish economy**: The incentive substrate. A 2021 study found predatory publication spans all fields and experience levels, driven by research evaluation policies and publication pressure. In South Africa, compensation tied to publication *quantity* produced up to a 140-fold increase in predatory publications.
- **Literature pollution**: Fake and low-quality papers entering PubMed, Scopus, and MEDLINE, then systematic reviews, clinical practice guidelines, and government documents.
- **Single-source legitimization**: One weak paper in a predatory venue converted into an entire commercial industry's proof of efficacy.
- **Predatory publishing as academic misconduct**: Yeo-Teh and Tang (2021) argue that wilfully submitting to a predatory journal is "an active act of avoidance of rigorous peer review" and therefore scientific misconduct.

## Mental Models
- Think of publication as **an address, not a credential** — the question is never "was it published?" but "where, reviewed by whom, cited by whom?"
- Use the **triple-whammy frame** on predatory publishing: public money funded the research, the researcher's salary, *and* the fee — plus, per Moher, thousands of sacrificed animals and wasted time from millions of research subjects.
- Think of a predatory paper as **an injection into the literature** — Runels's paper did its job by making an unproven procedure look settled, and the certainty then propagated outward into clinics and press.
- Use **"a five-year-old could expose this in one click"** (Daube) as the standard for how little verification these venues perform.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a journal name as a signal**: *Journal of Medical Research* versus *Journal of the American Medical Association*; *Scientific Journal of Research and Reviews* is, in Caulfield's phrase, the equivalent of a fake ID.
- **Trusting reputable databases implicitly**: Abalkina found roughly 400 publications from hijacked journals in the WHO's COVID-19 library; a 2023 study found Scopus carrying work from 67+ hijacked journals, including unvetted medical research.
- **Assuming institutional gatekeeping catches it**: A 2020 study of 20 Canadian universities' tenure and promotion policies found no language explicitly discouraging predatory publication; a small Canadian study found predatory publication associated with *greater* financial reward via promotion and merit increments, and with receiving grants.
- **Rewarding quantity over quality**: The gasoline of the whole industry; also associated with reduced job satisfaction, poorer research quality, publication bias, and fraud.
- **Believing "peer-reviewed" because the journal says so**: An anti-vaccine correspondent stressed to Caulfield that his source was a "peer-reviewed scholarly open access journal." It isn't.

## Worked Example
The O-Shot. Dr. Jen Gunter, an OB-GYN, publicly called the O-Shot — Dr. Charles Runels's trademarked procedure injecting platelet-rich plasma into genital tissue to improve sex — "potentially very dangerous." Runels responded by posting "just some of the research." That research was essentially one small study, Runels as lead author, eleven women self-reporting, no control group, no blinding, no standardization. Gunter's assessment: "abysmal." Run the red-flag audit: the publisher was OMICS International, notoriously predatory (later fined $50 million by the FTC in 2019 for deceiving thousands of authors by claiming it provided rigorous peer review); the corresponding author's address was the ambiguous "Medical School Birmingham," which Google Street View showed to be a modest two-storey building with a dumpster out front, 250 miles from the actual University of Alabama at Birmingham medical school; submission June 12, acceptance June 25 — under two weeks against a typical 91–639 days; and the paper explicitly noted no ethics review, in an author who had already been sanctioned by the FDA for a research ethics violation. Downstream effect: no well-done clinical trial supports the sex/orgasm claims, and Gunter notes there is no biological reason PRP would help with orgasms — yet clinics worldwide sell the procedure for thousands of dollars, citing that study, and *Cosmopolitan*, *Harper's Bazaar*, the *Daily Mail*, *The Sun*, and others have run uncritical coverage. One predatory paper manufactured an industry's illusory certainty. (Runels also markets the P-Shot, which he claims to have discovered by injecting his own penis; a 2022 *JAMA* paper found PRP for erectile dysfunction offered at substantial cost "despite a paucity of evidence," and the Sexual Medicine Society of North America's 2021 consensus statement says it should not be offered in routine clinical practice.)

## Key Takeaways
1. Before accepting any single study, run the red-flag audit: publisher, affiliation, submission-to-acceptance time, ethics review, methods, author history.
2. An acceptance turnaround under a few weeks is near-proof that no meaningful peer review occurred.
3. Check citation patterns — self-citation within one journal and its own editorial board marks propaganda, not science.
4. Do not treat presence in PubMed, Scopus, MEDLINE, or even a WHO library as validation; hijacked and predatory content reaches all of them.
5. Fix the incentive, not the symptom: block public funds from paying predatory fees and shift institutional metrics from quantity to quality and community impact.
6. When a claim is both counter-consensus and conveniently supportive of someone's agenda, verify the venue first and the content second.
7. Remember the reach: Moher and Lalu found over 800 national and international clinical and public health policies citing papers from a single predatory publisher.
