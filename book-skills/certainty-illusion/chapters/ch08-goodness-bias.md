# Chapter 8: Ancient Aliens, Wishful Thinking, and the Goodness Bias

## Core Idea
Wishful thinking doesn't just distort consumers — it distorts the production, publication, and reporting of science itself. When a research goal is perceived as noble, weak data gets called conclusive, low-quality studies get amplified, and even questionable research practices start looking defensible. Bias doesn't have to be self-serving or nefarious to wreck an evidence base.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **White hat bias** (coined by David Allison and Mark Cope, 2010): "Distortion of research-based information in the service of what may be perceived as righteous ends." Research on noble topics is more likely to get funded, published, hyped, and believed.
  - When to use: Assessing literature or media coverage on any topic with an obviously virtuous goal — childhood obesity prevention, pandemic therapeutics, organic food, clean cosmetics, CBD.
  - How: (1) Identify the righteous goal in play. (2) Check study design — is it observational or randomized? (3) Compare the abstract's tone with the effect size. (4) Ask whether contrary or null results would have received equal attention. (5) Weight the *body* of evidence, not the loudest study.
  - Why it works: Allison — "People seem far more willing to accept lower-quality data and call it conclusive when it fits with what they perceive to be righteous ends or feel-good ideas." Lower-quality observational work is *more* prone to white hat bias because selective analysis and reporting make weak data easy to spin.
- **The goodness bias**: The broader tendency for perceived moral worthiness to warp judgment — extending even to how researchers evaluate misconduct.
  - When to use: Auditing your own or an institution's standards when the cause is sympathetic.
  - How: Ask the counterfactual — would I accept this method, this sample size, this press release, if the finding cut against a goal I like?
  - Failure mode: The self-reinforcing loop — noble goal produces weak data, weak data gets disproportionate media coverage, coverage shapes public perception and future research priorities, and correcting it takes years, if it happens at all.
- **Pareidolia check**: The mind's tendency to see familiar patterns in random data (the face on Mars, the man in the moon) is amplified by prior belief. Before treating an apparent pattern as evidence, ask whether you wanted to see it.

## Key Concepts
- **Wishful thinking**: A catch-all for the perception traps that let us construct certainty — "we want to see evidence of aliens; we see evidence of aliens."
- **Confirmation bias**: Finding, favouring, and remembering information that supports pre-existing beliefs.
- **Motivated reasoning**: Evaluating and selecting arguments and evidence to endorse a preferred outcome.
- **Attentional bias**: Focusing on some elements and ignoring others, often based on recent attention, obscuring alternative explanations.
- **Pareidolia**: Seeing meaningful shapes in random data; a likely evolutionary survival shortcut. A 2013 Finnish study found the religious and paranormal-believing more prone to it, as are those receptive to "pseudo-profound bullshit."
- **Pseudoarchaeology**: Origin myths dressed as research — the ancient alien narrative, connected by researcher Stephanie Halmhofer to antisemitism, white supremacism, occult fascism, and QAnon.
- **Publication and press-release bias**: A 2015 analysis found methodological rigour had no effect on press coverage; large RCTs were no more likely to get a journal press release, and news media are *more* likely to cover low-quality observational studies.
- **Placebo–media feedback loop**: Placebo-driven positive trials generate coverage, coverage inflates expectations, inflated expectations strengthen the next trial's placebo response.

## Mental Models
- Use **"who would want this to be true?"** as a bias probe *before* "who funded this?" — the noble-cause distortion is invisible precisely because it isn't corrupt.
- Think of **a righteous research goal as a risk factor for weak evidence**, not a reassurance about it.
- Think of **an appealing pattern as a hypothesis you are unusually badly placed to evaluate** — Caulfield saw a rocket on a Mayan sarcophagus lid that actually depicts a king's descent into the underworld, and got pulled back in by the face on Mars *after* he'd already rejected the theory.
- Use **"be open to the wonder, but not closed to the facts"** as the calibration point: too much cynicism closes your mind, too much wishfulness closes it differently.

## Anti-patterns
- **Grading evidence by the virtue of its goal**: A 2019 University of Southern Mississippi study of 100+ NIH-funded principal investigators found scientists rated unethical research practices as *more defensible* when the research was being done for noble reasons.
- **Treating "seems goofy" as "harmless"**: Ancient-alien claims are aimed almost exclusively at non-white civilizations — the pyramids of Giza, Machu Picchu, Sacsayhuamán, Pumapunku, Teotihuacán, Rapa Nui — but never the Colosseum, reinforcing racist ideas about European paramountcy.
- **Confusing a real clinical benefit with a real mechanism**: Placebo-driven pain relief is genuine relief and a worthwhile outcome, but reporting it as pharmacological effect damages the long-term evidence base.
- **Assuming only industry money biases research**: Institutional and financial pressure is the obvious source; white hat bias shows pressure needn't be self-serving to distort integrity and reporting.
- **Amplifying observational findings with causal language**: The easiest place for white hat bias to enter, and the hardest to walk back.

## Worked Example
**Hydroxychloroquine and COVID-19, as a white hat bias case.** The righteous goal — an urgently needed therapy to reduce deaths and hospitalizations — was unarguable. A 2021 study found this contributed to a publication bias favouring positive results. Positive findings from *lower-quality* studies carried more influence than the higher-quality randomized controlled trials showing no benefit and possible harm. The evidence ultimately established the drug was ineffective for COVID-19. The paper's conclusion: research quality "may be negatively affected by white hat bias, which comes from personal beliefs and the urgent need of an effective intervention." The same analysis found nonrandomized studies were the more susceptible category, because selective analysis and reporting are easier when the data are weaker.

**The CBD loop.** Everyone wants a cheap, safe, "natural" therapy, so CBD research is saturated with hype (Caulfield, Snyder, and Zenone's 2020 study found CBD cancer-therapy claims on crowdfunding platforms riddled with harmful misinformation). A 2022 Karolinska Institutet study on cannabinoids for pain found that placebo responses contribute significantly to the pain reduction seen in cannabinoid RCTs — *and* that the placebo response was itself bolstered by media coverage of earlier placebo-driven positive studies. Placebo produces positive trials, positive trials produce coverage, coverage produces more placebo, and around it goes.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the counterfactual test on every study that pleases you: would you accept this design if the result cut the other way?
2. Check study type before study conclusion — observational correlation is where noble-cause spin lives.
3. Expect media coverage to be *inversely* related to rigour; don't use press attention as a quality proxy.
4. Treat a strongly desired outcome as a warning that you and the field are both primed to over-read weak data.
5. Watch for feedback loops — coverage can manufacture the very effect the next study measures.
6. Recognize that "harmless fun" narratives often carry ideological payloads worth naming explicitly.
7. Ask whether the pattern you see is in the data or in your wanting.
