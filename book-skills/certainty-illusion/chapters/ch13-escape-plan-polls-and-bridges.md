# Chapter 13: An Escape Plan: Polls, Taste Makers, and Liking What You Like

## Core Idea
There is no way to extract reliable signal from consumer reviews, so the escape plan is to substitute independent comparative expert sources, apply explicit quality criteria to polls and surveys, and recognize that aggregated opinion shapes not just what you buy but what you believe you *like*. Deliberately widening your tastes is both freeing and, per the research, a small bridge across polarization.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The escape plan for reviews** (Caulfield's synthesis of what the experts he interviewed actually recommend):
  - When to use: Any consumer decision you care about.
  - How, as explicit steps: (1) Default to "be very skeptical of online reviews" (Proserpio). (2) Seek side-by-side comparisons of performance in standardised environments (de Langhe). (3) Go to independent expert outlets that describe products professionally — Consumer Reports, Wirecutter, video reviews (Khalifah). (4) Use *multiple* independent sources doing comparative analysis, since even experts are sometimes incentivized. (5) Ignore star ratings and aggregate consumer scores. (6) Check your own motivated reading: if you already want the product, positives will weigh more; if unsure, negatives will.
  - Why it works: It replaces a manipulated authenticity signal with comparative, standardised measurement — the one thing consumer reviews structurally cannot provide.
  - Failure mode: Expert outlets can be incentivized too; single-source reliance reintroduces the problem.
- **The four questions for any survey or poll** (with Frank Graves, EKOS Research):
  - When to use: Whenever a percentage is quoted at you in news, marketing, or politics.
  - How: (1) **How big?** Bigger is better; ~1,000 respondents is reasonable for a national survey. (2) **Representative?** Random selection beats a self-selecting open internet poll. (3) **Who did it?** Survey experts, or a social media influencer? (4) **Obvious biases?** Does the sponsor have a vested interest? If the wording is available, check whether questions are leading — good surveys pre-test questions.
  - Why it works: Graves' standard — "sound survey research should be a mirror measuring public opinion, not a force shaping it." The four questions test whether it is a mirror or a lever.
- **Who's reviewing the reviewers?**: Before accepting an aggregate as "what people think," ask who is actually in the aggregate.
  - When to use: Critic scores, aggregate ratings, best-of rankings.
  - How: Ask who is doing the reviewing, which reviews are being aggregated, and whether that population's tastes are relevant to yours.
- **The deliberate taste-widening experiment**: Force exposure to what you have decided you dislike, with the iconography consciously bracketed off.
  - When to use: When you notice a preference is doing identity work rather than pleasure work.
  - How: For a set period, listen to (or watch, or read) the things you claim to loathe. If it comes on, you must engage attentively. Actively seek genres you expect to hate. While engaging, suppress thinking about what liking it would signal about you.

## Key Concepts
- **Spiral of silence**: People withhold opinions they believe are in the minority, so visible consensus signals silence dissent.
- **Likes as consensus cue**: A 2019 study found likes directly changed personal opinion because they read as a signal of societal consensus.
- **Faux research**: Graves' term for the flood of flawed or fabricated polling that corrodes the public interest.
- **Illusory public opinion**: A manufactured consensus that then produces real consensus.
- **Iconography over sound**: Caulfield's rough split of musical taste — ~70% iconography, 25% quality, 5% random exposure.
- **Social 'badge' of group membership**: Stated musical taste as an identity marker producing in-group favouritism.
- **Stated vs. measured taste**: What you say you like diverges from what you actually enjoy — 60% of liberals in one study said they liked R&B but tested as liking it less than conservatives.
- **Social-not-musicological algorithms** (Kelefa Sanneh): Streaming recommendations track "people who like that also like this," treating genres as social worlds rather than sounds.
- **Narrowing of taste**: Higher education and greater childhood musical exposure correlate with liking *fewer* artists, because status signalling rewards exclusion.
- **Incidental similarities**: Non-political common ground that increases felt closeness and, in turn, openness to opposing political views.

## Mental Models
- Use **independent comparative testing** when the question is quality; use **your own attention** when the question is taste.
- Think of a poll as **a mirror or a lever** — and assume it is a lever until the four questions say otherwise.
- Think of aggregated critic scores as **a demographic sample, not a verdict**: 64% of reviews of the 100 highest-grossing films were written by white men; 89% of "top critic" reviews were by white critics.
- Use "**if you are a fan, you are a fan**" as the resolution — identity-shaped taste is still real taste; the point is not to feel fraudulent but to stop letting signalling narrow what you get to enjoy.

## Anti-patterns
- **Trying to filter signal from review noise**: The experts uniformly say individual consumers cannot do it. Substitute sources; don't sharpen your filter.
- **Treating an open internet poll as data**: Self-selection guarantees it measures who cared to click.
- **Letting an aggregate score gate your experience**: A hard rule like "nothing under 90% on Rotten Tomatoes" outsources taste to an unrepresentative panel.
- **Mistaking a critic's verdict for your reaction**: Exposure to a critic's review influenced whether people liked a song *more than listening to the song did*.
- **Assuming market forces or regulation will clean this up**: Incentives align against it; the analogy is trying to regulate the stock market.

## Worked Example
FiveThirtyEight's regendering of the IMDb rankings. Of over 4,000 movies on IMDb, only 97 — 2.4% — had more ratings from women than from men. For 90% of films, male ratings outnumbered female by at least two to one; for 51%, by more than five to one. When the ranking was recomputed using only women's ratings, the canon rearranged: *The Godfather* fell from second to tenth, *The Godfather II* from third to twenty-second, *The Empire Strikes Back* from thirteenth to forty-first. Caulfield's added point is that the gap is probably *understated*, because women rating those films were already rating inside a male-dominated critical discourse. The takeaway is procedural, not about any specific film: an aggregate is a population, and changing the population changes the "objective" answer. Alongside it he runs his own year-long counter-experiment — a lifelong punk-rock snob forcing himself through yacht rock, Fleetwood Mac, AC/DC, and '70s disco with the iconography bracketed off, expanding his "this is great" list by 30–40%, while noting some dislikes never budged.

## Key Takeaways
1. Replace consumer star ratings with two or more independent, comparative expert sources; ignore the aggregate score.
2. Run the four survey questions — size (~1,000 for national), representativeness (random, not self-selected), who conducted it, and sponsor bias — plus question wording where available.
3. Treat visible consensus (star averages, likes, poll toplines) as an active force that shapes opinion and silences minority views, not a passive readout.
4. Before trusting any aggregate, ask who is in it and whether their tastes bear on yours.
5. Audit your own motivated reading: wanting the product weights the positives; ambivalence weights the negatives.
6. Deliberately consume what you have written off, with the identity signalling suppressed — it costs little and reliably expands what you can enjoy.
7. Use non-political common ground ("incidental similarities") as a route into contested conversations; forced exposure to other perspectives measurably moved even hardcore partisans in the Berkeley/Yale Fox-to-CNN study.
