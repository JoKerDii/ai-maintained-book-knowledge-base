# Chapter 3: The Hype Machine, Catastrophizing, and Science Investment

## Core Idea
The vocabulary that hucksters exploit was manufactured by the legitimate research community itself, because funding incentives reward exaggerated promises at every stage from grant application to press release. The same machinery inflates predicted harms as readily as predicted benefits, and the fix is not less science funding but structurally de-hyped science.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The hype pipeline (Caulfield & Condit, 2012)**: Exaggerated claims of benefit and social impact are generated throughout the knowledge-production process — from initial excitement, through grant writing, through publication, through media representation.
  - When to use: Tracing any "breakthrough" headline back to its source, or auditing your own institution's communications.
  - How: Check each stage — (1) Was the funding case built on near-future benefit or economic-growth promises? (2) Does the abstract contain spin? (3) Does the press release exaggerate advice, causation, or animal-to-human relevance? (4) Does the media story compress a decade-plus timeline?
  - Why it works: hype begets hype and creates a culture where *not* hyping is penalized as failing to be a team player.
- **Understatement as default**: Caulfield's prescription for researchers and clinicians in a misinformation era — never hype your work, while still making accurate science engaging and shareable.
  - When to use: Any science communication, grant, or press release you control.
  - How: Strip breakthrough/revolution/paradigm-shift framing; state timelines honestly; state limitations; invest in creative collaboration (artists, authors, social-media-savvy communicators) to compete with sensationalist noise without borrowing its methods.
- **Funding by lottery (Page & Barnett)**: Peer reviewers determine basic fundability — that the research meets reasonable methodological and organizational standards — and the rest is allocated by lottery.
  - When to use: Policy-level argument about grant allocation.
  - How/why: The current process is already near-random and biased; a lottery is cheaper, faster, more equitable, may encourage out-of-the-box work, and critically removes the incentive to hype. Tried by the Swiss National Science Foundation and the Health Research Council of New Zealand.
- **The big-picture / body-of-evidence rule**: Never rest a conclusion on a single dramatic claim; when a genuine significant development happens, you will hear it from numerous reputable sources and there will be a supporting body of evidence.

## Key Concepts
- **Hype**: Exaggerated claims of potential benefit and social impact.
- **Impact merchants**: A 2016 interview study's term for how senior academics saw themselves; interviewees described "marketizing" research as "a matter of survival in academia." One: find an academic who has never had to bullshit or embellish for grants, and you'll have found one in trouble with their department head.
- **Hot stuff bias**: The tendency for every grant application to converge on whatever field is currently fundable (when genomics is hot, everything becomes genomics).
- **Spin**: Distortion in reporting of results; a 2017 study found nearly 40 percent of biomedical abstracts contained at least some spin.
- **Press-release exaggeration**: Sumner et al. (2014), 462 health/biomedical press releases — 40 percent contained exaggerated health advice, 33 percent exaggerated causation, 36 percent exaggerated inferences from animal studies — and the hype was transcribed into the popular press.
- **Media-attention/replication inverse relation**: A 2023 study of 14,000 papers found media attention *negatively* associated with replication — more coverage, less reliable.
- **Catastrophizing**: The mirror-image failure — overstating potential harms of emerging science, an "academic cottage industry."
- **Grant-process waste**: Canadian success rates around 15 percent; a 2015 Australian analysis estimated one large biomedical competition wasted over 600 years of researcher time.

## Mental Models
- Think of **hype as an emission**, not a lie: it is the predictable byproduct of a funding system, so fix the incentives rather than blaming individuals.
- Use the **timeline test** on any therapy claim: clinical trials alone take 10–15 years and roughly 90 percent fail — so a "five to ten years or sooner" promise (found in almost 70 percent of stem-cell newspaper articles Caulfield analyzed with Kalina Kamenova) is impossible by construction.
- Think of **utopian and dystopian narratives as the same machine running in two directions**: both distort public perception and policy; both make good marketing material.
- Use **"invest in science generally, discount every specific promise"**: you cannot predict where research leads, which is precisely why the promises used to justify it almost never materialize.

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing economic-impact projections**: Texas (2009) projected 230,000 jobs and $88 billion from state stem-cell investment; New Jersey projected 20,000 jobs and $71.9 billion; California's 2004 analysis projected "modest" health-care savings of $6.4–12.6 billion from cures for stroke, Parkinson's, spinal cord injury, and Alzheimer's, and persuaded voters to commit $3 billion. Two decades later none of those therapies exist.
- **Trusting grant rank as a quality signal**: A 2018 PNAS study of 40+ reviewers on the same grants "showed no agreement among reviewers"; a 2016 analysis of 100,000+ successful NIH grants found no relationship between ranking and post-award productivity.
- **Reading only the abstract**: Often the only thing read, including by academics, and roughly 40 percent contain spin.
- **Treating animal data as human-relevant**: The single most common press-release exaggeration.
- **Blaming only external bad actors**: Holden Thorp — until the scientific community deals with misinformation from within, it cannot expect to deal with it from without.
- **Letting Big Science hype crowd out the unglamorous**: Walkable neighbourhoods, food and exercise policy, smoking cessation, community mental health — high aggregate impact, low headline value.

## Worked Example
Hype measured. A University of Tsukuba team analyzed nearly a million abstracts from successful NIH grant applications spanning 1985 to 2020, coding for hype — adjectives promoting a project's significance, novelty, scale, and rigour. Hype in successful applications rose approximately 1,400 percent over that span. This is not social media; it is the peer-reviewed, prestigious primary funder of U.S. biomedical research. The same team then examined the language NIH itself used in its funding-opportunity announcements and found a comparably massive increase in hype. The causal loop is visible: funders hype the call, applicants hype it back, and the successful applications become the source vocabulary that press releases, media coverage, and eventually unproven-therapy clinics inherit. Caulfield's own experience closes the loop — at a 2013 workshop on whole genome screening, a senior genetics researcher pulled him aside and told him to stop criticizing the clinical value of genomics or the money would go to stem cells.

## Key Takeaways
1. Be leery of breakthrough, revolution, and paradigm-shift language; science progresses incrementally and messily.
2. If a genuine major advance occurs, multiple reputable sources will report it and a body of evidence will exist — so always ask for the body, not the study.
3. Discount near-future clinical timelines by an order of magnitude; account for the ~90 percent clinical-trial failure rate.
4. Treat heavy media coverage as weak evidence *against* replicability.
5. When reading a study, go past the abstract and check the press release against the paper.
6. Fix hype at the funder level — it is where the loop starts, and a lottery-based allocation would remove the incentive entirely.
7. Apply the same skepticism to catastrophizing as to hype; overstated harms feed the scary side of misinformation and get weaponized by marketers.
