# Chapter 5: AI and Zombie Science

## Core Idea
Generative AI is about to multiply the volume of plausible fake science faster than experts can filter it, while discredited research already refuses to die — retracted papers keep being cited, keep drawing attention, and are actively reanimated by those whose agenda they serve. The single durable defence is refusing to rest any conclusion on one paper.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The single study syndrome rule (Ivan Oransky, Retraction Watch)**: If a claim is based on one study, be "much more suspicious."
  - When to use: Any argument that points to a paper rather than a literature.
  - How: A single study can be interesting and can invite revisiting prior work — but if someone is literally pointing to *one* paper, suspicion is the default. Always consider the *body* of evidence. Caulfield names this the most important takeaway of Part I.
  - Why it works: It neutralizes predatory publications, hijacked papers, agenda journals, AI-fabricated abstracts, and zombie retractions in one move, without requiring you to detect each fraud individually.
- **Two-stage retraction (Holden Thorp)**: Split retraction into validity first, blame second.
  - When to use: Designing or advocating for journal/institutional retraction policy.
  - How: Stage one determines whether the paper is valid, with no attribution of fault, so a defective paper can be removed from the literature immediately (even provisionally). Stage two — "who screwed up?" — handles misconduct, which is slow because reputations and careers are at stake.
  - Why it works: Retractions currently average two to three years (Wakefield took over ten); misinformation research shows the longer bad information circulates, the more entrenched and harder to debunk it becomes. The U.K. parliament's April 2023 scientific-integrity report proposed a retraction process taking no longer than two months.
- **Make retraction unmissable**: Databases must flag retracted papers in search results, not just on the journal page; Google and Google Scholar should surface it too; a clear red "retracted" banner. Journalists should never rely on retracted studies, and when reporting a retraction should explain how and why it happened — trustworthiness of the retraction's source is a strong predictor of its effectiveness.

## Key Concepts
- **Zombie science**: Discredited or retracted findings that continue circulating and influencing academic literature, public perception, and health debates after formal correction.
- **Motivated reasoning**: The cognitive bias leading to the embrace of ideas based on desirability rather than evidence — the mechanism by which zombies are kept alive, including reasoning the retraction itself into proof of suppression.
- **Post-retraction citation**: A 2021 analysis of 13,000+ papers retracted 1960–2020 found only 5.4 percent of post-retraction references acknowledged the retraction. An *Economist* analysis of 20,000 retracted articles found 84 percent received at least one post-retraction citation — 95,000 in total — and those citing papers were themselves cited in another 1.65 million papers.
- **Retraction attention paradox**: A 2022 University of Michigan analysis of nearly 4,000 retracted papers found they received *more* attention over ten years — press, social media, academic literature — than never-retracted studies. Caulfield's explanation: retracted content is often more exciting, more extreme, and better suited to a cultural moment demanding supportive data.
- **Paper mills**: A multi-million-dollar industry selling authorship on publications that usually involve faked or poor-quality data.
- **AI as "weapon of mass disruption"**: Eurasia Group's 2023 designation — as content volume rises exponentially, most citizens will be unable to reliably distinguish fact from fiction.
- **Loss of the middleman**: Sandra Wachter's concern — if experts can no longer determine what is true, we lose the intermediaries we need to guide us through complicated topics.
- **Pollution of the literature (Oransky)**: The cumulative effect of bad research, retractions, and predatory journals, slowing science's march toward objective truths on a timescale Oransky calls "generational."

## Mental Models
- Think of a zombie paper as **an infectious agent**: it lives, mutates, and spreads its poison through citation networks — killing the original does not clear the descendants.
- Use **"exciting is a risk factor"**: the more extreme and headline-friendly a finding, the more likely it is bad science and the more attention it will retain even after retraction.
- Think of retraction denial as **a self-sealing narrative**: for the motivated reasoner, the retraction *is* the evidence ("Big Pharma can't have those conclusions floating around"), which makes the bogus study appear more valid, not less.
- Use **Oransky's swamp**: picking off a floating piece of plastic is necessary but not sufficient — the PCBs are at the bottom. Whac-A-Mole on individual studies cannot substitute for system-altering fixes.

## Anti-patterns
- **Citing without checking retraction status**: A 2022 study found most authors say they check references, yet 89 percent were unaware a cited article was retracted — mainly because of inadequate notification in journals and databases and the use of stored copies.
- **Trusting AI-detectable fakes to be detectable**: A Northwestern/University of Chicago study found ChatGPT writes believable scientific abstracts that academics could not identify as AI-created.
- **False balance in media**: Wakefield should have been positioned as fringe; instead outlets platformed him and his celebrity amplifiers.
- **Letting a case study carry causal weight**: Wakefield's 1998 paper described twelve children with no randomization and no control group; a study of that nature cannot establish causation.
- **Slow retraction**: Delay lets misinformation entrench past the point where debunking works.

## Worked Example
The Wakefield zombie. In 1998 Andrew Wakefield led a paper in *The Lancet* claiming a link between the measles vaccine and autism. It was effectively a case study of twelve children — no randomization, no control group, a potentially highly biased sample, and structurally incapable of establishing causation. At a 1998 press conference (called by one science journalist one of the biggest PR disasters in medicine) Wakefield asserted a clear link anyway. The *Daily Mail*, *New York Times*, CNN, and *60 Minutes* amplified it; Jenny McCarthy took it to *Larry King Live* and *Oprah*, where she called the measles vaccine "the autism shot" and was hailed as a "mother warrior." Vaccination rates fell, outbreaks rose, children died. Journalist Brian Deer's investigation revealed the study was funded by lawyers acting for parents suing vaccine manufacturers — the "undisclosed goal" was to help sue the manufacturers — and that "no case was free of misreporting or alteration." Wakefield lost his medical licence in 2010; the General Medical Council found he acted "dishonestly and irresponsibly" with "callous disregard" for the child participants. *The Lancet* retracted the paper in 2010, twelve years on. And still: a 2015 survey found 21 percent of Americans under thirty believed vaccines cause autism, with the identical 21 percent figure appearing in an Alberta survey of nearly 3,000 people the same year. A 2021 study of the academic literature found post-retraction citation *increased* — 337 academic references from 2011 to 2017, roughly 62 percent of which failed to note the retraction, and only about half of those that did were clearly negative in tone. Matthew Motta's diagnosis: anti-vaccine activists continually reanimate the work, using the language of science — a prestigious journal, credible-sounding methods — to cast doubt on consensus, while folding the retraction itself into the narrative of a persecuted truth-teller. The fake science supplies science-y credibility; the retraction feeds the martyrdom story. Zombies are hard to kill. (Ivermectin for COVID and the retracted 2019 police-shootings study — condemned as "scientific malpractice" by 850+ co-signing academics, yet echoed by Donald Trump after George Floyd's murder — follow the same pattern.)

## Key Takeaways
1. Never accept a claim resting on one paper — ask for the body of evidence. This is the load-bearing habit of Part I.
2. Check retraction status before citing or sharing; assume databases and stored PDFs will not tell you.
3. Treat unusually exciting findings as elevated risk, and treat heavy post-retraction attention as expected, not anomalous.
4. Recognize the retraction-as-persecution move as motivated reasoning and do not argue it on its own terms.
5. Advocate for fast, two-stage retraction — validity first, blame second — and for red, unmissable retraction flags in databases and search results.
6. Expect AI to make fabricated science undetectable to experts; shift defence from detection of individual fakes to structural habits (body of evidence, source legitimacy, citation patterns).
7. Remember the stakes: roughly $2.5 trillion a year in global R&D flows into this swamp, and science co-opted as a certainty-affirming *brand* — rather than practised as a rigorous process — is now a core commodity of the information economy.
