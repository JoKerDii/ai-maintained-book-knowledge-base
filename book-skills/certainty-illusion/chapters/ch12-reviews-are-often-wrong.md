# Chapter 12: Reviews Are Often Wrong — and You Can't Spot the Fakes

## Core Idea
When online consumer ratings are checked against independent expert assessment or market reality, agreement is barely better than a coin flip — and it is worst exactly where the stakes are highest (physicians, cancer clinics). Worse, essentially nobody, including experts and detection algorithms, can reliably identify a modern fake review.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The accuracy test: reviews vs. independent expert vs. market**: Ask whether consumer star ratings actually predict quality. Compare them against a comparative expert body (Consumer Reports) and against an independent outcome measure (used-product resale price).
  - When to use: Deciding how much weight to give a rating on any product with objective, testable properties (cars, computers, appliances, electronics).
  - How: (1) Ask whether the attribute is objective or purely a matter of taste — there is no expert answer on beach-towel colour. (2) If objective, find a source doing *comparative* evaluation across the product class, not single-product scores. (3) Prefer sources that evaluate over time, not immediately after purchase.
  - Why it works: The two failures of consumer reviews are non-comparativeness (a reviewer scores one product in isolation) and timing (reviews are written before the reviewer has full experience with the product).
- **Knowledge asymmetry rule for expert services**: The more expertise separates you from the provider, the less your review tracks quality — and the less anyone's review should guide you.
  - When to use: Choosing physicians, lawyers, dentists, psychologists.
  - How: Separate *experience* (wait times, courtesy, communication — things patients can legitimately judge, and which matter) from *clinical quality* (which patients structurally cannot judge). Use reviews only for the first, and even then with caution.
  - Failure mode: Ratings reward likability and reward avoiding hard conversations. A physician who withholds an unwelcome truth may score higher than one who tells it.
- **Assume-you-can't-detect rule**: Treat your own fake-detection ability as near zero and act accordingly.
  - When to use: Always, before congratulating yourself on spotting a phony.
  - How: Still watch the legacy red flags — thin reviewer profiles, over-the-top headlines, no balance — but never conclude "the rest are real." Machine-generated reviews defeated tech-savvy users at rates indistinguishable from random guessing.

## Key Concepts
- **Illusory superiority**: The universal tendency to think unreliable information fools other people but not you.
- **Dunning-Kruger effect**: The worse you are at a skill, the less equipped you are to know it — applied here to lie detection.
- **Overconfidence in misinformation detection**: Three in four Americans overestimate their ability to sort credible from bunk, and the most overconfident are the most likely to spread falsehoods.
- **Fake negative review / one-starring**: Coordinated negative reviews used against competitors or for ideological punishment; ~50 fake negatives can be enough to overtake a competing hotel in visibility.
- **Review polarization for professionals**: Physicians are 64% more likely than other businesses to get five stars and 194% as likely to get one star.
- **Patient experience vs. clinical quality**: A distinct pair — a good experience can accompany a serious clinical error, and vice versa.
- **Consumer-oriented ethos in medicine**: The drift from "what is in the patient's best interest" toward "what makes the customer happy."
- **Goldilocks average as a manipulation tell**: Alternative cancer clinics averaged 4.4 stars — precisely the believably-good high end.
- **Neural machine translation fakes**: AI-generated reviews so plausible that detection rates fall to chance.

## Mental Models
- Think of a consumer star rating for an objective product as **a coin flip with a marketing budget**: higher-rated products beat Consumer Reports' pick only 57% of the time, and star ratings bore essentially no relationship to used prices.
- Use expert reviews when quality is *comparative and testable*; use consumer reviews only when the question is *taste and there is no expert answer*.
- Think of your fake-spotting confidence as **the bias itself, not a defence against it**.
- **The higher the stakes and the more desperate the search, the stronger the incentive to manipulate** — so trust ratings least exactly where you want them most.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using ratings to pick a physician**: 84% of surveyed Americans would not see a physician rated under four stars, yet studies consistently find no clear relationship between online physician ratings and clinical outcomes, and no prediction of peer-assessed clinical performance.
- **Believing awareness protects you**: 52% of surveyed Australians suspect they have fallen for fakes; 86% of German consumers still call reviews credible. Knowing does not immunize.
- **Reading glowing reviews of unproven treatments as evidence**: Alternative cancer clinics carry emotionally overwhelming testimonials; patients using alternative therapies for cancer have *worse* outcomes.
- **Assuming AI changes nothing**: It makes persuasive fakes cheaper, better, and undetectable. The correct response is more suspicion, not more filtering skill.

## Worked Example
The 2016 accuracy study Caulfield leans on: researchers took over 1,200 products and compared Amazon consumer star ratings against Consumer Reports scores, chosen for the organization's impartiality and technical expertise. Products with a higher Amazon rating scored higher with Consumer Reports only 57% of the time — barely above chance. The researchers then checked both against an independent market signal: used-product resale prices. Consumer Reports scores predicted resale value; average star ratings had essentially no relationship to it. So consumer reviewers agreed with neither the experts nor the marketplace. Co-author Donald Lichtenstein gave two mechanisms: consumers evaluate a single product in isolation while Consumer Reports evaluates it relative to the whole product class, and consumers review right after purchase, before they have lived with the thing. Caulfield's companion case is Marco Zenone's analysis of 1,000+ reviews across 39 alternative-medicine cancer clinics: a 4.4-star average, dominated by stage-four and terminal patients, for treatments with no supporting evidence.

## Key Takeaways
1. For objective products, replace star ratings with comparative expert testing — Consumer Reports, Wirecutter, video reviews — and check more than one.
2. Do not choose a professional by rating; use reviews at most for experience-level signals, and prefer peer or institutional measures for quality.
3. Assume you cannot detect fakes. Keep watching red flags, but never treat surviving reviews as verified.
4. Treat a "believably good" 4.2–4.5 average as neutral; treat a wall of emotional cure narratives as a warning sign.
5. Weight the review's timing: an immediate post-purchase rave says nothing about durability.
6. Negative-review campaigns are cheap and effective — a wave of one-stars can reflect ideology, not quality, and can kill a small business.
7. Caulfield's floor for professional reviews: don't rely on them; at minimum, know their limits and use them with caution.
