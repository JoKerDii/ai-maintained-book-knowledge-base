# Glossary — The Certainty Illusion

**Accuracy nudge** — A prompt shifting attention to accuracy before sharing; measurably raises the quality of news people share (Pennycook & Rand) (Ch 14)
**AI as "weapon of mass disruption"** — Eurasia Group's 2023 designation: content volume rising past most citizens' ability to sort fact from fiction (Ch 5)
**Anchoring effect** — An inflated average rating becomes the reference point pulling honest reviewers' own ratings upward (Ch 11)
**Animal magnetism** — Mesmer's 1779 magnets-and-magnetic-fluid pitch; the historical prototype of scienceploitation (Ch 1)
**Appeal to nature fallacy** — Presuming what is natural is inherently good, safe, or better; refuted by arsenic, venom, lightning (Ch 6)
**Attentional bias** — Focusing on some elements and ignoring others, obscuring alternative explanations (Ch 8)
**Backlash** — The "it's all so confusing, I give up" effect; conflicting health information causes outright rejection of new recommendations (Ch 10)
**Body-of-evidence test** — Judging a claim against the weight of existing literature and the scientific consensus rather than any single study (Ch 3, Ch 14)
**Bro-science** — Cherry-picked data, anecdote, and guru testimonial offered in place of real science (Ch 9)
**Catastrophizing** — Overstating the potential harms of emerging science; the mirror image of hype (Ch 3)
**Certainty grift** — Selling false certainty about both a virtuous goal and the path to it, monetizing the gap between wanting to do good and doing it (Ch 10)
**Certainty illusion** — The book's thesis: the more we seek certainty, the harder it becomes to find, because valued guideposts attract distortion (Ch 0)
**Certainty Six** — Caulfield's six personal strategies: pause for accuracy; watch for scienceploitation; resist hype; scrutinize goodness marketing; consult the body of evidence; like what you like (Ch 14)
**Chemophobia** — Fear of "chemicals," where the word has drifted from all physical matter to a stand-in for artificial and unsafe (Ch 6)
**Choice supportive bias** — Post-purchase rationalization; verified reviewers already paid, so they are motivated to report liking it (Ch 11)
**Clean beauty** — An undefined, circular market category promising safety without specifying a standard (Ch 7)
**Confirmation bias** — Finding, favouring, and remembering information that supports pre-existing belief (Ch 2, Ch 8)
**Devious Dozen** — Caulfield's list of pervasive health-halo terms: natural, holistic, healthy, organic, non-GMO, gluten-free, chemical-free, toxin-free, locally grown, the colour green, immune-boosting, personalized (Ch 6)
**Dose makes the poison** — Anything harms at some dose and is safe at a low enough one, making "toxin-free" content-free (Ch 6)
**Dunning-Kruger effect** — Overestimating one's knowledge because lacking knowledge impedes recognizing the lack (Ch 2, Ch 12)
**Faux research** — Frank Graves' term for flawed or fabricated polling that corrodes the public interest (Ch 13)
**Fake review cycle** — Business buys fakes, sells more, buys platform ads with the gain; platform, business, and faker all profit, only consumers lose (Ch 11)
**Fear marketing** — Selling by manufacturing anxiety about conventional products; the cosmetics industry's fallback (Ch 7)
**Galileo gambit** — Answering the weight of evidence with a persecution narrative: "they laughed at Galileo too" (Ch 2)
**Goldilocks zone (4.2–4.5 stars)** — The believably-good rating band that sophisticated fakers target, adding small complaints for credibility (Ch 11, Ch 12)
**Goodness bias** — Perceived moral worthiness warping judgment, evidence evaluation, and research conduct (Ch 8)
**Goodness illusion** — A messaging strategy making a choice feel like the right thing without close examination (Ch 0, Ch 6)
**Great flattening of knowledge** — A race to the bottom in which real science, worthy goals, and valued opinion become equally meaningless (Ch 0)
**Health halo** — A word or phrase creating an immediate impression of health or virtue that the evidence does not support (Ch 6)
**Hijacked journal** — Criminal cloning of a legitimate journal's name and domain to harvest publication fees; fraud, distinct from predatory publishing (Ch 4)
**Hot stuff bias** — Grant applications converging on whatever field is currently fundable (Ch 3)
**Hype** — Exaggerated claims of potential benefit and social impact, generated at every stage of the knowledge pipeline (Ch 3)
**Illusory superiority** — Believing fakes fool other people but not you (Ch 12)
**Impact merchants** — How senior academics describe themselves; "marketizing" research as a survival requirement (Ch 3)
**Incentivized review** — A review paid or discounted into existence; inflates ratings 40–70%; FTC requires disclosure (Ch 11)
**Incidental similarities** — Non-political common ground that increases closeness and openness to opposing political views (Ch 13)
**Infodemic** — The WHO's term for harmful misinformation spreading like an infectious disease (Ch 0)
**Intention-behaviour gap** — Wanting to do good without converting it into behaviour; only ~2.7% of Americans live a healthy lifestyle (Ch 10)
**Knowledge asymmetry rule** — The more expertise separates you from a provider, the less any review tracks actual quality (Ch 12)
**Knowledge production crisis** — Degradation of the methods and institutions that make knowledge, distinct from downstream misinformation (Ch 0)
**Literature pollution** — Fake and low-quality papers entering databases, then systematic reviews, guidelines, and policy (Ch 4, Ch 5)
**Marketing placebo effect** — Price and brand altering measured experience; pricier wine tastes better (Ch 11)
**Masculinity as a health halo** — "Manly" functioning as an unargued proxy for good, lending certainty to unproven interventions (Ch 9)
**Motivated reasoning** — Selecting evidence and arguments to endorse a preferred outcome (Ch 5, Ch 8)
**Motte-and-bailey fallacy** — Advancing a weak controversial claim, then retreating to an unobjectionable truism when challenged (Ch 7)
**Negativity bias** — Encoding and remembering negative information more strongly (Ch 1)
**Paper mill** — A multi-million-dollar industry selling authorship on publications with faked or poor-quality data (Ch 5)
**Pareidolia** — Seeing meaningful patterns in random data; amplified by prior belief (Ch 8)
**Post-retraction citation** — Continued citation of retracted work; only 5.4% of such citations acknowledge the retraction (Ch 5)
**Precarious manhood principle** — Men devaluing feminine-coded brand names to defend masculinity (Ch 11)
**Predatory journal** — A publication charging author fees with lax or nonexistent peer review; est. 11,000–16,000 exist (Ch 4)
**Pseudoarchaeology** — Origin myths dressed as research; the ancient alien narrative and its extremist linkages (Ch 8)
**Pseudo-profound bullshit** — Syntactically coherent but meaningless word salad deployed to impress through vagueness (Ch 2)
**Publish or perish** — The pressure regime creating the market for predatory journals, paper mills, and salami science (Ch 4, Ch 14)
**Retraction attention paradox** — Retracted papers drawing *more* attention over ten years than never-retracted ones (Ch 5)
**Sagan standard** — Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence; scale required proof to improbability (Ch 2)
**Salami science** — Slicing one project into many small papers to manufacture apparent productivity (Ch 14)
**Science washing** — Deploying "science" as a generic legitimizing badge — graphs, numbers, jargon (Michelle Wong) (Ch 1)
**Science-y** — Sounding scientific without being scientific; jargon as celebrity endorsement (Ch 1)
**Science-y / scary dyad** — Science-sounding jargon plus a fear message; the most durable misinformation engine (Ch 1)
**Scienceploitation** — Using scientific verbiage and hot research topics to market unproven or harmful products and agendas (Ch 0, Ch 1)
**Scientism** — Inappropriately deferential attitude to science; treating it as inherently good and infallible (Ch 1)
**Scientific consensus** — Solid pieces of evidence all pointing the same direction; emerges slowly, iteratively, unglamorously (Ch 14)
**Single study syndrome** — Treating one study as decisive, especially when it flatters priors or supplies a contrarian thrill (Ch 5, Ch 14)
**Social influence bias** — Prior positive reviews raising subsequent ratings via positive herding (+32% likelihood, +25% final ratings) (Ch 11)
**Spin** — Distortion in the reporting of results; ~40% of biomedical abstracts contain some (Ch 3)
**Spiral of silence** — Withholding opinions believed to be in the minority, so visible consensus silences dissent (Ch 13)
**Substitution risk** — Replacing a well-studied ingredient with a poorly studied "natural" one is information loss, not a safety gain (Ch 7)
**Super contributor** — A high-volume reviewer for whom reviewing is identity and brand; disproportionately influential (Ch 11)
**Transportation** — Narrative immersion producing emotional investment that persists after viewing (Ch 10)
**Vanity / agenda-driven journal** — A purpose-built pretend journal created to legitimize a predetermined position (Ch 4)
**White hat bias** — Distortion of research-based information in the service of perceived righteous ends (Allison & Cope) (Ch 8)
**Wishful thinking** — The perception traps that let us construct the certainty we want (Ch 8)
**Zombie science** — Discredited or retracted findings that keep circulating and influencing debate after correction (Ch 5)
