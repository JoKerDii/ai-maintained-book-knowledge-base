# Introduction

## Core Idea
The useful story about AI is not the accelerationist-vs-doomer argument about the future — it is the record of practitioners already using AI to fix concrete, load-bearing problems today. Judge AI by what working people build with it, not by what industry factions predict.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Hillis's Reframe — "Try to imagine the tech without the tech companies"**: Danny Hillis's answer to the question "what is AI actually good for?" Strip the vendors, funding narratives, and ideology out of the frame and look only at practitioners and outcomes.
  - When to use: whenever a technology's discourse is dominated by the people who profit from it, and you can't tell capability from marketing.
  - How: (1) ignore product launches, valuations, and forecasts; (2) find people outside the industry who adopted the tool to solve a problem they already had; (3) ask what specifically broke, what they built, and what measurably changed; (4) generalize capability from those cases, not from claims.
  - Why it works: practitioners have no incentive to overstate — their problem either got solved or it didn't. Failure mode: survivorship bias — the counterculture cases you find are the ones that worked, so you must separately account for the failures (see Ch 1).

- **Perna's "See Myself" Principle**: General Gustave Perna's operating rule from Operation Warp Speed — before you can act on a boundaryless problem, you need a real-time, standardized picture of your own position, supplies, and dependencies.
  - When to use: at the start of any large coordination effort spanning organizations that don't share data formats.
  - How: (1) enumerate every actor whose state affects the outcome (agencies, manufacturers, hospitals, truckers, dry ice makers); (2) reduce complexity by tracing one critical physical input end to end (Perna used plastic — syringes, vials, bags); (3) pipe all streams into one standardized interface; (4) use the shared view as the arbiter in disputes, not assertion.
  - Why it works: it converts arguments into lookups. Failure mode: a dashboard built on unstandardized or stale data manufactures false confidence.

- **The Definition-First Vendor Test**: Perna gave each consultant an hour; hiring went to the first team that could state the problem precisely, not the one with the flashiest solution. Palantir's Julie and Aaron passed because they described the deliverable — data sufficient to assess, determine risk, and decide rapidly.
  - When to use: evaluating vendors or partners in an unfamiliar technical domain.
  - How: ask each candidate to define your problem back to you before any demo; disqualify anyone who leads with capability instead of diagnosis.

## Key Concepts
- **Accelerationists**: the camp arguing AI's benefits (cured diseases, mitigated climate change, universal knowledge assistants) justify moving as fast as possible, partly to stay ahead of China.
- **Doomers**: the camp arguing AI risks catastrophe — superviruses, mass job loss, authoritarian entrenchment — and must be slowed to a responsible crawl.
- **p(doom)**: the doomers' numeric shorthand for the probability AI causes human extinction, traded casually as a social signal.
- **Predictive AI / classifiers / optimizers / generative AI**: the four working families of AI — forecasting behavior from patterns; labeling and sorting (tumors, email); split-second logistics and pricing decisions; and creating text, images, video, code.
- **Artificial general intelligence (AGI)**: a theoretical synthesis of those capabilities that would surpass human intellect across nearly every domain.
- **Speed, not sentience**: the actual common thread across AI types — they process information far faster than humans, and with fewer doubts.
- **Operation Warp Speed**: the US COVID vaccine development and distribution program Perna ran starting May 2020, initially with three colonels and no plan.
- **"God view"**: Perna's term for the unified real-time dashboard Palantir assembled from hundreds of public and private data streams.
- **AI counterculture**: Tyrangiel's term for the quiet, mostly non-technical practitioners using AI on problems that matter, outside the industry's attention.

## Mental Models
- **Think of AI as a puppy that will read the Quran in Portuguese and eat the TV remote** — extraordinary and useless in unpredictable alternation. Plan for weirdness now; assume the weirdness declines steadily.
- **Use the defensive crouch test when you catch yourself avoiding a technology**: crouching doesn't stop the hit, it only guarantees you're looking the other way when it lands.
- **Think of "AI" as a family resemblance, not a category** — there is no objective threshold for what counts, so argue about capabilities and outcomes, never about whether something "is really AI."
- **Use motive-mapping before trusting any AI forecast**: researchers holding university chairs plus corporate checks, doomers earning consulting fees from the firms they criticize, and founders with rival startups all have skin in their predictions.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Move fast and break things"**: smashing is fast because you don't need to understand a thing to destroy it — and you never learn who was still standing inside the system you broke.
- **Trusting confident forecasters who can't cite a concrete near-term example**: most doomers and accelerationists genuinely believed AI was transformative but struggled to name how it would touch an ordinary person's life soon. Certainty without an example is a tell.
- **Buying the McConaughey version of AI**: hype that is gorgeous, incomprehensible, future-tense, and detached from daily life — the tell is that benefits accrue mostly to people who are already rich.
- **Assuming public benefit by default**: unlike the Manhattan Project or the space race, US AI is a private enterprise; McKinsey Global Institute estimates $4.4T in annual corporate profits from generative AI alone, Morgan Stanley another $40T in operational efficiencies. Distribution is not automatic.

## Worked Example
**General Gustave Perna, Operation Warp Speed, May 2020.** A month from retirement after forty years in the Army — "an average infantry officer" and "a great logistician" by his own account — Perna was called to Washington to distribute the first COVID vaccines. He arrived with three colonels and no plan, facing a battlefield with no boundaries: state and federal agencies, drug companies, hospitals, pharmacies, manufacturers, truckers, dry ice makers. His stated need was to "see myself." The concrete version of that problem was plastic — without live visibility into national capacity for syringes, needles, bags, and vials, he could end up with hundreds of millions of doses and nothing to put them in, while thousands died daily.

Washington consultants queued up; Perna gave each an hour and none could even define the problem. Palantir's Julie and Aaron promised data sufficient to assess, determine risk, and decide rapidly, and were hired on the spot. They optimized hundreds of government and private data streams into a single interface. Within weeks Perna had his "God view"; months later Operation Warp Speed delivered vaccines to all fifty states simultaneously. When governors called claiming they'd been shorted, Perna shared a screen showing the exact vial count in their possession. A YouTube video of him describing this had only a few thousand views — which is the book's point about where the real AI story is being told.

## Key Takeaways
1. When a field's discourse is captured by its vendors, go find the practitioners — imagine the tech without the tech companies.
2. Hire the party who can define your problem, not the one who can demo hardest.
3. Reduce a boundaryless coordination problem to one traceable physical input to make it legible.
4. Map incentives before weighing predictions; brilliance and financial interest coexist comfortably in both AI camps.
5. Expect AI to get easier, faster, and less strange every day; expect people and institutions to resist, because change threatens habits, relationships, and traditions.
6. The choice is not AI or no AI — it's AI shaped by people who care about your problems versus people who don't. Shaping it is enlightened self-interest, not naive optimism.
7. Look for the practitioner profile the book keeps finding: quiet, practical, no prior software expertise, stuck on a problem conventional methods couldn't solve, and stubborn past the point of reason.
