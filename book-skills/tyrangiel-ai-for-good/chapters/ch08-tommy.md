# Chapter 8: Tommy

*(Part 2 — "Physician, Heal Thyself" — Chapter 1)*

## Core Idea
Tomas "Tommy" Mihaljevic, cardiac surgeon and CEO of Cleveland Clinic, runs AI adoption as an anti-hype discipline: standardize what is indisputably best practice so the organization has capacity for what surprises it, and never let AI become the mission.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Mihaljevic's Standardize-to-Innovate Rule**: "In order to innovate, you have to standardize the things that are indisputably the best practice."
  - When to use: deploying new technology inside a high-stakes operation where failure is measured in lives.
  - How: (1) Regiment processes to produce uniformity, transparency, and accountability. (2) Freeze the settled best practices so they consume no attention. (3) Spend the freed capacity on the unpredictable. (4) Apply the identical logic to AI — it is one more process to be regimented, not an exception to process.
  - Why it works: it converts a chaotic environment into a smaller surface of genuine uncertainty. Failure mode: standardizing things that aren't actually settled, freezing in mediocrity.

- **Mihaljevic's Two Goals Test**: an AI project qualifies only if it (a) improves patient outcomes or (b) cuts administrative cost that can be plowed back into access and care. No third category, no strategic-optionality projects.
  - When to use: triaging an inbound flood of AI proposals and vendor pitches.
  - How: state the reason your institution exists, then reject any AI initiative that doesn't serve it. Cleveland Clinic exists to treat complex conditions only it can treat — so it will not try to become "the Microsoft of health care."
  - Why it works: identity discipline pre-kills the projects that look exciting and serve nothing.

- **The Narrow-Partner Strategy**: attack internally identified problems with AI partners who have "a relatively narrow field of focus" — outsiders to medicine, or small scrappy AI firms that need Cleveland Clinic more than it needs them.
  - How: (1) Find the problem inside the institution first; never start from a vendor's capability. (2) Prefer partners with asymmetric dependence on you. (3) Deploy AI around your existing critical processes, not the reverse. (4) Green-light nothing without a lengthy pilot. (5) Require clinicians to work directly with technologists on design, test, and implementation.

## Key Concepts
- **God complex**: Ernest Jones's 1913 term for pathological belief in one's own infallibility, classically associated with surgeons and CEOs — the misdiagnosis the chapter opens by ruling out.
- **AI as magic dust**: the belief you sprinkle AI over a business and everything becomes perfect; Mihaljevic's name for the industry's dominant delusion.
- **Administrative burden**: 25–30% of US health-care costs are administrative — roughly $5B a year at Cleveland Clinic's scale, and the single largest AI prize on the table.
- **"Thousand flowers bloom"**: Mihaljevic's description of the decentralized experimentation his approach licenses across the organization.
- **The volunteer entrepreneur**: the person Mihaljevic says to look for — a doctor or nurse with little or no technology background who takes on AI experiments *on top of* an already brutal schedule.
- **Professionally obliged**: his framing of AI adoption as a duty, not an option, wherever it could produce better outcomes.
- **Caregiver orientation**: the eight-week program every one of the 5,000 Abu Dhabi hires went through — culture standardized before operations began.

## Mental Models
- Think of AI as **infrastructure that must fit around your critical processes**, not a platform your processes reorganize around. A hospital that becomes a passive customer has inverted the relationship.
- Use **"jump at the jobs no one else wants"** when you're behind. Mihaljevic took an internship with years more surgical experience than his supervisors, pioneered robotic cardiac surgery when nobody would scrub with him, and built a 4.4M-square-foot desert hospital knowing nothing about construction or the Middle East.
- Screen collaborators for **impatience** — entrepreneurial spirits are impatient by definition, and impatience is the trait that survives an institution's bureaucracy.
- Treat **skepticism of grand designs** as earned realism, not pessimism. A career of being tossed by randomness (war, exile, a residency denied for not being native Swiss) is what makes best-case scenarios untrustworthy.

## Anti-patterns
- **Enthusiasm-as-expertise**: finding technically keen internal staff and setting them loose to build products. Mihaljevic's verdict on his own institution: not one product anyone ever used.
- **The summit-meeting fallacy**: executives meet counterparts at Microsoft or Google, name five collaboration areas, and nothing happens — "people believe the meeting is the point. No one owns the initiative."
- **Rebranding as an AI company**: health-care CEOs across pharma, hospitals, devices, and software declaring themselves AI companies, which abandons the actual reason patients show up.
- **Starting from the technology rather than the problem**: the inversion that produces demos instead of care.

## Worked Example
**Tomas Mihaljevic → Cleveland Clinic's AI operating model.** Raised four-to-a-one-bedroom in Croatia; worked six months as a nursing assistant at the University of Zagreb med school to afford a copy of *Harrison's Principles of Internal Medicine*. Graduated on the eve of the war for independence, chose exile, learned German in three months for a Swiss job — and arrived in Zurich to find colleagues speaking Swiss German. Four years later he was denied a residency for not being native Swiss. Reached the US in 1999 and took the unwanted jobs: intern at Brigham and Women's, then robotic cardiac surgery at Cleveland Clinic, then Abu Dhabi, where he built the international facility and recruited 5,000 employees from 80 countries. Became CEO in 2018.

Exposure to AI came early because the UAE made it a national imperative while he lived there in the mid-2010s. As CEO he refused the "we are now an AI company" move, defined the two-goal test, catalogued the two standard failure modes (internal-enthusiast product teams; executive summits with no owner), and replaced them with internally sourced problems, narrow-focus partners, mandatory long pilots, and clinician–technologist co-design. The result he claims: a playground open to anyone in the organization willing to volunteer, with a thousand flowers blooming — and an explicit invitation to the author to go find them.

## Key Takeaways
1. Name your reason to exist first; use it to kill AI projects that don't serve it.
2. Standardize settled best practice specifically to buy attention for the unexpected.
3. Reduce your AI portfolio to two acceptable payoffs: better outcomes, or admin cost recovered into care.
4. Source the problem internally, then find the partner — never the reverse.
5. Prefer partners who need you more than you need them, and who focus narrowly.
6. Assign an owner or the initiative is dead; a meeting is not an initiative.
7. Recruit volunteers, not appointees — the people who take on AI work on top of a full clinical load are the signal.
