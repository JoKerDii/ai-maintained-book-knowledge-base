# Chapter 10: Twenty-Dollar Burgers in a Haunted House

## Core Idea
When you deploy AI inside a legacy institution, the scarce resource is not the technology or the use case — it's the handful of insiders willing to stake their reputation on making it work. Find those people first; the problems will follow.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Chandra's People-Before-Problems Rule**: identifying opportunities for AI matters less than identifying people.
  - When to use: entering any large, change-resistant organization (hospital, government, utility, incumbent enterprise) as an outsider tasked with modernization.
  - How: (1) map the institution's personalities and hierarchies before proposing anything; (2) find problems AI can actually solve, not merely demo against; (3) match each problem to a clinician/operator partner who will absorb the frustration; (4) work exclusively with that short list and stop courting everyone else.
  - Why it works: in an org with fixed reimbursement and 10-week meeting latency, an unsponsored project dies of scheduling attrition, not technical failure. Failure mode: mistaking enthusiasm in a first meeting for commitment — the same person shows up to the follow-up asking what the meeting is about.

- **The "Live and Die With This" Test**: Chandra's screen for a real partner versus a tourist.
  - When to use: before committing engineering resources to any internal pilot.
  - How: ask directly whether the person will put their neck on the line. Look for someone who can meet your eye and commit to driving it personally. If the answer is hedged, curious, or enthusiastic-but-conditional, classify it as entertainment and move on.
  - Why it works: it converts a vague culture problem into a binary staffing decision you can make in one conversation.

- **The "Thousand Flowers Bloom" Impresario Role** (Mihaljevic's strategy, executed by Chandra): the digital leader's job is cross-pollination, not deployment — pairing internal clinical problem-owners with external technical builders, repeatedly.

## Key Concepts
- **Chief digital officer as outsider hire**: Cleveland Clinic deliberately recruited from Silicon Valley rather than health care, prioritizing disruption over domain expertise.
- **Default outcome is failure**: Chandra's stated baseline expectation for changing a hundred-year-old institution — adopted as a planning assumption, not pessimism.
- **Change management debt**: the accumulated organizational friction (scheduling, forgotten context, no urgency) that makes even cost-cutting, care-improving proposals stall.
- **Fixed reimbursement squeeze**: Medicare/Medicaid rates are largely fixed and private insurers negotiate their own, so hospitals cannot raise prices to match cost inflation.
- **Operating margin gap**: in 2023 nearly 40% of US hospitals operated at a loss (KFF); the median "winner" ran a 2% operating margin.
- **Amityville awareness**: Chandra's shorthand for clinicians who already know the economics are unsustainable — the precondition for urgency.
- **Resumé-building vs. big-swing phase**: the career stage where you no longer optimize for credential accumulation and can accept high-failure-rate work.

## Mental Models
- Think of institutional AI adoption as a **haunted-house problem**: you can see the house is haunted; your job is convincing others to leave, not proving the ghost exists.
- Use the **less-than-ten heuristic** when scoping a rollout: in an 80,000-person organization, assume roughly half a dozen true partners exist. Design the program around that number rather than around headcount.
- Think of a health system's finances as **an industry headed off a cliff with no emergency meeting scheduled** — urgency must be imported by individuals, because the system will not generate it.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Move fast and break things" / "seek permission, not forgiveness"**: Chandra treats these as after-the-fact justifications for immature behavior; in a hospital they burn the political capital you need for the next pilot.
- **Judging non-participants**: labeling the other 79,990 people as obstacles. Chandra's stance — "It's not my job to judge. They do what they do" — keeps the door open and preserves relationships.
- **Deploying AI without a clinical owner**: technology deployed into an ownerless workflow decays into an unused feature.
- **Assuming a first meeting equals momentum**: without an owner, the follow-up takes ten weeks and starts from zero.

## Worked Example
Rohit Chandra spent 2004–2017 at Yahoo!, rising from Yahoo! Mail engineer to one of its indispensable top technologists across seven CEOs, then Verizon, then advising Silicon Valley start-ups. Recruited on the pitch that Cleveland Clinic wanted a technologist explicitly from outside health care, he was named chief digital officer in 2022 after six months of interviews, splitting weeks between Cleveland and Los Altos to keep venture-funded AI health tech aimed at problems the clinic actually had. He arrived with no health-care knowledge and no peers. His first lesson was economic: US health-care spending hit $4.9 trillion in 2023, up 7.5%, while 2021–2023 inflation of 12.4% far outpaced reimbursement growth — hospitals absorbed the difference. His second was organizational: an early meeting on an AI use case that would both improve care and cut costs took ten weeks to reschedule, and the department head arrived asking what the meeting was about. Chandra's response was to stop broadcasting and start recruiting — fewer than ten people in an 80,000-person institution, roughly half a dozen go-to partners with "that fight in them." Pressed for a name, he offered one: Eric Boose — grounded, objective, willing to call a spade a spade.

## Key Takeaways
1. Recruit your internal champions before you scope your use cases; the champion determines which use case is viable.
2. Screen partners with a commitment question, not an interest question — separate "I will drive this" from "this is fascinating."
3. Set the base rate honestly: assume failure is the default and decide whether the swing is still worth taking.
4. Expect fewer than ten real partners in a large institution, and build a program that a handful of people can carry.
5. Ground the case for AI in the institution's survival math (margins, reimbursement, inflation), not in the technology's capabilities.
6. Hire outsiders for pattern-breaking, but pair them with insiders for navigation — an outsider without clinical partners is a stalled meeting invite.
