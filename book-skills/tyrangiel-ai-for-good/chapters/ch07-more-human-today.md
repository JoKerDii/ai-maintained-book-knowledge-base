# Chapter 7: More Human Today

*(Part 1, Chapter 6 — closes the education section)*

## Core Idea
AI does not transform a classroom; a teacher willing to redesign the lesson around AI does. The measurable wins at Hobart came from teachers who changed *when and how* Khanmigo entered the workflow — not from the tool itself.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Richter's Confidence-First Sequencing** (Alaina Richter, 4th-grade math, Liberty Elementary): use the AI tutor to *affirm* mastery, not to drag students toward it.
  - When to use: any cohort where the tool works for high performers and everyone else disengages or "bullies the bot."
  - How: (1) Teach and test the unit yourself for a few weeks with direct instruction. (2) Only then assign the AI practice unit. (3) Give a fixed window ("two weeks to finish this unit"). (4) Make the AI session an event, not a remediation. Richter branded it "Math Madness."
  - Why it works: struggling students arrive with enough grounding to ask the AI a real question. Failure mode: front-loading the AI as first contact turns it into an answer-mining machine and a confidence sink.

- **Higgason's Three-Act Scaffold** (Melissa Higgason, HS chem/physics): use the AI to generate the activity, but keep authorship of structure, pace, and tone.
  - When to use: converting a passive assessment (a quiz, a lecture) into an active lab, fast — same day, not next summer.
  - How: (1) Tell the AI the *assessment objective* (e.g. "assess principles of stoichiometry"), ask for active alternatives, and list the physical materials you already have. (2) Take its idea, then impose your own act structure with a human checkpoint gating each act — instruct the AI: when a student reaches understanding, tell them to grab me. (3) Enter the tool's student mode and delete prompts that race ahead of where you want the class to land. (4) Accept partial drift — some students will get further than planned.
  - Why it works: the AI supplies variation and per-group patience; the teacher supplies sequencing and judgment, which is what AI is worst at.

- **Atherton's Modeled-Failure Writing Loop** (Matt Atherton, 7th-grade LA): break the writing coach into steps and have the AI produce both "awesome" and "not awesome" versions of topic sentences and argument statements, so students calibrate against contrast. Require students to adopt at least three AI suggestions, revise, and justify their choices in an exit ticket.

- **Buffington's Bottle-It Method** (Supt. Peggy Buffington): scan performance data for anomalous classrooms (Richter's class at the 69th percentile while peers sat in the teens), interrogate what that teacher actually did differently, then put that teacher in front of far more senior colleagues in district PD.

## Key Concepts
- **Dynamic action bubbles**: AI-generated multiple-choice menus offered in response to Khanmigo's own Socratic question, so a stuck student can pick rather than compose.
- **Productive struggle**: getting a student's half-formed thought into the chatbot at all — Vicki Zubovic's bar for success, deliberately set low.
- **"idk" problem**: student chats stay short and answer-mining; many won't even type "I don't know."
- **Differentiation machine**: Khanmigo's teacher-side trajectory — rubrics, unpacked standards, exit tickets, and eventually custom tutoring per struggling student.
- **Exit ticket**: quick informal end-of-class assessment; a heavy AI-generated teacher tool.
- **Credit recovery**: retaking failed courses for graduation credit — a Hobart dean with 30-year-stale geometry solved problems alongside a crying student *using* Khanmigo.
- **Lesson hook tool**: the Khanmigo feature Higgason used to improvise the airbag lab overnight.
- **Rubric**: scoring guide breaking an assignment into grading criteria; sharing it measurably improves student work.

## Mental Models
- Think of the AI tutor as **a colleague with no patience to exhaust** (Atherton's phrase) — assign it the first wave of feedback so the human handles meaning, not grammar.
- Use **"maybe I'm the problem"** as the diagnostic when engagement dies. Higgason's response to bombing a lecture was to redesign it, not to blame phones.
- Treat **teacher adoption as the product**, not student adoption. Khan built a jetpack for students; teachers flew it first, and Khanmigo went from a few teacher tools to nearly thirty.
- **Growth numbers are not impact data.** It takes ~3 years of broad use to know if ed software works.

## Anti-patterns
- **Expecting AI to fix motivation**: as eighth-grader Stella put it, some kids don't want to learn and nothing makes them. Sal Khan agrees there's no simple answer for engagement.
- **Dropping the tool into the existing lesson flow unchanged**: direct instruction → guided practice → AI practice produced shutdown and bot-bullying until Richter reordered it.
- **Requiring students to invent their own questions cold**: they mine for answers instead. Hence speech-to-text, action bubbles, and teachers literally taping AI-generated question cheat sheets to desks.
- **Mandating tools without funding and PD**: Buffington lobbied the state for permanent funding and ran PD for hundreds of teachers *before* insisting on use.
- **Judging the recursion**: teachers using AI to help kids use an AI built to teach them looks absurd; Zubovic's ruling is that only practice volume matters — "We can't be picky."

## Worked Example
**Melissa Higgason, Hobart HS chemistry/physics.** Told the night before that a journalist would visit, she scrapped a planned stoichiometry quiz. She fed Khanmigo's lesson hook tool her assessment goal plus her on-hand materials — Ziploc bags, vinegar, baking soda. Khanmigo returned an airbag design challenge. Higgason wrapped it in her own structure: students became engineers at a fictional "SafeRide Innovations," and in three gated acts (1) computed ideal airbag volume, (2) calculated grams of NaHCO₃ needed to produce that volume of CO₂ against excess vinegar, (3) performed the inflation live for the class. She loaded coaching instructions into Khanmigo, told it to send students to her at each checkpoint, and pruned prompts that would have exposed the ideal gas law too early (her strongest students got there regardless). She never returned to the front of the room — she logged 20,000 steps in-building, circulating between lab tables. Her verdict: the quiz would have produced silence; "AI made this class more human today." Cost: one evening of prep and grocery-aisle consumables.

Elsewhere in the same district: Richter's Confidence-First Sequencing tripled both scores and practice time in a 4th-grade math class.

## Key Takeaways
1. Change the *position* of the AI in your workflow before changing the AI. Sequencing beat configuration in every Hobart success story.
2. Keep authorship of pace, tone, and structure; delegate patience, volume, and first-pass feedback.
3. Prompt the AI with your *assessment objective plus your physical constraints* — that's what turned a quiz into a lab.
4. Build human checkpoints into AI-run activities; instruct the AI to hand the student back to you at a defined threshold.
5. Find your outlier practitioner, diagnose what they changed, and let them teach the veterans.
6. Accept that adoption compresses the improvement cycle from "next summer" to "next period" — Higgason retaught the same formula the same day, differently.
7. Expect the revolution to be piecemeal and quiet, carried by teachers trusted to experiment — not by tool replacement.
