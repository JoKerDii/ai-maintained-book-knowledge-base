# Chapter 10 (IX): Tactic #6 — Make It All About the Headline

## Core Idea
In a one-off world, the headline is everything — it must win a "Pick Me!" fight against infinite competitors. Headlines are built to *sell* the article, not represent it. So write the headline for the blogger and let them steal it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Headlines Sell, Not Represent**: outside subscription, a headline's job is to win attention against every other blog/story (and email, chat, video, porn). "On a blog, every page is the front page." Exaggeration, lies, and CAPS tags (EXCLUSIVE, EXTRA, UNPRECEDENTED, PHOTOS) are the creative solution — same as the yellow press.
- **"Naked little creatures that have to go out and fight on their own"** (Gabriel Snyder): each headline competes head-to-head; the test is not "Was this accurate?" but "Was it clicked more than the others?"
- **Write the Headline for Them**: like getting something from your parents at twelve — hint at the headline in your email/press release and let them think they came up with it. "Make it so obvious and enticing they can't pass it up… make them tone it down." They'll be so happy they won't check if it's true.

## Key Concepts
- **Google doesn't laugh**: Google News/search sends billions of clicks; algorithms reward literal, extreme, SEO-friendly headlines over witty ones. Yahoo tests 45,000 headline/photo combinations every five minutes.
- **A/B optimization for clicks, not accuracy**: HuffPost brags it tests which headlines "work best" — meaning most-clicked, in 32-point font.
- **The buffer of subscription**: subscription headlines only compete with articles inside the same publication; the reader already paid, so the headline writer's job is easy. Online there's no buffer.

## Mental Models
- **"Pick Me! Pick Me!"** — picture your story screaming against 500 others on Google News ("All 522 news articles"); it must be the loudest and most extreme to stand out.
- **The reader gets the buyer's remorse**: the click already happened; whether the reader gets anything is irrelevant.
- Headlines are the point of sale — **"that's where you make the sale."**

## Anti-patterns
- **Accurate, subtle, or witty headlines**: they lose the click fight (Andrew Malcolm proudly brags about "Hillary Clinton Shot a Duck Once"). Reasonable framing is a competitive disadvantage.
- **Trusting the CAPS "UNPRECEDENTED" tag**: the thing is almost always "not only not unprecedented but hilariously pedestrian."

## Worked Example
Contrast two headlines. The subscription-era NYT ran the Pentagon Papers under: "Vietnam Archive: A Consensus to Bomb Developed Before '64 Election, Study Says" — calm, circumspect, and it still reached everyone because the truth was enough. The author, by contrast, conned Jezebel into "Exclusive: American Apparel's Rejected Halloween Costume Ideas (American Appalling)" for a nonevent — the "leak" was fake (extra photos an employee sent over) — and it did nearly 100,000 pageviews. Same medium a century apart; only the one-off model differs. As Upton Sinclair recalled, Hearst's "War Declared!" newsboy headline actually read "War (may be) Declared (soon)."

## Key Takeaways
1. Headlines are sales pitches competing against everything; accuracy is not the metric.
2. Pre-write the headline and let the blogger claim it — they'll skip fact-checking.
3. Algorithms (Google, Yahoo) reward extremity and literalism over wit.
4. Remove the buffer (subscription) and headlines run to the same extremes as 1898.
