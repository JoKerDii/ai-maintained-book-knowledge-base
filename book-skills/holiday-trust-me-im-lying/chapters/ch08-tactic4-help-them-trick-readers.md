# Chapter 8 (VII): Tactic #4 — Help Them Trick Their Readers

## Core Idea
Blogs profit by tricking readers — with loaded-question headlines and pageview mazes designed to extract "engagement." Exploit the blogger's private ambivalence about deceiving people: hand them a way to do it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Question-Mark Headline**: "get the whole story into the headline but leave out just enough that people will want to click" (Brian Moylan). A question lets a blogger publish a false statement without being "wrong" — they were "only asking." ("Did Glenn Beck Rape and Murder a Young Girl in 1990?")
  - Denton's memo: don't dismiss a dubious claim with a skeptical headline — "nobody will get to your main argument"; set up a mystery, explain after the link. A question draws twice the response of an exclamation point.
  - The author's corollary: **remove the question mark and the headline becomes a lie.** The answer is always "No, of course not."
- **Feed Them Rhetorical Questions**: as an official comment, don't fully address the issue (leave room to speculate); as a fake tipster, ask leading questions ("Could [preposterous misreading] be what's going on?") and watch them become the click-friendly headline. Play the skeptic about your own client so bloggers will too.
- **The Pageview Maze (manufactured "engagement")**: registration, CAPTCHAs, confirmation pages, rating widgets, correction forms — each an extra pageview with more ads. "Engagement" means the reader is the sucker generating free pageviews.

## Key Concepts
- **Practical utility is a traffic *liability***: reviews, tutorials, recipes, technical analysis are popular with the initial audience but spread poorly — they're thorough (nothing left to debate) and hard to snark on.
- **Being final = being unprofitable**: authoritative, helpful, reasonable, or "waiting for the whole story" posts don't bait engagement. Exclamation points (finality) are avoided.
- **Users as switching circuitry** (Venkatesh Rao): "social media… is a set of embedding mechanisms to allow technologies to use humans to communicate with each other."

## Mental Models
- **The vicious cycle of escalation**: by screwing the reader (and getting screwed by the manipulator), each headline must be more irresistible/inflammatory than the last.
- **"Whoever makes things the most inconvenient for you wins"** (Richard Greenblatt) — the maze is deliberate.
- Low consequences make the trick safe: "What's infamy matter if you can keep your fortune?" (Juvenal).

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing the headline is a lure to a "nuanced, fairer article"**: the author read the articles — "they're rarely any better than the headline." That belief is a lie bloggers tell themselves; exploit it.
- **Confusing "user engagement" with a good thing**: in practice it's a ten-pageview obstacle course to leave one comment.

## Worked Example
The author's routine for getting Gawker to cover a client: give an official comment that *leaves the issue open*, and/or send a fake tip full of rhetorical questions — "Do you think that [juicy scandal] is what they're hiding?" The blogger poses those exact questions to readers in a headline. The author even says nasty things about his own client so the blogger will echo them on the front page. "I trick the bloggers, and they trick their readers." Everyone (blogger, manipulator, attention-seeking client) wins except the reader.

## Key Takeaways
1. Question-mark headlines are lies with legal cover — answer is always "no."
2. Feed rhetorical questions; they resurface as the headline.
3. Useful, final, reasonable content is punished by the engagement economy.
4. "Engagement" is a pageview-extraction scheme, not a conversation.
