# Chapter 20 (XIX): The Myth of Corrections

## Core Idea
Iterative journalism rests on the belief that errors can be easily fixed. That belief is false in both directions: bloggers resist correcting, and — worse — corrections literally *backfire*, making readers believe the falsehood more strongly. "Corrections online are a joke."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Backfire Effect** (Nyhan & Reifler, "When Corrections Fail"): readers shown a correction were *more* likely to believe the original false claim — and held it *more confidently* — than those who saw no correction. The correction reintroduces the claim, forcing it back through the mind, which tightens its grip.
- **We Don't Need an Update, We Need a Rewrite**: a story is the "concrescence of facts." Removing one fact via a correction leaves the premise and thrust standing. A challenged fact requires re-examining everything built on top of it — which almost no writer will do.
- **The Specious Present + Snapshot Reading**: the web lets content be *written* iteratively, but "the audience does not read or consume it iteratively" — each reader sees one snapshot once and concludes from it. Every corrected mistake was, for a time, "wrongly presented as correct."

## Key Concepts
- **Cognitive dissonance / rigidity**: "the bigger the fuckup, the less likely people want to cop to it." Once the mind accepts a plausible explanation, it becomes the framework that contorts all later information; "the facts that built an original premise are gone, but the conclusion remains."
- **The mind "first believes, then evaluates"**: suspending judgment until all evidence arrives is a skill detectives/doctors train for years; regular readers can't do it while reading gossip.
- **Unbelievable headlines warp the compass**: more exposure to fake/unbelievable headlines makes "the real seem fake and the fake seem real"; more extreme = longer processing = more likely believed; repetition breeds belief.
- **Corrections are amplifications**: the author's irony about the WSJ's "Corrections & Amplifications" — reintroducing a claim amplifies it.

## Mental Models
- **Making a point is exciting; correcting one is not** — an accusation spreads faster than a quiet later admission. Sinclair's water metaphor: sensational stuff flows through an open channel; corrections hit "the concrete wall of a closed dam."
- **We over-trust the written word**: centuries of expensive writing taught us no one wastes resources committing an untruth to paper — an association bloggers exploit.
- **The impossible position**: speaking out validates the story; staying silent means the news was never actually iterative. Acknowledging this would "undermine the premise of this very profitable practice."

## Anti-patterns
- **Chasing a correction at all**: it takes hours/days/weeks (bloggers drag their feet — "Oh, I never got your email"), by which time the traffic is gone; updates get buried at the bottom or framed as "your opinion" to keep the post alive.
- **Believing the collective eventually gets it right**: even when the iterative model reaches a correct final product, "it didn't make a difference. More people were misled than helped."

## Worked Example
Matt Drudge, based on an unnamed source, accused Clinton adviser Sidney Blumenthal of a covered-up history of spousal abuse — entirely false; an anonymous Republican source had used Drudge to settle a score ("I think I've been had"). Drudge's "correction" said only: "I am issuing a retraction of my information regarding Sidney Blumenthal…" — no apology, even under a $30M libel suit. Four years later he still defended the philosophy: "The great thing about this medium… is that you can fix things fast." And when the author asked Megan McCarthy at SXSW how to correct *subtle* untruths (not black-and-white ones like a fake death), she laughed: "I love your idea that there can be nuance on the Internet." Also: *Brand Channel* wrote that American Apparel "tapped Drew Carey" for a "back-to-school commercial" — not realizing the clip was an actual *Price Is Right* episode Carey hosts. "How does one begin to correct that? We're not dealing with the same reality." The author didn't even try.

## Key Takeaways
1. Corrections backfire — they entrench the false belief rather than removing it.
2. Readers consume snapshots as fact; iterative "self-healing" never reaches them in time.
3. A real fix requires a rewrite of the premise, which bloggers won't do.
4. The subject is trapped: responding validates the story, silence leaves it standing.
