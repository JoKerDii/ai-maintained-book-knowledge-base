# Chapter 17 (XVI): The Link Economy — The Leveraged Illusion of Sourcing

## Core Idea
Two habits — old media's "delegation of trust" and new media's "link economy" — combine into a dangerous engine of contagious misinformation. Links *look* like citations but rarely are, so unverified claims spread with the false appearance of proof.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Delegation of Trust (old rules)**: (1) if the outlet is legitimate, its scoops are; (2) if the story is legitimate, its facts are; (3) if the subject is legitimate, what people say about it probably is too. This let reporters build on each other's work — but it *required* shared high verification standards that no longer exist.
- **The Link Economy**: blogs trade traffic and information — link to each other's reports, add commentary, and "take more or less completed stories… add a layer of commentary, and turn it into something they call their own." It is **recursive**: blogs pull from prior blogs to make new content. Popularized by Jeff Jarvis, who advises against "replicating the work of other reporters."
- **May becomes Is becomes Has**: on the first site "may" becomes "are"; next time they add past tense to the earlier assertion "whether or not it actually happened." Officially sanctioned under link-economy rules.
- **Science vs. Journalism** (Kathryn Schulz, *Being Wrong*): scientists *replicate to disprove* each other (stripping out error); journalists *replicate to build on* each other's conclusions — "self-referential instead of self-critical." A single error ripples through the linked system.

## Key Concepts
- **Links imply credibility but rarely are citations**: a link can point to anything; it "vaguely complies" with the standard and shifts the burden of disproof to the reader. Google itself exploits this (citations = importance), but academic citations are peer-reviewed — online links are not.
- **Nobody clicks links**: only ~44% of Google News users click through; readers are "impatient birds… on the thin edge of distraction" (Zinsser). Dubious claims spread faster at viral speed, their nature obscured by "a bright little link."
- **The link illusion**: the blue html link "seems like it will support weight" but doesn't.

## Mental Models
- **Birds on a telephone wire** (Eugene McCarthy, updated): journalists follow each other's leads — but now "the wire need not exist"; they perch on illusions.
- **Subprime-mortgage analogy**: passing unverified info blog-to-blog (like a bank handing off subprime loans) externalizes a known cost (replication) into an unknown catastrophic one (failure of trust), borne by everyone.
- **"Reporting on what [another blog] is reporting"** launders low-standard material into higher-standard outlets without new verification.

## Anti-patterns
- **"But I linked to where I stole it from"**: knowing something came from *somewhere else* does not alleviate the delegation-of-trust problem — "it creates the appearance of a solution without solving anything."
- **Neutral Retweet (NR) tags / crowd fact-checking**: "Why are you retweeting things you don't believe in?!" Distributed crowd verification is *not* more accurate; the author sides with Descartes — every person responsible for his own work.

## Worked Example
A young Irish student posted a fabricated, obituary-perfect quote on composer Maurice Jarre's Wikipedia page after his death ("When I die there will be a final waltz playing in my head that only I can hear"). A blogger picked it up; eventually it reached *The Guardian*, "and from there it may as well have been real." It spread through the link economy — designed "to confirm and support, not to question or correct." Wikipedia editors removed the edit, but that couldn't update the obituaries; it stayed in The Guardian until caught separately. The student: "if I hadn't come forward, that quote would have gone down in history… once anything is printed enough times without challenge, it becomes fact." Separately: the author told Crain's *no* twice about closing NYC stores; Crain's found a realtor to say "might," ran "American Apparel likely to shed some NY stores," and Jezebel → The Cut → Racked → AOL Daily Finance all just *linked* rather than asking. A year later every store was still open.

## Key Takeaways
1. Links look like citations but carry no verification — the burden of proof silently shifts to the reader who won't click.
2. Delegation of trust needs shared standards that no longer exist; the habit remains and spreads error.
3. Journalism replicates to build (propagating error), unlike science, which replicates to disprove.
4. "May → is → has" launders speculation into fact as it climbs the linked chain.
