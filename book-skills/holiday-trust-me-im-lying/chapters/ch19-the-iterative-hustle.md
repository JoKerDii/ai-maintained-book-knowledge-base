# Chapter 19 (XVIII): The Iterative Hustle — Online Journalism's Bogus Philosophy

## Core Idea
"Iterative journalism" — publish first, verify later — is dressed up as a noble learning process but is really a money-making scam that externalizes its costs onto readers and subjects. "Getting it right is expensive; getting it first is cheap" (Arrington).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Iterative / Process / Beta Journalism (defined)**: report whatever you've heard *as* the news, then correct after posting. It is NOT "here's what we need to know for this to matter" or "everybody stop, I'll get to the bottom of this." Instead bloggers "throw up their hands, claim to be knowledge-less, and report whatever they've heard."
- **Jumpiness**: "as jumpy as reporters can get without outright making things up." A tweet, a comment, or an email tip triggers a live story with massive claimed implications (takeovers, resignations, criminal allegations).
- **The Update Dodge (covering your ass)**: hedge every claim — "We're hearing…", "Possibly…", "Sites are reporting…" — tossing the narrative into the stream "without taking full ownership," posing as an impartial observer of a process you began. When wrong, just update the headline (Business Insider: Paterson "Resignation To Follow" → "Governor's Office Denies Resignation").
- **Reporting *about* the rumors**: a self-serving distinction from reporting the rumors — "in reality there is no difference whatsoever."

## Key Concepts
- **Why publishers love it**: it eliminates fact-checkers and relationship-building, and it's *more* profitable — every correction is another post ("more pageviews"). "To call it a learning process… is a lie."
- **The specious present**: news exists only until it reaches those with "news interest," then becomes history — so it can never truly be iterative; once read, it becomes (poor, inaccurate) fact.
- **Cost externalization**: "makes the news cheap to produce but expensive to read"; subjects "write down millions each year in falsely damaged reputations."

## Mental Models
- **Software beta vs. news beta**: Google beta means strong fundamentals, superficial bugs. Iterative journalism is the *opposite* — the headline, links, and slideshows are there, but "the *facts* are suspect." "Software as beta means small glitches; the news as beta means the risk of a false reality."
- **The best case is worthless**: even iterative journalism's finest hour (the bin Laden death, ~20 minutes early) proves the point — "a whole twenty fucking minutes"; being first is a "vapid victory."
- **Slaves to the grind** (Bill Simmons): immediacy "entices reporters to become enslaved to certain sources, push transparent agendas, and 'break' news before there's anything to break."

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing corrections justify the speed**: the original stays up. *Eater LA* ran an anonymous, unverified tip that a wine bar had health violations and served fake gourmet items — "completely wrong." Even after apologizing ("shouldn't have published it"), the post stayed up two years later, updates buried below the debunked allegations, and only a lawsuit threat forced any admission.
- **Assuming the subject can respond**: exactly during a scandal/IPO/lawsuit/tragedy, legal or SEC rules or shock make it impossible to reply — "it is with the stories upon which we most need to tread lightly that bloggers are unwilling to."

## Worked Example
A prankster posted on CNN's iReport that a "source" said Steve Jobs had a severe heart attack — the user's first and only post, at 4 a.m., "obviously a hoax." Even MacRumors.com (which "writes about nothing but rumors") knew not to touch it. But Business Insider's sister blog *Silicon Alley Insider*, "following its iterative instincts," rushed it into a full post. Apple's stock plummeted. Twenty-five minutes later, tip deleted and rumor denied, Business Insider rewrote the lead: "'Citizen journalism'… just failed its first significant test." As the author notes: "Yeah, that's who failed here. You know who didn't? Those who were shorting Apple stock." Ray Donovan's question haunts every wronged subject: "Which office do I go to to get my reputation back?"

## Key Takeaways
1. Publish-first is a profit model (corrections = extra posts), not a truth-seeking process.
2. Readers consume a single snapshot as fact — the "specious present" makes iteration impossible.
3. Hedged qualifiers let blogs disown a narrative they started.
4. Its jumpiness is the manipulator's ideal entry point: a single tip introduces an unstoppable narrative.
