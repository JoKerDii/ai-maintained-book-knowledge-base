# Chapter 5 (IV): Tactic #1 — Bloggers Are Poor; Help Pay Their Bills

## Core Idea
You rarely bribe with cash. The criteria bloggers are *paid on* — pageviews, speed, buzz — can be co-opted into an indirect bribe just as effective as an overt payoff.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Indirect Bribe**: identify the levers an employer uses to set a blogger's pay (traffic, speed, career leverage) and feed them. Free product, exclusives, access, set visits, traffic — all pay the blogger without a check changing hands.
  - How: give product to fashion bloggers (rarely disclosed); offer affiliate commissions; hand out set tours, premiere invites, artist emails; buy influential tweets.
- **The Pageview Compensation Model**: Gawker pioneered paying writers bonuses on monthly traffic (potentially doubling salary), with a newsroom "big board" ranking every writer's stats — bottom of the board risks firing. Now standard (Forbes, Seeking Alpha, Business Insider).
- **The Blodget Rule**: a writer must generate ~3× the pageviews needed to cover their own salary+benefits+overhead+the publisher's cut. A $60k employee must produce ~1.8M pageviews/month or they're out.

## Key Concepts
- **Hired for quickness, not skill**: editors prize speed; journalism experience is only "a definite plus."
- **Digital sweatshop**: rates like $4/post (125 posts/month) or $12/post; median HuffPost contributor article worth ~$3 in revenue (Nate Silver).
- **The revolving door**: bloggers build a name, then sell it to old-media or tech jobs — so who does hard reporting on Google/Facebook when a cushy job awaits?
- **The *real* conflict of interest**: in a pay-per-pageview model, *every post is a conflict of interest.* The writer's stake isn't in *what* they write but in *how* — bigger, simpler, more controversial, more often.

## Mental Models
- **"I've never had to buy influence directly"**: you don't need to — the incentive structure already pushes bloggers to write what a manipulator wants.
- **Invest early in a blogger** and buy influence cheaply — give free clothes to a small video blogger who later gets an HBO show.
- "The reason the knives are so sharp online is because the pie is so small" (Kissinger, adapted).

## Anti-patterns
- **Hypocritical ethics rules**: Gawker's Hamilton Nolan wrote "media people shouldn't have a financial stake in what they're reporting on" — while being paid by his own pageviews.
- **Assuming small pay means bloggers are merely shafted**: it also means what they produce "isn't worth all that much" — even fair pay would be tiny.

## Worked Example
To promote a Tucker Max book, the author paid a Twitter account with 400k+ followers **$25** to tweet: "FACT: People will do anything for money." For a few hundred dollars more he got dozens of accounts to post humiliating promotional messages, driving the book to a #2 New York Times debut. A blog headline captured it: "Tucker Max Proves You Can Pay Celebrities To Tweet Whatever You Want." Separately: Samsung paid for a Business Insider staffer's trip to Barcelona; the writer disclosed it but admitted feeling "pretty warm and fuzzy about Samsung" — exactly the feeling the tactic cultivates.

## Key Takeaways
1. Bribery works through the pay levers, not envelopes of cash.
2. Free stuff, access, and traffic are currency because bloggers are underpaid and desperate.
3. The pay-per-pageview model makes bias structural — every post is conflicted.
4. The revolving door suppresses tough reporting on the very companies bloggers hope to join.
