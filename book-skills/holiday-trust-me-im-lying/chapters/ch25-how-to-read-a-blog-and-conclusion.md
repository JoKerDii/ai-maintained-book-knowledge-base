# Chapter 25 (XXIV + Conclusion): How to Read a Blog & Where to From Here

## Core Idea
The words we still trust — *exclusive, sources, leaked, developing* — are "abandoned shells": husks emptied of their old meaning while retaining their power. This chapter is the reader's decoder ring, plus the author's argument that only changing incentives (and reader demands) can fix it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Blog Decoder (translating the "substance words")**:
  - "According to a tipster…" → someone like the author tricked the blogger.
  - "We're hearing reports…" → random tweets or message-board posts.
  - "Leaked / official documents" → someone emailed the blogger; docs are usually fake/fabricated.
  - "BREAKING / more details as it develops" → published before basic facts were confirmed; no wait-and-see.
  - "Updated" → no one reworked the story; they pasted something at the bottom.
  - "Sources tell us…" → unvetted, uncorroborated, attention-hungry.
  - "EXCLUSIVE" → a favorable-coverage arrangement, or a stolen/multi-given story.
  - "Said in a press release" → probably just spam email, not a paid wire release.
  - "According to a report by" → the summarizer has minimal reading comprehension and every incentive to exaggerate.
  - "We've reached out for comment" → an email sent two minutes before publishing at 4 a.m.
  - "Said So-and-So" (quote) → likely stolen from elsewhere, claimed via a buried link.
  - "Which means / will result in" → the blogger has zero expertise and no consequences for being wildly wrong.
- **Abandoned Shells** (George W.S. Trow): bloggers use the "substance words" (like Wikipedia's weasel words) — "the language of Woodward and Bernstein" applied to a world "that would make even Hearst queasy." "In the age of no-authority, these are the authorities."
- **The Laurel Leaf Illusion**: film-festival laurels once signaled scarcity/prestige; now every neighborhood has a festival, so the marker survives while the meaning is gone. A metaphor for the whole web — a link looks like a citation but isn't.

## Key Concepts
- **The final empty shell is our knowledge**: "Our facts aren't facts, they are opinions dressed up like facts. Our opinions aren't opinions; they are emotions that feel like opinions. Our information… is just hastily assembled symbols."
- **"If the news is important, it will find me" is false**: what spreads rose "not through value, importance, or accuracy but through the opposite — slickness, titillation, and polarity."
- **Skepticism is not enough** — "not even enough to be a starting point," because we destroyed the old markers and built no reliable new ones.

## Mental Models (Conclusion — Where To From Here)
- **Incentives can be changed**: the NYT's shift from one-off to subscription (Ochs), and its paywall — "the new NYT incentive is to have more than twenty must-read articles each month" (Cowen) — vs. the pageview model's "must-clicks."
- **Blog traffic is worth almost nothing**: sites sell only a fraction of inventory, give the rest away for pennies, yet chase traffic above all — auto-refreshing tabs to rob advertisers of impressions. Trust-built smaller sites sell out ad space months ahead at higher prices.
- **"What do I plan to do with this information?"**: the question intelligent readers ask; most of what blogs produce has "no practical purpose… other than distraction." When readers demand quality over quantity, "manipulation and marketing will immediately become more difficult."
- **The first job of the journalist**: "'Is this information true?'" (Ed Wallace) — a mantle bloggers refuse.

## Anti-patterns
- **Delegated trust / deferred responsibility by readers**: we accepted the burden blogs abdicated — doing their fact-checking, correcting them, calling ourselves "contributors" when "actually we're cogs." "If we have to do all the work, what are we paying you guys for?"
- **Seeking a "solution"**: the author objects to the word — it "confirms that this problem needs to even exist" and takes the bad assumptions of blogs for granted. His job was to "prove that something was massively wrong."

## Worked Example
The author hoped to end on a hopeful quote from a courageous critic decrying "fake news… manufactured, hyped, rehashed, retracted — until at the end of the week you know no more than at the beginning." The gut-punch: the speaker was **Nick Denton** — founder of Gawker Media, "the man who fed and raised the monster more than anyone" — claiming he was on a "jihad… against fake news." "It's like Kim Kardashian complaining about how fake reality TV shows are." Denton "doesn't realize that his sites created the very market for fake news he claims to hate." Full circle: publisher and manipulator both unhappy, "right back to where we started" — but now the reader knows how the machine works, and gets a say in how the story ends.

## Key Takeaways
1. Decode the "substance words" — they're abandoned shells signaling the opposite of their traditional meaning.
2. Skepticism alone can't save you; the reliable markers of authority are gone.
3. Fix the incentives (subscription/paywall over pageviews); blog traffic is nearly worthless.
4. Reader complicity sustains the system — demanding quality, and asking "what will I do with this?", is the lever of change. There is no tidy "solution."
