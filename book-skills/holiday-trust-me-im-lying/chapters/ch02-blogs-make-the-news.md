# Chapter 2 (I): Blogs Make the News

## Core Idea
Blogs set the agenda for all other media. Because they must fill *infinite* space and profit from traffic, they literally manufacture reality — creating candidates, celebrities, and controversies that then get legitimized by mass media.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Blog Cycle (agenda manufacturing)**: Political blogs need things to cover; traffic rises during elections → reality (election far off) doesn't align → blogs create candidates early / move up the cycle → the person covered *becomes* an actual candidate by virtue of coverage → blogs profit, the public loses.
  - When to use as a lens: any time a story seems to appear "from nowhere," ask what the covering outlet's business needed.
- **"A blog isn't small if its readership is media elite"**: Influence is measured by *who* reads, not how many. Gawker, Politico, Business Insider, BuzzFeed, HuffPost, Drudge matter because TV producers and national reporters read them.

## Key Concepts
- **"Blog" (broad definition)**: all online publishing — Twitter to newspaper websites to group blogs — anything subject to the same attention incentives.
- **Infinite space problem**: newspapers fill one edition/day, cable fills 24 hours; blogs must fill *infinite* space, so "the site that covers the most stuff wins."
- **Traffic > truth**: internet economics make traffic "more important — and more profitable — than the truth."
- **Fiat over culture**: mastering the rules that govern blogs grants control over "the memes that become our cultural references."

## Mental Models
- **What rules the press rules the country**: public opinion governs a democracy, the press governs public opinion, so whoever governs the press governs everything.
- Think of coverage as a **self-fulfilling bet**: "Politico picked Pawlenty [because] they had the power to control the outcome" — covering something makes it real.
- **The monster can feed itself**: misinformation now spreads even without a deliberate manipulator (the fake "Brett Favre on Dancing with the Stars" meme spread from a misread joke with no one pushing it).

## Anti-patterns
- **Mistaking coverage for newsworthiness**: Politico following non-candidate Pawlenty (while the NYT couldn't afford to) wasn't crazy — it was a business decision to manufacture an election "leg" to profit from.
- **Assuming predictability is harmless**: "what is predictable can be anticipated, redirected, accelerated, or controlled."

## Worked Example
Tim Pawlenty, January 2011: not a candidate — no director, no bus, few donors — yet a Politico beat reporter followed his "noncampaign" town to town. The NYT then covered *Politico covering a noncandidate*. Coverage generated millions of impressions online, then print, then TV; Pawlenty became a real candidate whose eventual endorsement was courted by the front-runner. When speed stopped working, Politico shifted to scandal (Herman Cain), decimating its own manufactured candidate to refill the cycle. "Another one bit the dust so that blogs could fill their cycle."

## Key Takeaways
1. Coverage is not a mirror of importance; it is a product shaped by what the outlet's business model needs.
2. The person or topic a blog "arbitrarily decides to cover" can be turned into something real — donors, ballots, presidents.
3. Predictable business logic makes blog behavior anticipatable and therefore exploitable.
4. Even without a manipulator, the interdependent system distorts and exaggerates whatever it touches.
