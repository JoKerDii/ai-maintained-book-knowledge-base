# Chapter 11 (X): Tactic #7 — Kill 'Em With Pageview Kindness

## Core Idea
Blogs live by measurable metrics — pageviews, comments, Most Popular lists — because these are simple to communicate as goals. Those same metrics are the handles by which a manipulator picks up and hijacks the news.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Metrics as a Compass to Sabotage**: the "Most Read/Most Popular" list is a compass for editors. "Mess with the magnet inside the compass and watch as its owner goes wildly off track." Give a blogger anything that moves them toward their pageview quota and you serve their interest while serving yours.
- **Simplistic Measurements Matter**: rephrase "what gets measured gets managed." Blogs measure only the cheap, clear things (traffic, comments, "did Gawker/Drudge pick it up?") — never "wonderfulness." Like Vietnam body counts, bad metrics make bloggers "do awful things."
- **Fake the Metrics (pseudo-metrics)**: because other blogs' coverage signals traffic, competitors copy it — so sending links to an unoriginal blogger produces coverage. Inflate stats yourself: post to your brand's social accounts; buy traffic via StumbleUpon/Outbrain (~$100 = 1,000+ visitors); leave fake comments/emails (good and bad) to prove a "hot debate." Stat counters can't tell fake from real, and no one checks.

## Key Concepts
- **Warnock's Dilemma**: publishers can't tell whether silence means satisfaction or apathy — and every interpretation is "utterly unprofitable." So they fear silence and chase whatever generates *any* feedback.
- **Comments as a false quality signal**: HuffPost's *paid* articles got ~800 comments each vs. ~43 for amateurs — treated as "better." In a sane system thousands of comments would mean the discussion went wrong; online it's the mark of a professional.
- **The AOL Way** checklist: writers must ask pageview/CPM/SEO questions — everything "*except* 'Is what I am making any good?'"

## Mental Models
- **Blogs deliberately do not want to help** — a definitive, satisfying story earns silence (the "good" kind), which is unprofitable. So they avoid being final.
- **Provide the flimsy evidence they crave**: fake positive/negative feedback cements the impression that you're high-valence, coverage-worthy material.
- **The beast bites back**: once a site sees traffic in a topic, it won't stop — good news runs out, so it fabricates scandal to keep the high-traffic subject alive.

## Anti-patterns
- **Chasing pageviews into indefensible territory**: Mashable posting a mass-shooter's suicide note because "other blogs were doing it"; HuffPost's front-page "What time does the Super Bowl start?" — justified purely by the algorithm.
- **Trusting Most-Popular lists**: Susan Orlean — they "never include a story that is quiet and ordinary but wonderful to read."

## Worked Example
The author exploits pseudo-metrics constantly. To get a story onto Site B, he sends Site B's unoriginal blogger the links showing Site A already covered it — the links "scored under false pretenses" but no one can tell. To make himself a recurring subject, he posts the resulting story to the company's Twitter/Facebook (inflating stats) and buys a burst of a thousand StumbleUpon visitors for ~$100 — "illusory confirmations to the blogger that you are newsworthy." He has even shown bloggers factories of workers whose jobs were threatened by inaccurate coverage and *begged* them to be fair — and it made no difference, because favors don't drive pageviews.

## Key Takeaways
1. Blogs optimize the cheap, simple metrics — so those metrics are the attack surface.
2. Coverage begets coverage; manufacture the appearance of traffic and buzz.
3. Fear of silence (Warnock's Dilemma) makes flimsy fake feedback highly persuasive.
4. Comment counts and Most-Popular lists reward the worst content, not the best.
