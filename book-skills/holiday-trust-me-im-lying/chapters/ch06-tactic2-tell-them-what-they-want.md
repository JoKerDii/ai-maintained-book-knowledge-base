# Chapter 6 (V): Tactic #2 — Tell Them What They Want to Hear

## Core Idea
Journalists are "entirely dependent on self-interested sources" (Edward Jay Epstein) and rarely verify. Package your message as the thing they already want — a leak, a press release, an expert quote, a Wikipedia "fact" — and they'll run it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Deliberate Leak**: don't issue an official statement; fabricate an internal "leaked" memo/document and email it as if you're an anonymous insider. Bloggers who ignored the facts run "EXCLUSIVE! / LEAKED!" because it's told "in words they wanted to hear."
- **Press Release 2.0**: blogs love press releases — the material is pre-written, the angle laid out, and "since it comes from an official newswire, they can blame someone else if the story is wrong." SEO'd releases (PRWeb) also surface in Google and auto-syndicate to Google Finance/Yahoo Finance for stockholders. Put out a release for *everything*.
- **Wikipedia as the certifier**: reporters research on Wikipedia. Edit the descriptors and framing there, and reporters and readers adopt them. "May becomes is." Seed a client's talking point in an interview, then cite that interview on Wikipedia to make it "doubly real."
- **HARO (Help a Reporter Out)**: a service that connects "self-interested sources" to reporters. Tagline: "Free Publicity." Use fake emails and fast replies to become a quoted "expert," then bootstrap that quote into more reputable outlets.

## Key Concepts
- **Online "anonymous" ≠ verified**: quotes and tips are pulled from unsolicited emails, angry comments, or people with something to gain; identities go unverified.
- **Rules of thumb bloggers follow**: if a source can't be reached by email, they can't be a source; if background isn't easily available, it isn't included.
- **Manufactured false balance**: reporters ask for "a response" to a rumor only so a denial (which readers skip) justifies publishing the rumor.
- **HARO manufactures self-promotion disguised as research**: reporters solicit sources to confirm what they already intend to say.

## Mental Models
- **Leave room to speculate**: give an official comment that doesn't fully address the issue; as a fake tipster, ask leading rhetorical questions and watch them become the headline.
- **Reverse-engineer the story from a search-friendly premise** — that's what pageview journalists do; feed them the premise.
- **Control your Wikipedia page or risk the "failed screenwriter" problem** (a reporter reading a vandalized page as fact).

## Anti-patterns
- **Blind faith in press releases as fact**: enabled stockbroker Lambros Ballas (charged by SEC) to pump stocks with fake releases — Local.com jumped 75% on a fake Microsoft acquisition rumor.
- **Poor reading comprehension as a feature**: the author sourced a Wikipedia note that Tucker's book hit the list "in 2006, 2007, and 2008"; a journalist cribbed it as "over 3 years on the bestseller list," which was then re-cited on Wikipedia.

## Worked Example
Halloween images the author couldn't legally use: he had an employee email them to Jezebel/Gawker writing, "I shouldn't be doing this but I found some secret images on the American Apparel server." The post did 90,000 views. The writer helpfully replied: don't leak from your company email, you might get caught. (The author's private thought: "but how else could she be sure they were real?") The same sourcing standard later ended a U.S. congressman's career via an anonymous Gawker tip — which "took a little of the fun out of it."

## Key Takeaways
1. Verification is optional online; frame your material as the thing they already want to publish.
2. Fake leaks, press releases, and HARO quotes all outperform honest official statements.
3. Wikipedia is a citation laundering machine — control the framing at the source.
4. "Balance" and "requests for comment" are often theater that justifies publishing rumor.
