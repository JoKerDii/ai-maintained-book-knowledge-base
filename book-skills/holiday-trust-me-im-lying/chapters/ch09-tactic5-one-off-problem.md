# Chapter 9 (VIII): Tactic #5 — Sell Them Something They Can Sell (The One-Off Problem)

## Core Idea
Media history repeats. News sold story-by-story (one-off) — the yellow press then, blogs now — is structurally forced to sensationalize and fabricate. Understand how newsmen must sell their product and it's easier to sell them yours.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Phases of the Press**:
  1. **Party Press** — one-man shops selling a *service* (communicating ideas) by subscription to party members. Editorial, not "news."
  2. **Yellow Press** — Benjamin Day's *New York Sun* (1833) sold "cash and carry," one copy at a time on the street. Papers had to be loud to sell on street corners → decline of editorial, rise of gossip and manufactured news (Bennett, Pulitzer, Hearst). Aim: "not to instruct but to startle."
  3. **Modern Stable Press (subscription)** — Adolph Ochs (*NYT*): "decency meant dollars." Subscription aligned incentives with readers — misled readers unsubscribe, errors get corrected, reputation beats notoriety; nuance, opposing views, and uncertainty become possible.
- **The One-Off Problem**: when each issue/story must sell itself anew, there isn't enough real news that's exciting enough — so publishers must *create* news to sell. Reporters "cover the news when it was there and make it up when it was not."
- **Death of Subscription = Rebirth of Manipulation**: online, news is sold à la carte again — each article competes on Google/Twitter/Facebook. RSS (the only viable subscription method) is dead *on purpose*, because subscription puts users in control; the one-off model keeps competition on the publisher's terms.

## Key Concepts
- **Markers of yellow journalism** (W.J. Campbell): screaming headlines about unimportant news; lavish irrelevant pictures; impostors/frauds/faked interviews; ostentatious underdog support; anonymous sources; high-society coverage. (Sound like Gawker/HuffPost?)
- **Seekers/glancers, not subscribers**: top traffic sources are Google, Facebook, Twitter — visitors sent to one article "for a disposable purpose."
- **Junk-food media diet**: single-use traffic favors "the refined and processed sugars of the information food pyramid."

## Mental Models
- **"Look at media hoaxes from before your grandparents were born"** — the same cons play, maybe better now (author drew ideas from *The Harder They Fall*, *All the King's Men*).
- **Subscription subsidizes nuance**; one-off exploits valence. Whichever model, the incentives predict the content.
- Chase the **mythical Other Reader in viral land** — the death of subscription means blogs court big hits over loyal readers.

## Anti-patterns
- **Building loyal readerships via RSS/subscription**: antithetical to blog interests, so it was allowed to die despite working well for readers.
- **Assuming twentieth-century subscription press was pure**: it had top-down corruption (rich owners in the same club — Chandler's Marlowe), just fewer of the masses-driven ills.

## Worked Example
The historical parallel itself is the worked example: George Templeton Strong nearly ignored newsboys shouting "Extra! — bombardment of Fort Sumter!!!" for four blocks because he assumed it was a "sell" (a fake). He broke down and bought it; that issue sold 135,000 copies in a day — the most-printed in the Herald's history. The One-Off Problem's incentive to sensationalize even drove papers to clamor for (some say create) the Spanish-American War. Benjamin Day: "We newspaper people thrive best on the calamities of others." Today's à-la-carte web recreates the identical pressure at the scale of millions of blogs.

## Key Takeaways
1. One-off distribution *forces* sensationalism and fabrication; subscription enables nuance.
2. The web returned us to the yellow-press incentive structure at massive scale.
3. RSS/subscription died deliberately because it hands control to readers.
4. Sell blogs "something they can sell" — high-valence, one-off-friendly material.
