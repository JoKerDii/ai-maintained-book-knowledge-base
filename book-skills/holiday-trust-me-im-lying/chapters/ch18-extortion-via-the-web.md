# Chapter 18 (XVII): Extortion via the Web — Facing the Online Shakedown

## Core Idea
When the system repeats and sensationalizes any information it finds, PR flips from spreading the word to *stopping* the spread of untrue words. Blogs, trolls, and rivals can dig a reputational hole you must pay to fill — and sometimes pay them to stop digging.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Implicit Shakedown**: extortion "couched in slightly more opaque terms" than "pay us or else." Arrington's "Why We Often Blindside Companies" — a veiled threat: he has dirt on everyone and his whim decides if it gets out; "Treat us with respect and you'll get it back." The Harvard Business Review scolding an airline for not stopping online speculation is the same threat: "It'd be *a pity* if something happened to this nice little shop of yours."
- **The Arms Race**: one company plants stories on a competitor → the rival hires a firm to defend and strike back → "an endless loop of online manipulation that can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars." Even a $200B company (Google, hit by Facebook's whisper campaign) is reduced to "Make it stop. We just want to be left alone."
- **The Illusion of a Lawsuit**: leak a lawyer's *intention-to-sue* letter (not a real suit) to gossip blogs to inflict embarrassment and force a payout.

## Key Concepts
- **"Why is my job to do your job?"**: bloggers demand subjects catch and correct their errors, and even that the *industry* train the bloggers to write better.
- **Being right matters more to the subject than the writer** — so the subject blinks first, spending thousands to counteract undeserved bad press or hiring a spinmaster.
- **Reputation-management industry**: dozens of firms dressed in metrics jargon whose real service is handling "the disturbing, nasty, and corrupt dealings" — stretching the rules "as much as possible."
- **Overstock.com 10-K**: formally named social media as one of three major risk factors — "Use of social media may adversely impact our reputation," with harm "immediate, without affording us an opportunity for redress or correction."

## Mental Models
- **A culture of fear → the unreal**: constant threat forces public figures into two fake modes — "deliberately provocative or conservatively fake." Nuance becomes a weakness; everyone limits exposure by being fake.
- **"You only have nothing to fear if you're a nobody"**: to be important is to be a target for a shakedown, malicious lie, or planted story.
- **The tools are frictionless**: the author "didn't even remember doing" a shakedown until reminded — "it was almost difficult *not* to do so."

## Anti-patterns
- **Hoping to just "go back to normal"**: once the arms race begins, it escalates; focusing on "great products" (Google) doesn't stop it.
- **Ignoring how blog rumor moves markets**: a fake Engadget email about an iPhone/OS delay knocked >$4B off Apple's stock; bogus posts erase hundreds of millions in market cap.

## Worked Example
Fernando Motolese, a Brazilian video producer, approached the yogurt giant Danone with two hypothetical videos: a "fun spoof" and a "disgusting version" full of scatological images — he'd be "more inclined to release the first" if Danone paid him a fee each time it was seen. Danone's rep: "It felt sort of like blackmail." "Well, that's because it *was* blackmail. It was extortion via viral video." Separately, the author advised a friend to have a lawyer draft an *intention-to-sue* letter against a famous talent agent and leak it to gossip blogs; the threat hit TMZ, ESPN, and others. Outcome: "They paid him five hundred thousand dollars to go away." "They may have stolen from my friend, but I still shook someone down."

## Key Takeaways
1. Modern PR is largely defensive — stopping false spread, not creating it.
2. Extortion is usually implicit ("a pity if…"), and the arms race escalates once begun.
3. The subject blinks first because being right matters more to them than to the writer.
4. Fear drives public figures into fakeness — the seed of "unreality."
