# Chapter 4 (III): The Blog Con — How Publishers Make Money Online

## Core Idea
Understand the business and you understand the news: blogs run on "traffic by any means," and they are ultimately built not to be profitable but to be *sold*. Every editorial choice contorts to fit this mold.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Advertisement × Traffic = Revenue**: ads are sold per thousand impressions (CPM); revenue = CPM × pageviews. Publishers can't differentiate impression types — "a click is a click." So the only lever is traffic. Every decision obeys one dictum: **traffic by any means.**
- **Scoops Equal Traffic**: one big exclusive can propel a blog from obscurity to notoriety (TMZ hit ~$20M/yr on a handful of scoops). Hence blogs will manufacture or steal scoops.
- **The Pseudo-Exclusive** (Nick Denton memo): "take ownership of a story even if it isn't a strict exclusive" — i.e., pretend you have a scoop, because most readers see it in only one place.
- **Blogs Are Built to Be Sold**: real money is selling the whole site to "a rich sucker" for a multiple of traffic and earnings. HuffPost → AOL for $315M; Weblogs Inc → AOL $25M; TechCrunch → AOL $30M. Scoops and marquee bloggers exist to inflate the exit valuation.

## Key Concepts
- **Media was once about protecting a name; on the web it's about building one.** Established outlets guard reputation; blogs bend the truth to gain recognition.
- **Using names to build a name**: locking up A-list bloggers (Andrew Sullivan, Pete Rojas) drives scoops and traffic like a franchise athlete; their deals make questionable tactics *more* necessary ("the big names have to stay big").
- **Mini-Ponzi scheme**: each blog values traffic growth over financials, brand over trust, scale over sense — built so "one stupid buyer cashes out the previous ones."
- **Conflicts of interest are endemic**: Michael Arrington invested in startups TechCrunch covered; Denton invests in companies he links to and shuffles traffic between sites to charge advertisers twice. The author is simultaneously ad buyer *and* PR contact for his clients.

## Mental Models
- Read every story as **contorted to fit the revenue mold** — topic is secondary to traffic.
- **Desperation = opportunity**: the intense short-term pressure for traffic-to-exit is "the greatest quality you can hope for in a potential victim."
- **Enter the manipulator (the third player)**: bloggers building names and publishers seeking exits are like two crooked businessmen pumping a bogus stock; a third player exerts massive influence because "the media is too busy chasing profits to bother trying to stop us."

## Anti-patterns
- **Trusting the "serious news operation" claim**: TMZ's founder claimed "rigid standards" while running a "JFK photo" that was actually a 1967 Playboy spread.
- **Assuming scoops are earned**: Gawker's 3.2M-view Tom Cruise Scientology scoop was handed to them by another journalist (Mark Ebner) who got no credit or link — "blog fortunes were made off the backs of others."

## Worked Example
Gawker's Tom Cruise Scientology videos: Hollywood journalist Mark Ebner unearthed the tapes and showed them to friends at Gawker. Gawker immediately posted a story with the videos "before Mark or anyone else had a chance to." The post did 3.2M views and brought a new audience. Ebner got nothing — Gawker didn't even link back. Gawker "owned a story that was not theirs." This is the pseudo-exclusive and "traffic by any means" in one move.

## Key Takeaways
1. The business model *is* the editorial filter — every story bends toward traffic.
2. Scoops (real, stolen, or faked) are the growth engine; pseudo-exclusives fake the scoop.
3. Blogs are financial vehicles built for an exit, which intensifies the short-term traffic desperation manipulators exploit.
4. Rampant conflicts of interest mean loyalty runs to the con, not the audience.
