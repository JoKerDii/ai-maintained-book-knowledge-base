# Chapter 21 (XX): Cheering On Our Own Deception

## Core Idea
The media and public are supposed to be on opposing teams — the media protecting the public from marketers. Instead marketers and media are on the *same* team, and readers are baited into watching (and applauding) their own manipulation, dressed up as news.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Advertising as Content**: turn ads into news. Send bloggers "an exclusive leak of our new controversial ad" and get "Exclusive! American Apparel's Controversial New Ad" — free coverage for something companies normally *pay* to place. Variants: get a video/commercial "banned" to make it a news story (Rihanna "S&M" banned in 11 countries; PETA's rejected Super Bowl ad). Mashable literally runs a "Top 20 Viral Ads" chart — a chart of advertisements.
- **Coverage About Coverage**: after bin Laden's death, blogs immediately wrote "how the story broke" (FastCompany, NYT's Media Decoder). "Coverage about coverage is not more coverage" — it's worthless self-glorifying filler that borrows relevance from real news.
- **The "How to Pitch Us" Genre**: blogs periodically publish how publicists can better manipulate them ("Rules of Thumb for Pitching Silly Claims to TechCrunch"; "DEAR PR FOLKS…"). Each is "a manual with step-by-step instructions on how to infiltrate and deceive that blogger."

## Key Concepts
- **Blogger-sponsored conferences**: sites host events (TechCrunch Disrupt, Mashable Connect), then liveblog "the words that came out of the mouths of the people the site paid to speak" — a pseudo-event manufactured to look newsworthy so people pay to attend next year.
- **Impossible to burn a blog**: no matter how many times caught leaking bad info or manufacturing news, "it never changed anything." Bloggers don't mind being deceived — it often yields a bonus "story behind how we got the story wrong."
- **PR is not meaningful or cool**: "something companies do to move product," but because it's cheap and lucrative to cover, blogs convince you it's news.

## Mental Models
- **"Why are we cheering on our own deception?"** — discussing how a corporation (Toyota) could "better" market to us is helping bait the hook that catches us.
- **Missing the forest for the trees**: critiquing that Tiger Woods's apology had "too many clichés" ignores that "the whole thing is a cliché" — a staged event.
- **Only when you see the coverage as lazy, cheap, and self-interested** does it lose its allure — "only then can you stop watching your own manipulation as entertainment."

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating PR performance as news**: a news org (HuffPost) evaluating someone else's PR "instead of evaluating the veracity and quality of its own coverage."
- **BuzzFeed's "Top Viral Predictors" game**: users guess whether content will go viral — "producing content around whether other pieces of content might be read." (BuzzFeed uses this data with paying brands to make advertorials more viral.)

## Worked Example
At a Spago lunch, HuffPost CEO Eric Hippeau asked the group how Toyota "could have better responded" to the unintended-acceleration PR crisis. The room pontificated: "transparency is critical," "be proactive," "get out in front of it," "reach out to bloggers." The author interrupted: "None of you know what you're talking about… sites like yours pass along rumors as fact… The Internet is the problem here, not the solution." Vindication followed: (1) HuffPost, sued by unpaid writers, "clammed up" and failed its own transparency test; (2) NASA later largely exonerated Toyota — most cases were driver error (hitting the accelerator, not the brake). "Toyota hadn't been reckless, the media had." His better line, in hindsight: "What the fuck are you guys talking about this for?" — why help a corporation market to us better?

## Key Takeaways
1. Marketers and media are on the same team; the reader is the mark.
2. Ads-as-content, coverage-about-coverage, and "how to pitch us" posts are self-interested filler passed off as news.
3. You literally can't burn a blog — deception yields a bonus meta-story.
4. Debating how companies should spin us is cheering on our own deception.
