## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The master strategy — Trading Up the Chain**: Plant a fabricated/exaggerated story with a tiny low-standards blog → it becomes the source for a larger blog → then for national outlets → "until the unreal becomes real." Use the **Three-Level Chain**: Level 1 (small, high-trust, understaffed blogs — the entry point) → Level 2 (legacy-media blogs that *look* legit) → Level 3 (national). Don't pitch the big reporter; seed what they read. 3–4 links = a "trend piece." (Ch 1, 3)

**The one rule that explains everything — "Traffic by any means"**: Advertisement × Traffic = Revenue. Every editorial choice contorts to fit this mold. When blog behavior confuses you, ask what the business needed. Blogs are built not to be profitable but **to be sold** to a bigger buyer — so short-term traffic desperation is maximal, and "desperation is the greatest quality you can hope for in a potential victim." (Ch 4)

**Feeding the Monster**: The controlling metaphor — new media is a cute monster you feed for attention until it grows dangerous and turns on you. Applies to manipulator, publisher, and reader alike. The manipulator's fatal flaw is the **illusion of control** (feeding the crocodile hoping to be eaten last). (Ch 1)

**Virality = Valence**: The intensity of positive OR negative emotion drives sharing; the reasonable middle dies. Ranked: **anger** (strongest — one SD ≈ 3 hours as NYT front-page lead) > awe/excitement/titillation > positive (≈1.2 hrs). **Sadness is unviral** (low-arousal). Result: **suppression by transmission** — the public is misinformed by what the sharing filter selects, not by censorship. (Ch 7)

**The One-Off Problem**: When news is sold story-by-story (yellow press then, blogs now) rather than by subscription, publishers must create and sensationalize news to sell each unit. Subscription aligns incentives with readers (misled readers unsubscribe); one-off exploits valence. RSS/subscription died *on purpose* because it hands control to readers. (Ch 9)

**The Nine Tactics** (Book One): #1 Indirect bribe (feed the pay levers, not cash); #2 Tell them what they want (fake leaks, press releases, Wikipedia, HARO); #3 Give them what spreads (crank valence); #4 Help them trick readers (question-mark headlines); #5 Sell them something they can sell (the One-Off Problem); #6 Make it all about the headline (pre-write it, let them steal it); #7 Kill 'em with pageview kindness (fake the pseudo-metrics); #8 Use the technology against itself (on-format spoon-feeding); #9 Just make stuff up (manufacture urgency; the self-feeding two-blog hustle). (Ch 5–13)

**Iterative Journalism** ("publish first, verify later"): Sold as a noble learning process; really a profit model (each correction = another post). "Getting it right is expensive; getting it first is cheap" (Arrington). The subject is trapped: speaking validates the story, silence leaves it standing. (Ch 19)

**The Myth of Corrections + Backfire Effect**: Corrections don't just fail — they *entrench* the false belief (Nyhan & Reifler). Readers consume one snapshot as fact (the "specious present"), so iterative "self-healing" never reaches them. You don't need an update, you need a rewrite (which no one does). (Ch 20)

**The Link Economy vs. Delegation of Trust**: Old media built on each other's *verified* facts; blogs trade links recursively, and a link *looks* like a citation but rarely is. "May becomes is becomes has." Journalism replicates to *build* (propagating error), unlike science, which replicates to *disprove*. (Ch 17)

**Unreality**: The cumulative endpoint — a netherworld where fake and real build on each other and can't be told apart. Driven by **pseudo-events** (Boorstin: anything staged only to attract media) and the **News Funnel** (the media systematically *limits* what the public sees). Fake never stays fake: real responses to pseudo-events alter the real world. (Ch 24)

**How to Read a Blog — Abandoned Shells**: Trusted words (*exclusive, sources, leaked, developing*) are husks emptied of meaning while retaining power. Decode them (see cheatsheet). Skepticism alone "is not even enough to be a starting point." (Ch 25)
