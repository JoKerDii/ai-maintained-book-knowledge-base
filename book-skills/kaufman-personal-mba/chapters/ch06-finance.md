# Chapter 6: Finance

## Core Idea
Finance is the art and science of watching money flow into and out of a business, then deciding how to allocate it and judging whether the results are enough. You don't need spreadsheets full of jargon—you need a handful of numbers that tell you whether the business is operating the way you intended and producing sufficient results.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four Methods to Increase Revenue**: There are only four ways to grow revenue.
  - When to use: any time you want to raise top-line revenue and need to know your real levers.
  - How: pick from (1) increase the number of customers you serve, (2) increase the average size of each transaction (upselling), (3) increase the frequency of transactions per customer, (4) raise your prices. Focus the methods on ideal customers—those who buy early, buy often, spend most, spread the word, and pay a premium.
- **Lifetime Value**: The total value of a customer's business over the entire lifetime of their relationship with you.
  - When to use: choosing markets, justifying acquisition spend, deciding how much to invest in retention.
  - How: estimate average purchase value × purchase frequency × expected relationship length. Subscriptions maximize Lifetime Value because they collect revenue for as long as possible.
- **Allowable Acquisition Cost (AAC)**: The maximum you can spend to acquire a new customer—the marketing component of Lifetime Value.
  - When to use: setting marketing budgets and testing channels.
  - How: start with Lifetime Value, subtract Value Stream costs, subtract Overhead ÷ total customers (your per-customer Fixed Costs), then multiply by (1 − desired Profit Margin). The result is your max spend per customer. High Lifetime Value lets you even lose money on the first sale (a "loss leader").
- **Pricing Power**: Your ability to raise prices over time without losing too many customers.
  - When to use: choosing markets and protecting Sufficiency against inflation and rising costs.
  - How: imagine doubling prices—if you'd lose less than half your customers, raise them. You can't know your power until you experiment; changes rarely have permanent effects unless prices are widely scrutinized.
- **Amortization**: Spreading the cost of a large resource investment across its estimated useful life.
  - When to use: deciding whether a big capital expense (factory, equipment, software) is worth it.
  - How: divide total cost by units it can produce (or projects it enables) over its life to get per-unit cost. Remember useful life is a prediction—if you don't sell what you produce, the per-unit cost balloons (see Crocs).
- **Return on Investment (ROI)**: The value created from an investment of time or resources.
  - When to use: comparing competing alternatives.
  - How: (gain ÷ cost). Works for time and other Universal Currencies, not just money. Every future ROI is a semieducated guess—account for risk.
- **Hierarchy of Funding**: A ladder of financing options, from cheapest/most-control to costliest/least-control.
  - When to use: deciding how to fund growth.
  - How: climb only as far as needed—Personal Cash → Personal Credit → Personal Loans → Unsecured/Secured Loans → Bonds → Receivables Financing → Angel Capital → Venture Capital → Public Stock Offering. The more money you take, the more control you surrender.

## Key Concepts
- **Profit**: Bringing in more money than you spend; nothing operates at a loss forever.
- **Profit Margin**: The gap between revenue captured and cost to capture it, in percentage terms (spend $1 to get $2 = 100% margin).
- **Value Capture**: Retaining a percentage of the value created in each transaction; capture too much and customers flee.
- **Sufficiency**: The point where profit makes it worthwhile to keep the business going ("ramen profitable"); track it with target monthly revenue (TMR).
- **Overhead**: The minimum ongoing resources required to keep operating, regardless of sales.
- **Fixed Costs vs Variable Costs**: Fixed costs are incurred no matter how much you produce (reductions accumulate); variable costs scale with output (reductions are amplified by volume).
- **Breakeven**: The point where cumulative total revenue exceeds cumulative total expenses—where the business starts creating wealth instead of consuming it.
- **Purchasing Power**: The sum of all liquid assets (cash, credit, available financing) you can deploy; run out and it's game over.
- **Leverage**: Using borrowed money to magnify gains—and equally magnify losses.
- **Sunk Cost**: Unrecoverable past investments of time, energy, or money that should be irrelevant to forward-looking decisions.
- **Internal Controls**: Standard procedures that keep the business's finances accurate and protected.

## Mental Models
- Use **Value Capture minimization** when you want long-term loyalty and word-of-mouth—capture just enough to stay Sufficient rather than nickel-and-diming customers.
- Use **Bootstrapping** when you value control and self-sufficiency; climb the Hierarchy of Funding only when a capability is otherwise out of reach.
- Use **Sunk Cost reasoning** when a project sours—judge only the additional investment required versus the expected reward, ignoring what's already spent.
- Use **Amortization** when a big upfront expense looks scary but the per-unit cost over a realistic useful life is small.

## Anti-patterns
- **Maximizing Value Capture**: Squeezing every penny erodes the very reason customers buy and drives them away.
- **Incremental Degradation**: Cutting costs by quietly degrading quality (mass-market "chocolate") seems like a small trade-off but accumulates until customers notice and leave.
- **Chasing the Sunk Cost**: "Throwing good money after bad" to recoup losses (the Baranel building cost 3× its budget and exceeded its worth).
- **Over-leveraging**: Borrowing heavily amplifies losses as much as gains; the 2008–9 crisis was driven by 30–40× bank leverage.
- **Confusing Receivables with cash**: IOUs don't pay the bills—businesses close with millions in "sales" on the books.

## Worked Example
An insurance agency wants to know how much it can spend to win a customer. Average premium is $200/month = $2,400/year, and customers stay 10 years, so **Lifetime Value** ≈ $24,000—versus a lemonade stand's one-time $1 customer. To compute **Allowable Acquisition Cost**: say Lifetime Value is $2,000 over five years; subtract $500 in Value Stream (creation/delivery) costs, leaving $1,500. With $500,000 Overhead over five years across 500 customers, Fixed Costs are $1,000/customer, leaving $500. Targeting a 60% Profit Margin, you can spend 40% of that $500—an **AAC of $200/customer**. Any channel that acquires customers for ≤$200 is worth testing. High Lifetime Value is exactly why Guthy-Renker can "go negative" on Proactiv's first $20 sale: the subscription makes it back many times over.

## Key Takeaways
1. Business is about what you keep, not what you make—generate enough Profit to reach Sufficiency, then you're already successful.
2. There are only Four Methods to Increase Revenue; aim them at ideal customers.
3. Know your Lifetime Value first—it sets your Allowable Acquisition Cost and tells you how hard to fight to keep customers.
4. Cash and Purchasing Power are king; Receivables and "sales on the books" are not cash.
5. Cut costs carefully—Incremental Degradation and over-cutting destroy the value customers pay for.
6. Ignore Sunk Costs; decide forward on additional investment versus expected reward.
7. Bootstrap as far as you can, then climb the Hierarchy of Funding only as needed, knowing each rung costs control.
