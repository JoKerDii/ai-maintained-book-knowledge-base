# Chapter 3: Marketing

## Core Idea
Marketing is the art and science of finding prospects — people who are actively interested in what you offer — and capturing their attention quickly and inexpensively. It is about getting noticed (not closing the deal, which is Sales), and the cardinal sin is being boring.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Remarkability**: Design your offer to be literally worth remarking on — unique enough to violate expectations and pique curiosity (Seth Godin's "Purple Cow"). Remarkability is the best way to attract Attention because satisfied customers do your advertising for free.
  - When to use: When building or positioning an offer and you have limited or no advertising budget.
  - How: Find the dimension where you can violate the prospect's expectations (Vibram FiveFingers look like frog feet); make the offer itself the conversation starter.
- **End Result**: Market the distinctive experience or emotion the prospect actually wants, not the features. "People don't buy quarter-inch drills; they buy quarter-inch holes."
  - When to use: Writing any marketing message; whenever tempted to list features.
  - How: Translate every feature into the benefit it provides; connect it to a Core Human Drive; aim for the prospect to conclude "This is for me."
- **Qualification**: Determine whether a prospect is a good customer *before* they buy. Not every paying customer is worth having.
  - When to use: When some customers cost more time, energy, risk, or money than they're worth (Progressive Insurance funnels bad risks to competitors).
  - How: Define your ideal customer; ask screening questions; turn away or redirect poor fits.
- **Visualization**: Help prospects imagine what life will be like once they accept your offer; the mind automatically simulates consequences (the car test drive shifts buyers from comparing to *wanting*).
  - When to use: At the moment of consideration, when you need to move a prospect from rational comparison to emotional desire.
  - How: Expose the prospect to as much sensory information as possible (test drive, hands-on product, vivid description).
- **Hook**: A single phrase or sentence conveying the offer's primary benefit (Apple's "1,000 songs in your pocket"; *The 4-Hour Workweek*).
  - When to use: Anywhere a prospect first encounters you — title, tagline, website, business card.
  - How: Brainstorm benefit-related words, combine into short phrases, generate many options, pick the one that grabs Attention fastest.
- **Call-to-Action (CTA)**: Direct prospects to take one single, simple, obvious next action ("Take exit 25 and turn right…"). Attention is wasted if the prospect disappears.
  - When to use: Every marketing message, without exception.
  - How: State one action clearly (visit, enter email, call, buy); make WHERE/WHY/WHAT/what-happens obvious. The best CTAs ask for the sale or for Permission. "If you think you're being too obvious, you're doing it right."
- **Permission**: Ask to follow up after providing Free value, rather than interrupting strangers (Godin). Permission is a reusable, low-cost asset.
  - When to use: After delivering genuine value to a new prospect.
  - How: Ask if you may continue providing value; state exactly what they'll get and how it benefits them; never abuse the privilege with spam.

## Key Concepts
- **Attention**: The scarce, rationed resource you compete for against everything else in a prospect's life; must be earned by being more interesting or useful.
- **Receptivity**: How open a person is to your message; has two components — *what* and *when* — and is shaped by the medium/form.
- **Probable Purchaser**: The specific type of person perfectly suited to your offer; focus resources here instead of "everyone."
- **Point of Market Entry**: The moment someone crosses the interest threshold (e.g., expecting a baby); reaching them then makes you the standard competitors are judged against.
- **Addressability**: How easily you can reach people who might want your offer; embarrassing topics have low Addressability, public gatekeepers (doctors, yoga studios) high.
- **Desire**: The visceral wanting that effective marketing intersects with — you can't manufacture it, only connect your offer to what people already want.
- **Framing**: Emphasizing critically important details and de-emphasizing the rest; inevitable in all communication, and not the same as lying.
- **Free**: Giving genuine value away to attract Attention quickly; subsidized by the sales it generates — but attention alone doesn't pay the bills.
- **Narrative**: A story (often the Hero's Journey) that makes your offer better; customers want to be the Hero, so testimonials and case studies are powerful.
- **Reputation**: What people generally think of your offer or company ("branding" demystified); not directly controllable, only improvable over time.

## Mental Models
- Use **End Result** framing when a prospect is comparing features — sell the hole, not the drill.
- Use **Point of Market Entry** timing when *when* you reach someone matters more than *who* — get there first and become the default standard.
- Use **Free + Permission** when reaching new people is expensive — earn Attention with value, then convert costly cold outreach into cheap warm follow-up.
- Use **Narrative** when prospects need confidence to act — show them someone like them who already walked the path and "vanquished the foe."

## Anti-patterns
- **Chasing attention for its own sake**: A pink bunny suit gets attention but no sales; broad publicity (national TV, viral) often fails to deliver paying customers.
- **Trying to appeal to everyone**: 99.999% would rather not give you money; marketing Harleys on *Oprah* wastes limited resources.
- **Feature-dumping**: Comfortable but ineffective; prospects care about benefits, not what the offer *does*.
- **Spamming / interruption**: Blanketing undifferentiated masses; filters now make people actively avoid you.
- **Over-giving Free value**: Chasing "going viral" while starving the profit-generating parts of the business.

## Worked Example
Apple, launching the first iPod, faced a category where rivals advertised "geekspeak": megabytes of disk space. That message addressed features, not the **End Result**, and grabbed no **Attention**. Apple instead built a **Hook** around the primary benefit — "1,000 songs in your pocket." Five words let prospects **Visualize** carrying their entire music collection in one elegant device, intersecting with an existing **Desire** for portable music. The Hook went on the packaging, ads, and store displays as the first thing prospects saw. Result: ~236,000 first-generation units sold in under a year. The Hook grabbed Attention; product quality closed the sale.

## Key Takeaways
1. Marketing finds and attracts the *right* people inexpensively; it gets you noticed, but Sales closes the deal.
2. Attention is scarce and rationed — earn it by being Remarkable, useful, or genuinely Free, never boring.
3. Sell the End Result (benefit/emotion tied to a Core Human Drive), not features.
4. Focus narrowly on your Probable Purchaser, and reach them at their Point of Market Entry through Addressable channels.
5. You can't create Desire, only intersect with what people already want — then help them convince themselves.
6. Every message needs one clear Call-to-Action: ask for the sale or for Permission to follow up.
7. Reputation is the most effective marketing there is, earned over time by making people glad they chose you.

## Connects To
- **Ch 4 (Sales / Value Delivery)**: Marketing hands qualified, attentive prospects to Sales; the CTA "ask for the sale" is the literal bridge, and Education-Based Selling extends the Desire principle.
- **Core Human Drives (Ch 1)**: End Result and Desire both reduce to connecting your offer to fundamental human wants.
- **Reputation / Expectation Effect**: Framing and honest marketing protect long-term Reputation; misrepresentation violates expectations and destroys it.
