## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**Speak the Language of Business.** Every business, everywhere, speaks one language: money — money made, money saved, money your skills will make and save. Don't merely speak it, *think* in it. Nouns and verbs beat adjectives and adverbs because they're closer to actuality; nouns and verbs *linked to numbers* beat everything. Convert an activity list ("made 15 sales calls") into an achievement list ("made 15 sales calls to customers representing $XXX in FY20XX business"). (Ch 2)

**Inventory achievement, not stuff — then convert to transferable skills.** A *skill* is affixed to one job ("I know how to sell the Model A Widget") — a good fit and a dead end, because it invites your boss to imagine someone cheaper in the same socket. A **transferable (portable) skill** is know-how you own and carry ("I know how to sell"). Achievements answer "What have you done for me lately?"; transferable skills answer "What will you do for me next?" — and the future is what you're actually selling. (Ch 2)

**"I think I am a verb."** Grant and Fuller used the identical self-description. Convert adjective → verb → verb+object: "I am persuasive" (a trait) → "I persuade" → "I persuade people." Every transferable skill works on **People, Data, or Things**; pick the principal category, then place yourself on the ladder — *following directions < communicating < persuading < negotiating < coaching and mentoring*. Perceived height on the ladder is perceived value: claim *persuading* and you keep the sales job; claim *negotiating* and you open account executive; demonstrate *mentoring* and you look like a VP. (Ch 3)

**Your boss is a customer.** He trades value (a paycheck) expecting value (your productivity). So: a good salesperson makes a sale; a **great salesperson creates a customer**. Keeping your job is the single sale — the desperation play. Making him want to do business with you *next* year is the goal. And a salesperson's best prospect is always a current satisfied customer, never a cold call — which is why your current boss is your best prospect for advancement. (Ch 2, Ch 8)

**Approach every boss conversation as an offer, not a report or a request.** Nine times out of ten these are one or the other. Reframe as a negotiation — value for value. Prepare beforehand (spontaneity is overrated; face time is too valuable to improvise), focus on what your customer wants, and ask: what good news can I bring? What concern of his can I raise? What problem of his can I offer to solve? (Ch 8)

**Features vs. benefits.** Features are your skills; **benefits are how applying them makes your boss feel**. FedEx's feature was reliable overnight delivery; the advertised benefit was peace of mind. Of positioning's three dimensions — Benefit, Target, Competition — target is largely chosen for you and competition is a given, so **benefit is the one dimension entirely yours to control**. (Ch 4)

**The Eternal Truth, both parts.** *Part 1*: however you present yourself, however liked you are, there is no substitute for knowing your job and doing it well. *Part 2*: being well liked can hold your job longer than your numbers merit — but only *postpones* the consequence — and **being a top performer guarantees nothing.** Satisfy both: top-quality work *and* the right feelings. (Ch 8)

**Rapport is cumulative, and pronouns are the cheapest instrument.** Rapport = mutual trust and emotional affinity, built and eroded by interactions great and small. Move every conversation from *I* and *you* to **we, us, our**: "You've really got a problem" → "*We've* really got a problem" → "What should *we* do about it?" Trading *you* for *we* says you're thinking of the other person, which invites reciprocity — two people thinking of each other is rapport in a nutshell. (Ch 5)

**FIDO — Forget It, Drive On.** The first test on any irritation. Most causes of anger pass quickly; why enshrine a transitory annoyance with destructive permanence? When it fails the test, act on the **situation**, never the person: situations can be fixed, people are not readily fixable. Address issues, not egos. (Ch 5)

**Conflict is physics, not pathology.** Without friction your feet slip and you can never stop. Patton: *"If everybody is thinking the same way, nobody is thinking."* Your job as **office diplomat** — as opposed to office politician — is not to eliminate conflict but to manage it: label the issues and list them publicly, build first in the areas of agreement so everyone gains a stake, then push "not working" into "working" with named owners. And an *executive* is someone who **executes** — write the memo, set criteria and deadlines, follow through. (Ch 6)

**You do not have to fight every battle.** Successful generals walk away from as many battles as they fight. Before engaging, ask: is it worth the energy? Worth risking goodwill? Have they already decided? Can you live with them winning? Does conceding buy future chips? Does this have to be solved *now*? The exception: a team about to choose a destructive course. (Ch 6)

**Brand yourself the problem solver — and never the problem creator.** *Problem solver* is the one position that can never be filled redundantly. Its inverse is the real reason most people are fired: not inability, not missing skills, not falling below productivity minimums, but being **high-maintenance**. No boss says it aloud, but she's thinking *I fired him because I just don't like him.* (Ch 7)

**Work that is late is value that is lost.** Time, more than money, is business's most chronic shortage — hence *dead*line. Be the **clutch player**. If you miss one, give an explanation *with a plan*, never an excuse. (Ch 7)

**Wear the smile as discipline.** Patton practiced his scowl in a mirror and called it his war face; yours is a smile. Try to make not a single negative observation. Never walk through the boss's open door to complain. And competence is no refuge: HR research consistently shows most people would rather work with a marginally incompetent but friendly colleague than a super-capable whiner. (Ch 7)

**Career kryptonite.** The very assets that make you exceptional are the liabilities that expose you to others' envy and insecurity. The conformist is a generic plug in a generic socket, first out in any downturn; the striver who lifts her head risks getting it lopped off. Watch three fine lines: confidence vs. arrogance, decisive vs. reckless, calculated risk vs. foolish gamble. And remember **Patton's foxhole**: hunkering down feels safer and turns a moving target into a stationary one. When the danger signs appear, engage *more*, not less. (Ch 10)

**Fit in while standing out.** Teamwork vs. individual recognition is a false choice — work in a team context but so hard and smart that your efforts are recognized. Teamwork is a stage with more than one spotlight, not a mass grave. **Your value is measured as your value to others.** (Ch 10)

**Give credit generously; promote yourself relentlessly.** One failure to share credit can undo months of corporate karma. But the only blunder more quickly fatal is failing to give *yourself* credit — no one knows the scope and benefit of your achievements better than you. Both require the same foundation: a record of genuine, measurable value. (Ch 10)

**Your commitment is to the firm, not to the position you happen to occupy** — and your deepest loyalty is first and foremost to yourself. Everything you build is portable. (Ch 7, Ch 12)
