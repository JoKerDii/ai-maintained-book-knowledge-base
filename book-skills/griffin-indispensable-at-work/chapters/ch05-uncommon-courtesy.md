# Chapter 5: Uncommon Courtesy

## Core Idea
You don't just work with these people — you live with them for about as many waking hours as you live with your family. Rapport (mutual trust and emotional affinity) is built and destroyed in interactions great and small; go the extra mile on *common* courtesy and it becomes uncommon, and so do you.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Korporate Karma**: choose which cliché you build your workplace on.
  - "Nice guys finish last" (Leo Durocher, 1939) vs. "What goes around comes around" — backed by the Golden Rule and the Hindu/Buddhist idea of karma.
  - Why it matters: the workplace isn't static, it's continually shaped by what you and others do. Follow Durocher and you help build a dog-eat-dog gladiatorial arena you must then inhabit for half your waking life. Follow the karmic model and you help create the collaborative success your own well-being depends on. If members value the community they have a stake in, they will value *you* as part of it.
  - Caveat: this is not passivity. Being a "nice guy" by rolling into a ball and letting come what may gets you kicked around and eventually kicked out. Good karma requires the *active* exercise of walking a step beyond your own ego — the vigorous application of empathy.

- **The Helping Vocabulary**: accelerate rapport through pronoun choice.
  - Foundation: the first-person plural — **we, us, our**. Look for every occasion to move a conversation from *I* and *you* to *we*.
  - Build on it with words compatible with collaboration: *brainstorm, collaborate, confer, cooperate, huddle, learn, listen, solve, team up, work together.*
  - Why it works: trading *you* for *we* conveys that you're always thinking of the other person, which invites them to reciprocate. Two people thinking of each other is rapport in a nutshell.

- **Exclusionary Language (the rapport wrecker)**: *I, mine, you, yours* instead of the inclusive plurals, plus words that negate, deny, contradict, reject, or refuse — *afraid, bad luck, cannot, crisis, delay, fail, fault, fear, final, forgot, impossible, lose, nonnegotiable, stupid, tired.*

- **Exclusionary Behavior (seven categories)**:
  1. Inappropriate language — off-color, tasteless, or thoughtless comments, rude jokes, demeaning nicknames. (Beyond wrecking rapport, these can be legally construed as creating a "hostile work environment," exposing you and the company to civil and possibly criminal liability.)
  2. Habitual tardiness — broadcasts four destructive messages: you're disorganized, you're incompetent, you lack respect, you don't care.
  3. Sloppy or disrespectful dress and grooming — no formal dress code doesn't mean no dress code. **The colleagues and bosses you most admire *are* your firm's dress code.**
  4. Bad phone and email manners — not picking up, not returning calls, not relaying messages, holds, talking before the receiver is in position, eating while talking, mumbling, slamming down; and by email: not answering, forwarding what the sender wouldn't want shared, deluging with nonessentials, illiterate writing.
  5. Failure to greet warmly — a limp handshake and a bonecrusher both wreck rapport; so do avoided eye contact, frowns, hand-wringing, sighs.
  6. Failure to listen *or* to show you're listening — maintain eye contact, nod, widen your eyes, don't interrupt, ask relevant questions.
  7. Intruding on space or privacy; plus generally swinish behavior (not replacing copier paper, pilfering break-room food).

- **Proxemics — the Territorial Imperative**: treat every cubicle as an office with a closed door.
  - Never enter an absent colleague's cubicle except to deliver something or leave a note. Wait outside until the owner arrives for a scheduled meeting. Ask before rearranging furniture ("Do you mind if I move this chair closer?"). Pick up after yourself, cup included. Don't set a cup on someone's desk without asking — the desk is the inner sanctum. Don't borrow without permission; return what you borrow. Never snoop or appear to snoop.
  - Shaping your own territory: pay most attention to your **chair** — what we sit on transmits a powerful nonverbal message about power. An expensive-looking chair reads as a throne, a cheap one as a footstool; armrests read as executive, no armrests as assistant. Position your chair so you can lean back without touching a wall — give the impression of space to spare. Personalize within company policy: awards and certificates always appropriate; **avoid** nude or suggestive art, political slogans, humorous or off-color material, religious images/symbols/texts, childish decorations like stuffed animals, and military memorabilia especially weaponry. Keep it neat; avoid questionable screensavers.
  - Governing principle: *everything you put in your office speaks for and about you — broadcasting not simply who you are, but who you think you are and who you want others to think you are.*
  - Assertion without aggression: "Joe, I need my stapler back, please." / "Jack, next time you get here before I do, please wait outside."

- **Asking for Help Beats Offering It**: a request for assistance is a vote of confidence that empowers the other person.
  - Before asking, identify the best sources: Who does what job? Who commands influence and enjoys respect? Who is always in the loop? Who do others usually ask? Who makes the important decisions? — answering these also identifies the people worth connecting with.
  - Calibrate urgency: urgent → "Tom, I need your help." Not urgent → "Tom, I need your help with something. When's a good time to talk?" No hurry → "Tom, mind if I pick your brain about something?"
  - When asked for help, the only proper response is to give it — and if you can't help directly, help find someone who can: "Alice, I've never used one of those machines, but Esther has. Let me get her on the phone for you."
  - When offering, be proactive but never judgmental: ask permission ("Bill, can I help you with that?"), never "You sure look like you can use some help."

- **The 12 Rules of Constructive Criticism**:
  1. Never criticize *anyone* — address an issue, project, action, or outcome; never a person, trait, attitude, or personality.
  2. Confirm the situation actually requires criticism: *Is this worth criticizing?*
  3. Confirm it can be improved or corrected. If it can't be fixed, hold your tongue.
  4. Always ask permission: "Would you like to hear my take on this?"
  5. Choose the right place and time; never criticize in front of others. Make an appointment.
  6. Back it with substance — cite specific problems, incidents, actions.
  7. Arm yourself with alternatives. Never tear down without helping build up.
  8. Adopt an informal, friendly tone; avoid command language ("you must," "you have to").
  9. Temper with praise where possible; don't let them feel like a complete failure.
  10. One issue at a time — no serial criticisms.
  11. Don't hit and run. Follow up with positive feedback: "Bill, thanks for making those changes. They've made a real difference! We're up by at least 20 percent."
  12. No threats or dramatic predictions — but don't minimize or sugarcoat facts either. Be objective and factual. Address issues, not egos.

- **The FIDO Formula**: **F**orget **I**t. **D**rive **O**n.
  - When to use: first decision on any irritation — most causes of anger pass quickly. Why act in ways that enshrine a transitory annoyance with destructive permanence?
  - When it fails the test: if a *situation* is making your work life miserable or hurting productivity, act — and note the word *situation*, which is not synonymous with *person*. Situations can be fixed; people are not so readily fixable.

- **The Four-Component Apology**: (1) saying you are sorry; (2) an embrace of responsibility — you own the error; (3) an expression of empathy — you understand the other person's feelings; (4) an offer of remedy or a promise to help resolve it, moving from *I/you* to *we/us*.
  - Must be **timely**: don't wait to be asked for an explanation; be proactive, apology plus explanation.
  - Frame: every time you err you give someone the opportunity to forgive — to act in a godlike manner. Managed correctly, an apology eases your guilt while making the other person feel good.

- **Commemorate and Celebrate**: appoint yourself keeper of birthdays, work anniversaries, births, graduations; send the announcing email; organize the lunch. Present a collection for a gift as an *opportunity to contribute*, never a demand — and check company policy, since some firms prohibit solicitation for any reason.
  - On loss: express condolences personally; the fear of intruding on grief is misplaced, because grief is lonely and your words make it less solitary. Ask permission before informing others ("Pete, I'm so sorry for your loss. Would you like me to let the others know?"). On their return, add a personal detail focused on the life lived rather than the life lost.

## Key Concepts
- **Rapport** — a relationship of mutual trust and emotional affinity; cumulative, built and eroded by interactions great and small.
- **Proxemics** — the anthropological study of how we define and use personal space.
- **Helping vocabulary** — inclusive pronouns plus collaboration verbs.
- **FIDO** — Forget It, Drive On.
- **Criticism as perception** — not gospel truth, not to be dismissed; a signal about how you're coming across even when factually unfounded.

## Mental Models
- **Sell yourself the way a great salesperson sells.** All successful salespeople do two things: take the focus off themselves, and put it on the customer. They don't open with a declaration; they ask questions to find out how to satisfy you. The one word for that process is empathy — and the motive isn't altruism: to get, you must give, and to give effectively you must know what the other person wants.
- **Be grateful for criticism.** Either it's useful and constructive, or — even if off-base — the act of criticizing shows the person cares about what you're doing. You are having an effect. You are being recognized.
- **Address issues, not egos; create empathy, not victory.** In conflict, your goal isn't to win by defeating him, it's to make him see reality from your perspective. Success usually produces an apology you accept gracefully and briefly, looking forward.
- **If you can't resolve a hot situation, interrupt it.** It takes two to argue, so remove yourself: "Bob, this is getting too hot. I'll be back in about half an hour. Let's see if we can take this up more productively then." Guilt by association with unproductive drama makes you look anything but indispensable.

## Anti-patterns
- **Apologizing for asking for help** — "I'm sorry to bother you . . ." Stop thinking of the request as taking something; you're *giving* the opportunity to be helpful.
- **Apologizing after receiving help** — "I'm really sorry I had to bother you" tells the helper she should feel annoyed. You want her to feel happy she helped.
- **Belittling yourself** — "I'm an idiot when it comes to these things." Never plant a negative assessment of your own competence. Anything you say can be used against you.
- **Vague praise** — "You're doing a wonderful job" suggests insincerity. Say "Cutting turnaround time by half an hour really means a lot." Nouns and verbs over adjectives and adverbs.
- **Patronizing praise** — "*You* did *this*! I'm amazed!"
- **Praise that looks like sucking up** — avoid it by congratulating the boss for specific achievements only.
- **Telling the person how they make you feel.** Focus on the *issue* they're responsible for and how that issue makes you feel: "Bob, when you took credit for the widget idea, I really felt hurt."
- **The three impulses when criticized** — respond defensively and angrily; dig in and refuse to listen; fold up and abandon all self-confidence. All three destroy rapport.
- **Surrendering to criticism as gospel.** Listening carefully is not the same as agreeing. Listen, think, then explore the substance with yourself and the critic.
- **Making the apology about your own feelings.** "I feel awful about this" doesn't help the other person and may add guilt to their load. Keep it objective, add empathy immediately, then offer remedy.
- **Becoming the office ghoul** who always broadcasts bad news, or the jerk who disrupts work several times a week to celebrate something.
- **Gossiping about an angry exchange** afterward. (If you feel physically threatened, remove yourself *and* talk to a supervisor or Security.)

## Worked Example

**Building rapport through pronouns, four responses to the same complaint.** Boss: "I can't seem to get Auditing to deliver their monthly reports on time."
> **Response 1** — "You've really got a problem." Shows you're listening, but *you* separates the boss from you. Rapport is never about separating; it's about joining.
> **Response 2** — "*We've* really got a problem." The plural joins you.
> **Response 3** — "What are *you* planning to do about it?" Shows interest, but tears down what you just built.
> **Response 4** — "Well, what should *we* do about it?" ✔

**The four-component apology in practice:**
> **You:** Ted, I apologize for being late with that data update. I understand I put you in an uncomfortable situation. I'm sorry.
> **Ted:** Well, the boss wasn't very happy with me.
> **You:** I bet you were ticked off about that. I'm going to meet with the boss now, and I'll explain what happened. I didn't get the update on the first three tests on time. I don't know if I could have done anything about that, but I certainly should have alerted you. I didn't mean to leave you blindsided like that. I'll let him know what happened.
> **Ted:** I'd appreciate that. Thanks.

Sincere without being dramatic; apology → ownership → empathy → remedy.

**Accepting an apology and looking forward:** "Thanks, Bob. I appreciate your apology, and I understand that you didn't mean to cut me out of the loop. What you said will make it easier for us to continue to do great things with the project."

## Key Takeaways
1. Rapport is cumulative and fragile — every interaction, however small, either builds or erodes it.
2. Trade *I/you* for *we/us/our* and layer on collaboration verbs; that alone accelerates rapport.
3. The colleagues and bosses you most admire are your company's real dress code.
4. Asking for help builds more rapport than offering it, because a request is a vote of confidence — and never apologize for asking or for having been helped.
5. Criticize issues, never people; get permission first, be specific, offer alternatives, one issue at a time, and follow up.
6. Run every irritation through FIDO first; act only when a *situation* — not a person — is damaging work.
7. A good apology is timely and has four parts: sorry, ownership, empathy, remedy.
8. Make yourself the community's commemorator, and go the extra mile so *common* courtesy becomes uncommon — and so do you.
