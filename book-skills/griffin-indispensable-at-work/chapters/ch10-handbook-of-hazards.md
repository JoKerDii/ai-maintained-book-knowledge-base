# Chapter 10: A Handbook of Hazards

## Core Idea
Career kryptonite: the very assets that make you an exceptional achiever can be the liabilities that bring you down. You can't eliminate the hazards of standing out, but through self-awareness and strategic vigilance you can manage them — and the foxhole of mediocre conformity is more dangerous than advancing.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Moral of Kryptonite**: you may be very hard to kill, but you are not unkillable.
  - The tension: the plodding, clock-punching conformist is a generic plug in a generic socket, first out in any downturn and limited even in boom times — you don't want to be that guy. But the boldly ambitious striver who lifts her head above the crowd risks getting it lopped off, in good times or bad.
  - The three fine lines: **attractive self-confidence** vs. obnoxious arrogance; **productively decisive** vs. impulsively reckless; **a calculated risk** vs. a foolish gamble. What raises you above an expendable commodity also exposes you to the envy and insecurity of others.
  - Management, not elimination: prepare carefully before acting; be mindful of the feelings and needs of others; always assess with realistic objectivity the **proportion of risk to reward** before deciding.

- **Patton's Foxhole**: under heavy fire, soldiers were taught to dig in. Patton argued this created only the *illusion* of safety — it transformed an advancing army into sitting ducks. A stationary target is far easier to hit than a moving one. Although it feels safer, sitting in a foxhole is more dangerous than advancing.

- **The Ten Danger Signs** that you're under assault:
  1. A promotion you expected and deserved went to a coworker who, by your objective judgment, was significantly less qualified.
  2. People who used to ask your advice no longer do.
  3. In a meeting you find yourself under a barrage of unwarranted criticism.
  4. You aren't invited to the boss's pool party (an "oversight," more or less apologized for).
  5. You've become the subject of office gossip.
  6. People randomly ask, "Is there anything wrong?"
  7. You're being scapegoated for snafus that could not possibly be your fault.
  8. "Friends" are no longer very friendly.
  9. Your ability to assemble ad hoc work groups and project teams has diminished or evaporated — you can no longer rely on colleagues' cooperation and goodwill.
  10. Email queries and requests go unanswered; you have to nudge and nag.
  - The counterintuitive response: **the time for greater engagement in office politics is precisely when you feel yourself coming under assault.** Isolation is not just the enemy of advancement, it's the first step out the door. Fight the impulse to press deeper into the foxhole; stand up, move forward, make yourself harder to hit.

- **Sell Your Influence (four moves)**: the more ideas and initiatives you sell to others, especially bosses, the more influential you become — and the most influential people are those most thoroughly woven into the workplace fabric. Perceived power becomes actual power.
  1. **Begin with one idea or proposal.** Identify someone with the power to help and use them as a **lever** — promoting an idea to an influential person multiplies your influence the way a lever multiplies arm strength.
  2. **Sell the idea by selling its benefits** *to that person specifically*.
  3. **Create a deed by means of pronoun.** No idea sells easier than one the other guy thinks he already owns — once buy-in begins, call it "our" concept, not "my" concept.
  4. **Recruit your critics.** Don't deflect or get discouraged by resistance — enlist it. If you can't beat 'em, make 'em join you.

- **Make — or Remake — Friends**: rebuilding relations when people drift away.
  - Keep it positive: **do not ask if anything is wrong, and do not imply you think something may be wrong.**
  - Escalate gently: lunch ("Fred, how about lunch? It's been awhile") or, if that feels too big, coffee ("Fred, I'm on the way to the break room. Can I bring you back a cup of coffee?").
  - Ask about something of interest to *them* — the son's soccer, the '67 project Pontiac, a mutual friend. Keep the focus off yourself.
  - **Cultivate, don't plow.** No agenda. You're restoring a collegial climate in which others see you as more than the occupant of a cubicle.

- **The Ethics Test — "Are you a thief?"** Not Bernie Madoff criminality; the ordinary rationalizations.
  - Ordering office supplies on the company's dime for home use ("everyone does it"). Thief.
  - Padding an expense report because "it's S.O.P., it's expected, practically mandatory." Thief.
  - Billing the client the six hours you estimated when the job took you four, because you're fast and he approved the estimate. Thief.
  - Consequences: your boss probably won't call 911, but she may fire you — especially if in hard times she's looking for an excuse — and at minimum she'll remember you at salary review not as the rep who consistently hits her numbers but as the person who stole a stapler and three reams of paper.
  - The two hazards: getting caught, and **contributing to an unethical environment** whose pollution spreads through the organization, then the business community; you may not be singled out, but your firm's reputation will suffer and yours with it.
  - Beyond compliance: **sell ethics, brand yourself as ethical** — make it a value-added benefit you offer internal and external customers. There is security and value in the proposition.

## The Eight Ways to Make Yourself Dispensable

1. **"I'm not interested in being a 'team player.'"** The least vulnerable employees fit in while standing out — a team player without surrendering individual voice, creativity, and judgment, all exercised for the team's benefit. Being branded "not a team player" is *not* the same as "out-of-the-box thinker," "real original," or "creative individual" — those are assets; this is a liability, period, and it makes you toxic. Teamwork vs. individual recognition is a **false choice**: work in a team context but so hard and smart that your individual efforts are recognized. Don't picture teamwork as a mass grave, but as a stage with more than one spotlight.
2. **"That's not my job."** Understand your job description; never get comfortable with it. Don't define yourself by it, and don't let others define you by it. Volunteer, take on extra work in related areas, seize initiative. *Settle back into your job description as if it were a lounge chair, and you may find it very hard to get up — ever.* At entry level: do the job, express no discontent, never suggest you see it as a stepping-stone (bosses hate that) — but promote your untapped skills as a value-added proposition.
3. **"There's no hurry."** Procrastination is a common cause of unemployment and underemployment. *Why do you think **dead** is part of the word deadline?* It risks not just late delivery but compromised quality — no double-checking, no proofreading, no fact-checking, ramifications unconsidered, checklists unchecked. On time and sloppy defeats the point.
4. **"I'm working till I drop."** Employers reward a high-quality work product, not the humming and hopping appearance of production. **You wouldn't purposely run a machine until it breaks down** — every worker has a point of diminishing returns. Forty hours may barely hold the job; sixty may make you too valuable to fire; seventy may reduce production, if not in quantity then in quality. Slow to the point of optimum production and maintain yourself at least as well as you'd maintain a fine, expensive machine.
5. **"I know my job."** Failing to keep technical skills current is a career killer. Stay at least on par with colleagues and with those coming up from the ranks and out of the schools. **The faintest whiff of obsolescence will cause all around you to hold their noses.** Better still: be the one who introduces technical innovation to your shop.
6. **"I'm efficient. What else do you want from me?"** It can be fatal to confuse **efficiency with effectiveness**. Email is efficient; walking down the hall to the head of production is often more effective. The very ease of digital communication makes the apparent inefficiency of face-to-face more valuable than ever. Give yourself IRL.
7. **"I did it, I did it, I did it."** A single failure to share credit for a collaborative achievement can tear down months or years of goodwill and corporate karma. Grabbing all the glory leaves you empty-handed.
8. **"Modesty forbids me."** The only blunder more quickly fatal than failing to give credit is failing to give *yourself* credit when it's due. **Self-promotion is the most important kind of promotion** because no one knows the scope, depth, and benefit of your achievements better than you do. Write, collect, and share case studies and promotion bulletins; inform bosses, colleagues, subordinates, and clients. But it requires a record of genuine achievement — there is no substitute for creating measurable value. *Stand on the platform of the value you produce and make yourself famous.*

*(And the ninth, which opens the next chapter: **"Networking is just a lot of busywork."** Too many of us shut down our job-hunting networks the moment we find employment — a career-stunting mistake.)*

## Key Concepts
- **Career kryptonite** — the strengths that make you exceptional and simultaneously vulnerable.
- **The foxhole illusion** — the felt safety of hunkering down, which actually makes you a stationary target.
- **The lever** — an influential person through whom your idea reaches people you can't reach.
- **Cultivate, don't plow** — rebuilding relationships without an agenda.
- **Efficiency vs. effectiveness** — the cheapest path to a result vs. the one that actually produces it.
- **Fit in while standing out** — the posture of the least vulnerable employee.

## Mental Models
- **Invulnerability makes for dull stories.** Achilles had a bum heel; Superman has kryptonite. You are hard to kill, not unkillable — plan accordingly.
- **A stationary target is easier to hit than a moving one.** Applies to careers under fire exactly as it did to Patton's infantry.
- **Perceived influence becomes real influence.** The more influential you make yourself look, the more powerful you're perceived to be, and as a result the more powerful you actually become.
- **Teamwork is a stage with more than one spotlight, not a mass grave.**
- **Your value is measured as your value to others.** That's the essence of teamwork, and the thing that keeps you employed while propelling you upward.
- **No reward without risk — but a calculated risk and a foolish gamble are not always easy to tell apart.** The distinguishing work is done before you act, in the objective assessment of risk-to-reward proportion.

## Anti-patterns
- **Digging deeper into the foxhole when the danger signs appear.** The correct move is more engagement, not less.
- **Asking "Is anything wrong?" while rebuilding a relationship** — or implying it.
- **Pushing an agenda during relationship repair.** Cultivate, don't plow.
- **Rationalizing petty theft by consensus** ("everyone does it," "it's expected"). Unethical conduct is unethical regardless of justification, prevalence, or your confidence you'd get away with it.
- **Treating your job description as a lounge chair.**
- **Signaling that an entry-level job is a stepping-stone.**
- **Working past your point of diminishing returns** and calling it dedication.
- **Choosing the efficient channel over the effective one** by default.
- **Letting modesty suppress your own record.**

## Worked Example

**Selling influence through a lever, with the pronoun shift and critic recruitment.** You have an idea for a special customer support group. You identify Claire, your immediate supervisor, as the person with the clout to get it heard by the people who can approve it.
> **Sell the benefit to her, specifically:** "Claire, this customer support group will certainly gain *you* a stronger voice with upper management."
> **Then shift the pronoun** as buy-in begins: it stops being "my concept" and becomes "our concept."
> **And when she pushes back, recruit rather than defend:** "Claire, your criticism has given me food for thought. I need your input to develop the support group further so that we can make it work."

**Expanding beyond your job description as a value-added proposition (entry level):**
> "I'd like to expand my responsibilities to include research. You know I have training in that area. Why not let me give you the benefit of it? I can guarantee that the fact-checking you hired me for will get done in full and on schedule, but I know you can use another set of brains on research. I'm willing to put in the extra time."

Note the structure: the existing job is guaranteed first, the new work is framed as a benefit to the boss, and the extra time is volunteered — no discontent, no stepping-stone signal.

## Key Takeaways
1. The transferable skills that make you exceptional are the same ones that expose you to envy and insecurity — manage the hazard, you can't eliminate it.
2. The foxhole of mediocre conformity feels safer and is more dangerous; keep moving.
3. Learn the ten danger signs, and respond to them with *more* engagement, not withdrawal.
4. Multiply influence through a lever, sell benefits to that person, convert "my" to "our," and recruit your critics.
5. Rebuild drifting relationships positively and without agenda; never ask whether something is wrong.
6. Small rationalized thefts — supplies, padded expenses, over-billed hours — are theft, and they hand a boss looking for a reason exactly what she needs.
7. Fit in while standing out; "not a team player" is a toxic label that "creative individual" is not.
8. Guard against procrastination, burnout, obsolescence, and mistaking efficiency for effectiveness.
9. Share credit relentlessly — and promote yourself relentlessly, on a platform of genuine measured value.
