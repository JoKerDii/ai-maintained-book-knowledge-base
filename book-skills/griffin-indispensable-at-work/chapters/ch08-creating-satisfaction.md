# Chapter 8: Creating Satisfaction

## Core Idea
Your boss is a customer — somebody who trades value (a paycheck) expecting value (your productivity). Deliver top-quality work *and* create the right feelings, treat every conversation as an offer rather than a report or a request, and learn enough about leadership to see the world from his side of the desk.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Eternal Truth, in two parts** — both changeless, and you must satisfy both.
  - **Part 1**: However you present yourself, however much you are liked, there is no substitute for knowing your job and doing it well. Consistently unsatisfactory work loses you the job.
  - **Part 2**: Being well liked may let you hold your job far longer than your by-the-numbers performance merits — but absent satisfactory performance it only *postpones* termination. And the significant half: **being a top performer does not guarantee you'll keep the job, let alone advance.** Life isn't fair; the task isn't to lament the "maybe not," it's to minimize it by delivering both quality and feelings.

- **Setting the Tone Without Saying a Word** — regulate how you *act*, not what you feel. Trying to deny a feeling is like dieting: try to diet and you think of nothing but food; try to deny a feeling and it becomes uppermost. Let feelings fend for themselves.
  - **Breathing**: anxiety produces rapid, shallow breathing, and the voice of fear — thin, tight, quavering, high-pitched, utterly unpersuasive, the tone many adopt with authority figures. Counterintuitively, deep breaths are *not* the best fix for hyperventilation. In private, **pant rapidly, concentrating more on exhaling than inhaling** — this raises blood carbon dioxide, which automatically slows breathing to a relaxed rate.
  - **Pitch**: a deep voice reads as more persuasive and authoritative, for men and women alike. A soprano can't become a bass, but anyone can practice the lower register of the voice they have and make it their public voice.
  - **Pace**: lowering pitch naturally slows you and improves enunciation. Speaking at a pace that gives each word weight makes you sound intelligent and articulate — and keeps you from running short of breath. "Fast talker" is a synonym for a swindler; at minimum, talking fast says you're uncomfortable and want to make your getaway.

- **Act Your Age** — four childish tells that surface when a boss triggers a parent–child script. You don't have to *feel* your age; just don't act on the feeling, and practicing adult gestures gradually makes you feel more adult.
  1. **Failure to make and maintain eye contact.** Look him in the eye, one adult to another — though the "stare down" is childish behavior of another kind.
  2. **Fidgeting and squirming.** Gesture, look alive — but make movements purposeful and meaning-carrying.
  3. **Covering your mouth or touching your face**, especially while speaking. Adults keep hands away from the face.
  4. **Hands in pockets.** An unmistakable throwback, and it looks like you're hiding something. Keep hands visible and use open gestures, palms up — suggesting honesty and openness.

- **The Timing Rules**: get in synch with your workplace's rhythms; observe your boss's daily routine and learn the good and bad windows.
  - Unless pressing, avoid Monday conversations that could happen Tuesday.
  - Avoid raising important, complex, or disturbing issues right before lunch, at the very end of the day, before a weekend, or before either of you takes time off. Don't raise an action item you won't be present to act on, and don't give your boss something to stew about on the beach.
  - "I haven't got much time" / "I'm off to a meeting" / "I've just got a minute" all mean *I don't want to talk right now.* Unless the issue is genuinely urgent **to the organization**, come back later.

- **Make an Offer**: nine times out of ten a boss conversation is a report or a request. Reframe it as an **offer** — a negotiation, a pledge to return value for value.
  - Prepare beforehand; spontaneity is overrated and face time is too valuable to improvise. Make a written outline, leave it behind, review it before the meeting, and consider rehearsing on a friend.
  - Focus on what the customer wants, not your needs. Ask yourself: what really good news can I bring? What concerns of his can I raise? What problems of his can I offer to solve?
  - Why: it's hard to feel persuasive with your hand out. Walking in with something to offer gives you confidence in your own case.

- **Empathy ≠ Sympathy**: empathy has nothing to do with feeling sorry for someone. It's the ability to put yourself in another's place and see reality from their point of view — the same faculty you'd use on a customer, turned on your boss.
  - The most effective route: **learn about the nature of leadership.** This both improves how you deal with your boss and prepares you to lead later.

- **Leadership-Grade Credibility (three elements)** — credibility is the universal quality that makes one worthy of leadership. (Note: job titles notwithstanding, not every boss is a leader.)
  1. **Leaders know what they know and what they don't know.** Confidence comes from knowing your job, your facts, and the basis for your own decisions — and, just as important, understanding the limits of your knowledge.
  2. **Leaders listen.** Leadership credibility has more to do with listening intelligently and productively than with speaking.
  3. **Leaders are not defensive.** They express themselves with self-assurance, then invite response including frank criticism. An effective leader never shouts down critics; she learns from them.

- **Boss Imperfect — the four categories.** Begin by assuming the best and giving the benefit of the doubt; adjust with experience. Identifying deficiencies and sore spots is essential to building mutual loyalty.
  1. **My Way or the Highway (the authoritarian).** Prefers monologue to dialogue; asks questions to keep you on your toes rather than to gather information; demands answers rather than soliciting them; makes threats beginning "You'd better," always implying "or else"; shuns spontaneity; quotes rules and policy; sees the chain of command rigidly — *how hard a mid-level authoritarian comes down on you is directly proportionate to how hard he perceives his own bosses coming down on him*; reactive rather than proactive.
     - *Handling*: put him in perspective — what can he actually do to you? Can he fire you, and has he fired many before? Much of the style is just **style**; assess bark versus bite. Then satisfy this customer: make him feel good about something, anything, from his necktie to a recent decision; follow orders efficiently and to the letter, minimizing improvisation; **ask for advice** — always an empowering request, and the more power he believes he's acquiring the better he treats you.
  2. **Finger Pointers (the blamer).** Before labeling, assess objectively — criticizing results to improve performance is part of a manager's legitimate job. Assume the criticism is justified and helpful before concluding she's out to get you.
     - *Handling*: get all the facts, resisting a stream of denials — say you'll research it, get the facts, and make it right, shifting focus from emotions and personalities to causes, effects, and results. Confront the event, not the accusation. **Accept responsibility without accepting blame.** Respond with a promise of cooperation rather than denial, contradiction, or challenge.
  3. **The Bumbler.** Judge results, not personality: does he routinely forget appointments, lose details, thrash instead of deciding, or miss production and revenue targets?
     - *Handling*: be patient and never express exasperation; resist complaining about him to others; dot your i's and cross your t's, confirming instructions and correcting errors without a mocking or patronizing tone; **put everything in writing**, confirming verbal instructions by email or memo; be as helpful as possible and provide guidance.
  4. **The Volatile Boss.** Screamers who take pride in it ("I'm passionate about what I do!"). Intimidation and fear don't motivate top performance — they motivate avoidance, and sometimes a job search.
     - *Handling*: master your own fear reaction first. Let the lava flow harmlessly around you; endure the tirade with a calm expression and unbroken eye contact, acting as if this were a routine conversation. Look for a chance to defuse: acknowledge the anger ("I can't blame you for being angry, but…") and introduce an alternative ("…but I need to talk this through with you to arrive at a solution"). Empower him with choices: "Do you want to sit down and discuss this now, or should I come back?" Then analyze and address the issues — **performance is the most effective antidote to an angry outburst.**

- **Taking a Compliment (it's business, not personal preference).** Your boss praises you for two reasons — to give you good feelings about the work and the relationship, and to reinforce the behavior so it repeats. Both are bonding opportunities.
  - Do: smile broadly; say thank you while smiling broadly; compliment the giver ("Coming from you, that really means something"); share praise with the team who deserve it.
  - Useful lines: *"I am grateful for the opportunity." "I had a great team." "I look forward to the next challenge." "It's a win for all of us." "That's great to hear." "Your support made this possible."*
  - Never: confess unworthiness ("I really don't deserve this"), or deliver a long speech. Avoid *"I almost didn't make it." "I did what I could." "I had a lot of good luck." "I'm surprised you're pleased." "It could have gone better." "It was a close call." "Really? I didn't think things went all that well."*

- **The Five-Step Mistake Report**: (1) acknowledge the error; (2) accept responsibility; (3) apologize — **briefly**; (4) offer suggestions for remedying it; (5) if you can't offer solutions yet, promise to find them.
  - Timing: report promptly, without panicked frenzy, but take enough time to assess the full nature and degree and to formulate solutions. It is far better the news come from you than from someone else — or worst, from your boss discovering it himself.
  - While reporting: deliver facts objectively; take responsibility without pointing fingers; **don't beat yourself up** — your boss isn't interested in watching you wallow in self-bestowed guilt, he wants the fix.

## Key Concepts
- **Internal vs. external customers** — colleagues, reports, and bosses vs. the people across the counter; none more important than your boss.
- **The "routine" salary review** — never routine: your raise is on the line, and in tough times it may be a prelude to a cut or a layoff.
- **Value for value received** — the frame for every boss conversation, and especially for raise and promotion talks.
- **Responsibility without blame** — owning a problem you didn't cause.
- **Failure as an option** — a viable one, because the attempt itself signals confidence in the firm.

## Mental Models
- **You can't please everybody — and should still set that impossibility as a goal.** You can't control what others feel, but you can influence the feelings that produce the actions you want, and you set the tone of the relationship.
- **No one can read your mind. All they can do is read your actions and your words.** So you don't need years of therapy before talking to your boss; you need a good act.
- **A promotion is easier to negotiate than a raise in a downturn.** A raise is a reward for value already delivered; a promotion is *both* a reward for past performance *and* your proposal to deliver greater value in future. So frame it as a set of additional responsibilities you're willing to take on — a deal, not a reward.
- **In salary negotiation, never bring your needs into the discussion.** You know why you need money — everything is expensive. That is not the argument. Make it about what you can deliver.
- **Skilled negotiators don't lay cards faceup.** The number *you* name automatically caps your salary for the year. Let your boss name one first.

## Anti-patterns
- **Approaching the boss as a supplicant** — a request for something in return for nothing.
- **Improvising face time.** Plan it; rehearse it if useful.
- **Confusing empathy with sympathy.** Feeling sorry for your boss is not seeing the world from his seat.
- **Labeling a boss a blamer before assessing objectively.** Criticism of results is part of the job.
- **Defending yourself too quickly against unjust blame** — instead of getting the facts and owning the outcome.
- **Complaining about a bumbling boss to others.**
- **Accepting the first salary offer**, even a satisfactory one. A counteroffer bonds you more strongly than acceptance and presents your picture of your own value.
- **Expressing dismay or threatening to job-hunt** when offered a cut. (You don't have to threaten — your boss probably assumes it.)
- **Wallowing in guilt while reporting an error.**

## Worked Example

**Accepting criticism from an authoritarian boss with gratitude:**
> **Boss:** I'm disappointed in you. Turnaround times have hardly improved.
> **You:** I'm disappointed, too. I expected more progress at this point, and I was about to come to you for your advice on the present situation. When can we get together and discuss a strategic approach to improving turnaround?

Note: agrees rather than defends, then converts the reprimand into a request for advice — the empowering move for this boss type.

**Responsibility without blame, to a finger pointer:**
> "I understand that the customer is unhappy; however, I did not make the promises he has told you about. Nevertheless, he *is* my customer, and I own the problem. I will resolve this to his satisfaction and yours."

**The salary review, end to end.** Walk in with two data sets: (1) highlights of the year's accomplishments, emphasizing dollars earned and saved; (2) what others in similar positions are paid — generally published by trade organizations in your field. Don't name a number; let the boss go first. Then:
> **Counteroffer:** "I appreciate the offer, but I believe the value I've delivered this past year — especially with regard to X, Y, and Z — merits more. This is the figure I was thinking of."
> **If there's no raise available:** "I know times are tough, and, for the good of the company, I'm willing to continue at my present salary for now. When can we schedule another salary review?"
> **If it's a cut:** "I know times are tough, and, for the good of the company, I'm willing to take the cut, but when can we schedule another salary review?" — no dismay, no threat, and a clear statement that you'll keep working at a high level to help put the company where it can pay you what you deserve.

## Key Takeaways
1. Both halves of the Eternal Truth bind: excellent work doesn't guarantee retention, and being liked only postpones the consequences of poor work.
2. Regulate action, not feeling — pant to reset your breathing, lower your pitch, slow your pace.
3. Watch the four childish tells; adult gestures eventually produce adult feelings.
4. Time your approaches to your boss's rhythms and never force a conversation he's signaling he doesn't want.
5. Convert every boss conversation from report or request into an offer of value for value.
6. Empathize by learning leadership: credibility means knowing the limits of your knowledge, listening, and being undefensive.
7. Diagnose your imperfect boss as authoritarian, finger pointer, bumbler, or volatile — each has a distinct handling protocol.
8. Take compliments as a business skill; report mistakes fast with facts, ownership, brief apology, and a remedy.
9. In salary talks never mention your needs, never name the first number, and always counteroffer.
10. Failure is an option — the attempt itself is a vote of confidence in the firm and satisfies the customer.
