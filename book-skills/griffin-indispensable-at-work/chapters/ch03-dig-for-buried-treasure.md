# Chapter 3: Dig for Buried Treasure

## Core Idea
Chapter 2 counted the sales floor; this chapter searches the back room and basement. Convert yourself from a noun into a verb, inventory every transferable skill you own across People / Data / Things, and climb the skill hierarchy — because the deeper you dig, the higher you go.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **"I Think I Am a Verb"**: define yourself by what you actively do, not by a static package of personality.
  - Origin: Ulysses S. Grant and R. Buckminster Fuller independently used the identical self-description. Grant elaborated: "A verb is anything that signifies *to be*; *to do*; or *to suffer*. I signify all three."
  - When to use: any time you're about to describe yourself in a trait or a title.
  - How — **Just Say It**: convert the adjective into a verb. "I am persuasive" (trait) → "I persuade" (action). Then convert the résumé job title into a skill: "sales representative" → decide whether the job chiefly involves people, data, or things → "I persuade people."

- **The Three Categories of Transferable Skills**: every transferable skill is about working with **People**, **Data**, or **Things**.
  - When to use: to classify any skill before ranking it.
  - How: a job may touch all three (selling cars = things, using performance stats = data), but pick the *principal* one. For a sales rep, that's people.

- **The Transferable Skills Ladder**: skills within a category are ordered by complexity, and complexity is value.
  - People-skill rungs, low to high: *following directions* → *communicating* → *persuading* → *negotiating* → *coaching and mentoring*.
  - When to use: to decide which skill to claim, and which to develop next.
  - How: the higher up the ladder you are *perceived* to be, the more valuable you appear. Convince your boss you **persuade** people → you keep your sales job. Convince him you **negotiate** → you open the door to account executive. Demonstrate **mentoring** → you look like a potential VP of Sales.
  - Why it matters (four reasons to dig): (1) higher skills get you away from the plug-and-socket mentality — they make you hard, maybe impossible, to replace; (2) higher-skill positions pay more, last longer, and promise more advancement; (3) higher-skill jobs are careers — "jobs with a future"; (4) at low skill levels employees follow directions, at high levels they practice creativity, so you get more authority and control.

- **The Three-Sheet Dig (blank-sheet method)**: a brute-force inventory of everything you can do.
  - How: three sheets headed PEOPLE, DATA, THINGS. Under each write "I am good at . . ." and complete it as many ways as possible. Target **at least 25 items per sheet** — more if you can. No editing, no censoring; that comes later.
  - Alternative (**inventory rating method**): use the hierarchical skill inventories, rating each 1–4 with 4 strongest, based on reality rather than on how you'd *like* reality to be. Note that within People/Data/Things groupings the skills are hierarchical, but the *Things* subgroups (Machinery & Vehicles, Materials, Construction, Living Things, Body Skills) are not hierarchical relative to each other.

- **The Three-Step Sentence Elaboration**: turn a bare skill into a compelling claim.
  - How: (1) start with "I am good at **negotiating**." (2) Affix an object: "I am good at negotiating **deals**." (3) Add a clause explaining the benefit: "I am good at negotiating deals **that please the buyer as well as the seller**."
  - Do this for each of your 8–10 top-level skills to produce a rich, eloquent, concise skills profile.

- **The Mentor Criteria (all four must hold)**: (1) you have regular access to them; (2) they're active in an area that interests you *and* fits your background, skills, and training; (3) they are willing to mentor you; (4) **they have the power to promote you**.
  - How to approach: ask for business-related advice first — mild flattery is appropriate ("Ms. Smith, you're the authority on XYZ in this office. How would you increase . . . ?"). Make a habit of asking questions; responsiveness identifies a good candidate.

- **The Offer-Value Promotion Script (5 rules)**: ask for nothing; offer instead.
  1. Say nothing about what you need.
  2. Propose all you can offer the department and company *provided you're given increased responsibility*.
  3. Propose all you can do for the boss — paint a self-portrait of a problem solver who makes their life easier and more successful.
  4. Build your case; don't assume your boss memorized your résumé. Present a list of solid achievements.
  5. Use a self-promotional vocabulary short on adjectives, long on verbs and numbers — the language of dollars made and saved.

## Key Concepts
- **Buried treasure** — the high-level transferable skills you already own but haven't displayed or claimed.
- **Plug-and-socket mentality** — the boss's view of your job as a generic slot and you as an interchangeable plug; the thing all this digging exists to escape.
- **Playing offense vs. playing defense** — Chapters 1–2 were defense (avoid being let go); from here on the goal is climbing, not hanging on.
- **The office grapevine** — a two-way channel: pluck rumors from it *and* plant information on it.
- **Taming a wild dream** — carving out a specialty as a platform from which to display transferable skills, rather than leaping straight to the far rung.
- **Career** — a job with a future.

## Mental Models
- **Be a verb, not a personal pronoun.** Your being consists of your doing. Even in thought, be a person of action.
- **People don't rise in an organization — they climb, and first they must find the ladder.** Why look outside for a ladder when there's one in your own shop? Your current boss is your best prospect precisely because he'd rather develop proven human capital than gamble on an unknown from outside.
- **Don't let a ladder become a fence.** The one-rung-at-a-time metaphor stops being useful when you play offense. Look *way* up. If a position several rungs above opens, pull for it. You may not make it, but you will have made a point about your ambition and commitment.
- **Stop being a [job title] and start being a problem solver.** Translate every request for specific knowledge into a demonstration of broader transferable skill. Then you're not the go-to guy for XYZ software — you're the go-to guy for problems, period. And problem solvers are indispensable.
- **A tough time is the best time to show optimism, faith in the firm, and eagerness to serve.** Bold, eager, able people are always valuable; in times that cry out for maximum effort, they're indispensable.

## Anti-patterns
- **Claiming a trait instead of a skill.** "I am persuasive" describes a static package. "I persuade people" describes a verb.
- **Editing while you dig.** Don't censor the three-sheet lists in the first pass — you lose the buried items. Winnow afterward.
- **Rating yourself as you'd like reality to be.** The inventory only works if the 1–4 ratings are honest.
- **Cultivating a powerless mentor.** Mentoring costs your time and theirs; don't spend it on someone who cannot promote you.
- **Asking for what you need.** The promotion conversation is an offer, not a request.
- **Waiting for the Hollywood moment** — the boss throwing an arm around your shoulder and announcing a promotion and a raise. Probably it never happened; it certainly doesn't now.
- **Passive use of the grapevine.** Listening only is half the tool. Put the word out — as specifically as you reasonably can: "It's about time for me to step into a supervisor position."

## Worked Example

**The dig (partial three-sheet output):**
> **People** — I am good at . . . persuading · selling · helping customers decide what to buy · explaining mechanical and technical things · patient listening
> **Data** — I am good at . . . finding · reporting · compiling · interpreting · analyzing · combining · drawing conclusions from
> **Things** — I am good at . . . sorting · arranging · choosing · assembling · creating · fixing · understanding · improving

**Winnowing:** cross out the weakest and lowest-level items (or, on the rating method, keep only 3s and 4s; if still over ten, keep only the 4s). Land on 8–10. Then elaborate each in three steps.

**Reframing a request as a skill demonstration:**
> **He:** I can't figure out how to format this document. Are you any good with this software?
> **You:** I don't specialize in that program, but, tell you what, I've always been a problem solver. Let me take a look for you.

**The offer-value promotion pitch (customer service rep proposing a new role):**
> "Working with you has taught me a great deal about the relationship between sales and customer service. I'd like to put what I've learned to work for us. My experience has convinced me that service reps could and should be working more effectively with sales reps to increase sales, especially up-selling. Right now there is a gap between sales and service. I want to close that gap. You know that in the six months I've been working with you, sales have increased by an average of 12 percent — most of it in up-selling and accessories, and much of it from work I've done with the service reps, encouraging them to promote merchandise themselves rather than send customers back to sales. So far this has been casual, in addition to my other duties. I would like to propose a new position — service sales manager — and I would like to design it and, of course, fill it. I can promise even better numbers than I've delivered so far."

Note the structure: history → insight → named gap → quantified evidence → the ask framed as a proposal he designs and fills → a forward promise. He never says what he needs.

## Key Takeaways
1. Convert traits into verbs and job titles into functions: "I am persuasive" → "I persuade" → "I persuade people."
2. All transferable skills work on People, Data, or Things — pick the principal category before you rank.
3. Skills form a complexity ladder; perceived height on the ladder is perceived value. Following directions < communicating < persuading < negotiating < mentoring.
4. Dig for 25+ items per category without editing, then winnow to your 8–10 highest and elaborate each into skill + object + benefit.
5. Higher-level skills bring more pay, more longevity, more advancement, more authority, and more creative latitude.
6. Your boss is your best prospect for advancement — a satisfied customer beats a cold call.
7. Choose a mentor who can actually promote you, approach with questions, and convert the relationship into opportunity over months by offering value and asking for nothing.
8. Imagine bigger than the next rung; reaching and missing still makes the point about your ambition.
