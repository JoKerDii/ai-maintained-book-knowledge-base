# Chapter 2: Get Hold of Yourself

## Core Idea
Take a hard inventory of what you have done and what you can do — then translate it into the only language business universally speaks: money. Achievements expressed in dollars, converted into transferable skills, are what make a boss want to keep investing in you.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Speak the Language of Business**: the universal language of every business, everywhere, is money.
  - When to use: any time you describe yourself, your work, or your value — to a boss, colleague, customer, or yourself.
  - How: don't merely speak in the language; *think* in it, as fluent second-language speakers do. Inventory your achievements in terms of money made for the employer, money saved for the employer, and money your skills will make and save in the future.
  - Why it works: nouns and verbs beat adjectives and adverbs in all communication because they're closer to actuality. In *business* communication, nouns and verbs linked to numbers are best of all.

- **The Achievement Inventory (not a Stuff Inventory)**: convert a list of activities into a list of quantified results.
  - When to use: before any conversation about your future — raise, promotion, review, reassignment.
  - How: write freely, without organizing, censoring, or editing — just get every action item down. Then pass over the list a second time and attach a dollar value or percentage to each entry, elevating "what I did" into "what it was worth."
  - Failure mode: a list of activities says only that you're doing your job. Nothing special there.

- **Transferable Skills (portable skills)**: know-how you own and carry, as opposed to knowledge affixed to one job.
  - When to use: whenever defining what you bring to the table — the claim on your future rather than your past.
  - How: strip the company- and product-specific wrapper. "I know how to sell the Model A Widget" is a skill and a dead end; "I know how to sell" is transferable. Also strip out traits: "I am persuasive" is a trait, not a skill; "I am a persuasive salesperson" is just a job description. Neither is transferable.
  - Why it matters: job-specific skill makes your job a socket that anyone could be plugged into — and trained into more cheaply. Transferable skills make you human capital that competing employers will value.

- **The Offer of Mastery**: use past achievements as supporting evidence for a claim about future performance.
  - When to use: when making the case for investment in you, not just retention of you.
  - How: never state mastery in personal terms ("my sales record has given me a deep sense of satisfaction"). State it as benefit to the boss, in the language of business: "My sales last quarter were up XX percent, and I'm positioned to bring in XXX dollars more this quarter."

## Key Concepts
- **Human capital** — you as an asset with intrinsic, portable value, not an interchangeable plug in a socket.
- **Value proposition** — the basic offer you make your employer, stated in money.
- **Negative inventory** — your honest list of deficiencies; essential to know, never to volunteer.
- **Converting negatives** — reframing a criticism toward trajectory and remedy rather than excuse or apology.
- **"What have you done for me lately?"** — the question every employer asks mentally; answered by the achievement inventory.
- **"What will you do for me next?"** — the better question; answered by transferable skills.

## Mental Models
- **Think of your boss as a customer, and yourself as a salesperson.** A good salesperson makes a sale; a great salesperson creates a customer. Keeping your job is the single sale — the desperation play. Making your boss want to do business with you now *and* in the future is the real goal.
- **A salesperson's best prospect is a current satisfied customer, not a cold call.** That is exactly what you want your boss to be — which is why you take the long view of yourself so she takes the long view of you.
- **The past is done and the present doesn't last; the future holds all the promise.** Achievements are evidence; transferable skills are the claim on the future, and that's what you're actually selling.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confusing honesty with confession.** Be honest when asked about a weakness; never volunteer your negative inventory unprompted.
- **Making excuses, or apologizing for a deficiency.** Neither evade nor deny — convert. Highlight direction and improvement rather than underscoring the present shortfall.
- **A vague inventory.** "Nothing is more useless." Keep a short list of your most significant, most specific achievements at the top of your mind.
- **Promising your boss anything out of eagerness to please.** That's the desperation play; it wins a sale and loses a customer.
- **Describing yourself with traits or job titles.** "I am persuasive" and "I am a persuasive salesperson" both fail — the first is a trait, the second a job description.

## Worked Example

**The raw list (activity — says only that you did your job):**
> Made 15 sales calls last week to established customers · Followed up on 23 leads for new customers · Made more than 50 cold calls · Filed my September expense report · Attended XYZ Conference

**The same list, rewritten in the language of business (achievement):**
> Made 15 sales calls to established customers, *who represent $XXX in business for the company during FY20XX* · Followed up on 23 new customer leads; *3 requested quotes, representing potential sales of $XXX* · Made more than 50 cold calls *with the goal of growing this quarter's sales by X percent* · Filed my September expense report; *note that while increasing customer site visits by X percent, I reduced expenses by $XXX* · Attended XYZ Conference *and am preparing a report on a new XYZ process, which may save our department $XXX quarterly*

**Converting a negative, live:**
> **Boss:** I appreciate what you've done for us, I really do. Turnaround times in your department have come down, I know, but they're still not where they should be.
> **You:** I understand, but I think you'll agree the department is moving in the right direction. Once we've streamlined process XYZ, which I'm working on now, we will bring turnaround down to a point even better than what you've specified.

**Specificity template** — *"My most significant accomplishment this quarter is . . ."* → streamlining the problem-reporting process · increasing up-selling by 4 percent · increasing sales by 25 units · reducing order fulfillment errors by 8 percent over the last 90 days · reducing turnaround on report processing by an average of two hours.

## Key Takeaways
1. Learning about yourself isn't time taken away from the job — it's what lets you do the job better and get a better one.
2. Think in the language of business, don't just speak it: money made, money saved, money to come.
3. Nouns and verbs linked to numbers are the most persuasive units of business communication.
4. Inventory achievement, not stuff; then translate achievement into transferable skills.
5. A skill specific to one job is a good fit and a dead end — it invites your boss to imagine someone cheaper in the socket.
6. Know your deficiencies cold; volunteer them never; convert them when raised.
7. Don't make a sale — create a customer. Aim for the long-term relationship, so that staying is your option, not just theirs.
