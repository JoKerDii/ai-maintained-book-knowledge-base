# Chapter 12: Stepping Out and Stepping Up

## Core Idea
The goal of becoming indispensable isn't to stay put. The attitudes, skills, and perceptions you built are fully portable — yours to take to a new position, employer, or career. When you look beyond your current job, don't look at your feet or level with the horizon. Look up.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Filling the Prospect Pool**: measure the employer universe your current career offers. The more specialized your expertise, the smaller that universe — **but also the smaller the pool competing with you.** Geography matters too: an industry concentrated in one city (meat packing in Omaha, publishing in New York) makes slipping into a new slot easier than a Chicago-to-Osaka move.

- **Reading Industry Publications as Interactive**: don't just look for job postings.
  - Mine news and articles about specific companies and industry issues for hot firms, solid opportunities, trends, and growth areas.
  - Harvest names — article authors who are industry professionals, and people mentioned in the articles — as job contacts.
  - Watch for promotions, executive moves, retirements, and deaths; these identify possible management-level openings at named companies.
  - **Become a contributor**: write an article, submit a news item, or write a letter to the editor. Getting published or even quoted in a trade periodical gives you instant credibility and broadcasts your name across the industry.

- **The Résumé-Posting Caution**: **do not post your résumé on job sites if you are currently employed** — especially if you want to keep the job. Those sites exist for hirers as much as seekers, and someone at your company, maybe your boss, may well be surfing them. Even a job you dislike is probably better than no job at all.

- **The Three Kinds of Agency**:
  1. **Permanent employment agencies** — the prospective employer pays the fee; you pay nothing.
  2. **Headhunters** — search firms retained by an employer to fill a specific position; you pay no fee.
  3. **Permanent employment agencies you pay for** — here *you* are the client rather than the employer.
  - For most people changing companies within an industry, the first two are the most effective and certainly the least expensive. Note that most *general* agencies are fairly inefficient and rarely helpful at higher levels; look for industry-specific placement firms or specialized departments of larger executive recruiters.
  - **Don't waste time sending a résumé.** Use the online portal if offered, but also telephone and bid for a face-to-face interview.

- **Be a Surfer**: the problem may not be this particular job but the market, field, or industry that spawned it. *The lifelong typewriter repairmen who looked at the IBM PC in 1981 and retrained for an emerging technology were far better off five years later than those who stuck with what they knew.*
  - *Traditional (personal) reasons to change*: boredom; wanting more money; wanting more challenge; waking up and answering "no" to "is this what I want for the rest of my life?"; getting tired of popping antacid tablets; wanting a *less* demanding career with more time for family.
  - *The pressing modern reason*: **your career changes out from under you** — evolving technology, outsourcing, a contracting economy. The places to move within your field are dwindling.
  - *The five-step surf*: (1) ask what interests you — better, what **fascinates** you; (2) seek out people already doing it and ask three essential questions — *What do you love about your work? What do you hate about it? How did you get into it?*; (3) **start with what you want to do, not with what's "hot"** — only after deciding what you want, check whether demand allows it; (4) start from your **transferable skills**, since a good fit greatly improves your chances; (5) be proactive — **the best time to think about a new career is while you're still being paid in the old one.** If job loss forced the change, consider temporary or part-time work to sustain you while you prepare.

- **The Nine Parts of Any Career** — break a prospective career down and you'll see that even a radical change rarely requires changing all of them; typically the core of a change requires altering **only two or three**.
  1. **Where you work** — office, store, farm, etc.
  2. **Career or job goal(s)** — to teach math, repair air conditioners, sell widgets.
  3. **Assigned tasks** — for a widget sales rep: learn the line; identify and target prospects; contact them; persuade them; provide information; close; transmit the order to the warehouse; follow up on satisfaction.
  4. **Tools of the trade** — telephone, PC, email, contact-management software, samples and case, wardrobe, automobile.
  5. **Salary** — including commissions, bonuses, expense account.
  6. **Time requirement** — hours per week, after-hours availability, travel time.
  7. **Talents and/or traits required** — thinking on your feet, a "people" knack, good memory, pleasant phone voice, competitive spirit, can-do attitude.
  8. **Skills required** — persuading people to buy, organizational skills.
  9. **Knowledge required** — familiarity with the technology and industry advances; computer savvy.
  - Almost always you'll need new **skills** and **knowledge**, and possibly talents your old career didn't tap. Then ask: *Can I acquire them? Am I willing to invest the time and effort? Do they require talents or traits I may not possess?* (If you have no facility with numbers, you probably won't do well as an actuary.)

- **Estimate the Leap Gap — the Two-Stage Career Change**: your current **job title** plus your current **field** form the unit that describes your career. Changing both in a single jump "is like trying to swallow a foot-long sandwich in a single gulp." Change **either** the title or the field to take one job; gain experience; then change the other.

- **The Informational Interview — "Don't seek a career, seek information."** First address immediate subsistence needs so desperation doesn't drive you; rushing into a *job* is fine, rushing into a *career* never is.
  - *Setup*: identify a target career → identify the companies producing that product → evaluate their product → pick the one that most appeals → identify the individual people who produce it → contact them.
  - *The core discipline*: you must understand what you are **not** about. **You are not applying for a job.** If asked, don't snap at the bait — say you're not looking "at the moment" but are "very interested in exploring careers in [industry]."
  - *The five questions*: How did you get started in this line of work? What do you like most about it? What do you dislike most about it? What are your views on the present state and future prospects of the industry? **Can you recommend anyone else I might speak with?**
  - *Discipline of length*: follow the interviewee's lead — if she extends it, great; otherwise cap it around ten minutes.
  - *Always follow up with a thank-you email.* It's right — you've been given the gift of a busy person's time — and it sets your name in their memory, possibly filed for future reference. Conduct enough of these and **you will have broadcast your name to any number of potential employers before you've applied for a single job.**
  - *Format*: try for face-to-face, but a phone call, video call, or even an exchange of messages is far better than nothing.

- **The Growth-Industry Investment Model**: identifying careers in a growth industry is like investing in growth stocks. Savvy investors don't lavish sums on mature firms in mature markets — limited downside, but constrained upside too. Look for the undervalued position likely to increase dramatically.
  - *Research method*: internet searches on the target industry and its prominent firms; company websites with emphasis on press releases and annual reports; recent news stories; the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics site and especially the **Occupational Outlook Handbook**, published every two years, with long-term job growth projections for nearly three hundred occupations.
  - *Picking up the buzz*: web-search an industry and count the pages, articles, and blogs devoted to it over the most recent six-month period. Follow the search where it leads — if you land on a product, investigate it: ask a salesperson in the store, or read customer reviews online. Read the stock quotation pages for companies with a pattern of upward movement, especially technology-oriented ones or those in advanced services.
  - *The balance*: begin with what interests you and fits your transferable skills — but it does little good to pine for an industry with few jobs and dim prospects, because once inside a narrow industry your mobility is limited and you can't meet unexpected contingencies.

- **Easing Into It (four toe-dipping routes)**:
  - **Classes while employed** — if the new career needs schooling, attend some while still working. The most valuable thing you learn may be that the career you thought would be great is not right for you at all. *Better to find out in the classroom than in the office.*
  - **Freelance, volunteer, intern** — if you already have the training, take freelance assignments around your present job. If paid work is unavailable, seek volunteer assignments or a part-time internship: identify someone with power to hire volunteers, explain that you're contemplating the move and want to see firsthand how an industry leader operates, outline your present position emphasizing the transferable skills you'll contribute unpaid — and make clear you are **not** looking for a job offer, but for experience.
  - **A new place in the immediate family** — change careers without changing companies. Large organizations trying to make the best use of human capital in hard times often show remarkably little resistance. Check whether your company has programs for career changers; if not, identify leaders in the area you want to move to and seek their advice and support.
  - **A new place in the extended family** — a different company in the same industry, often via the two-stage transition. (Accountant at Acme Widgets → sales at Beta Widgets: same industry, different company, different title.) Staying in an industry where you have a track record makes the transition easier and draws less resistance from the target employer.

- **Channeling Passion — the nine criteria for a career worth entering**: adequate income; security including growth potential; a **physical** working environment you can tolerate or enjoy; an **emotional** working environment you can tolerate or enjoy (competitive and performance pressures, job hazards, daily stress); acceptable demands on your time; people you can work with (clients, coworkers, supervisors, subordinates); acceptable travel requirements (a demand or an opportunity, depending on what you want); perks and amenities; and status — how the world values or fails to value the career.

## Key Concepts
- **The leap gap** — the distance between your current title+field unit and the target one.
- **Two-stage career transition** — changing title *or* field first, the other later.
- **Informational interview** — a knowledge hunt deliberately not framed as a job hunt.
- **Vertical integration** — why book publishing, TV, film, and digital media may now be one "media industry" or even one company, making career changes possible without leaving your employer.
- **Occupational Outlook Handbook** — the BLS's biennial long-term projections for nearly 300 occupations.
- **Prospect pool** — the universe of employers your current career reaches.

## Mental Models
- **Think outside the box without walking outside of it.** Vertical integration means more of us can change careers without changing companies.
- **Surf your career the way you surf channels.** If the program doesn't interest you, the problem may be the channel, not the show.
- **A career change is a sandwich, not a gulp.** Two bites — title, then field, or the reverse.
- **Rush into a job if you must; never rush into a career.** Addressing immediate subsistence doesn't obligate you to abandon the change — it puts you in a *better* position to make it.
- **Invest your career like a growth-stock investor.** Mature markets have limited downside and constrained upside.
- **A door that closed can show you a door to something better** — one you'd never have looked for, let alone opened, if circumstances hadn't forced your hand.
- **The future, like any investment, takes time to pay off.** Do what you must to buy the time.

## Anti-patterns
- **Posting your résumé while employed.** Your boss surfs those sites too.
- **Sending a résumé to an agency or headhunter and waiting.** Call and bid for a face-to-face.
- **Choosing a career because it's hot.** Making yourself indispensable is a substantial investment of time and energy — worth it only if the organization isn't hot today and gone tomorrow.
- **Ignoring demand entirely.** The mirror error: an industry with few jobs traps you with no mobility for unexpected contingencies.
- **Taking the job bait in an informational interview.** It converts a generous conversation into an awkward rejection.
- **Letting an informational interview run long** without the interviewee extending it.
- **Skipping the thank-you email** — the thing that files your name for future reference.
- **Attempting title and field in one jump.**
- **Assuming a degree or certificate guarantees a job.** Education takes time and money and guarantees nothing; where possible, seek firms willing to finance your professional training, weighing a lower salary against future advances.

## Worked Example

**The two-stage transition, both routes.** You *are* a sales rep for a book publisher. You *want to be* an advertising copywriter for a maker of computer games — a change of both title and field.
> **Route A (title first):** find a position as an advertising copywriter inside your current publishing company. Work at it for a number of years. Then change the *field* by becoming an advertising copywriter for a computer-game maker.
> **Route B (field first):** leave the publisher (change field) to become a sales rep for a computer-game maker (same title). Later, move from Sales to Advertising (change title).
>
> Route A's first step is usually easier without leaving your current company — but your publishing experience also makes a *new* publisher in a *new* position feasible. The same optionality applies to step two.

**The informational-interview request, scripted:**
> "My name is So-and-so. I am very interested in [your industry]. I'm not looking for an immediate job, but I am thinking about a career in [your industry]. It would help me very much if I could spend a few minutes with you, getting your take on [your industry]."
>
> *And if they ask whether you're applying:* explain that although you are not looking for a job "at the moment," you are "very interested in exploring careers in [industry], and I would be grateful for a few minutes to talk to you about it."

Then run the five questions, cap it at ten minutes unless extended, and send the thank-you email.

## Key Takeaways
1. Everything you built is portable — leaving isn't only about replacing what you had, it's about looking up.
2. Specialization shrinks your prospect pool and your competition at the same time.
3. Trade publications are interactive: mine them for names and openings, and publish in them for instant credibility.
4. Never post a résumé while employed; never mail one to a headhunter instead of calling.
5. Start a career change with what fascinates you and what fits your transferable skills, not with what's hot — then check demand.
6. Break any career into nine parts; a radical change usually alters only two or three, almost always skills and knowledge.
7. Split the leap: change title *or* field first, the other later.
8. Hunt information, not a job — five questions, ten minutes, always a thank-you — and you broadcast your name before applying anywhere.
9. Dip a toe: classes while employed, freelance, volunteer, intern, or move within your own company or industry.
10. Channel passion through nine practical criteria — income, security, physical and emotional environment, time, people, travel, perks, status.
