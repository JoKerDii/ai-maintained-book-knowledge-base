# Chapter 4: Taking Aim

## Core Idea
Set new goals to remodel yourself in your *current* employer's eyes — delivering the impossible thing every boss wants: "something entirely new that's worked well in the past." Market yourself by translating skill features into emotional benefits, and where no opening exists, design the position yourself.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Five-Part Achievement Story (the homework)**: a structured narrative that surfaces skills you didn't know you had.
  - When to use: when the raw inventory of Ch 3 didn't produce enough "A material," and before any conversation where you'll need concrete evidence.
  - How — write **at least three** stories, each following this plotline exactly (unlike the free-form lists of Ch 3): (1) state your **goal** — what you set out to accomplish; (2) describe at least one **problem, conflict, or obstacle** blocking it — every good story turns on one; (3) narrate **step by step** what you did to solve it; (4) come to a definite **outcome**, including its effect and significance; (5) give an **objective evaluation**, quantified in the language of business — dollars saved, dollars made.
  - Then: compare each story against your People/Data/Things skill lists from Ch 3 and decide which transferable skills it demonstrates. This (a) lets you define and describe your skills, (b) uncovers skills you didn't know you had, and (c) identifies job and career goals.
  - Why it works: very few people ever stop to *think* about their jobs, let alone their careers. The story format forces the thinking that inventory alone doesn't.

- **The I Like / I Don't Like Fold**: measuring your current job against your own value.
  - How: fold a sheet lengthwise; head the left column "I like," the right "I don't like"; fill both. Expect items to land in **both** columns — most people have mixed feelings.
  - What it captures that Ch 3 didn't: physical environment, coworkers, tools and materials, salary, time demands, clients served, purpose of the job, tasks, required talents and training, prestige conferred or withheld.
  - Diagnostic: does this work let you use what you're best at? Does it increase your value as human capital? Or is the best part of you going to waste each day?

- **Positioning (the three dimensions of a market position)**: how your best potential customer perceives you.
  1. **Benefit** — the *emotional* reason to buy; how the product promises to make the customer feel.
  2. **Target** — your ideal customer.
  3. **Competition** — the others vying for the same customers.
  - Key move: target is largely chosen for you (your boss) and competition is a given you can neither hire nor fire. **Benefit is the one dimension entirely yours to control.**

- **Feature → Benefit Translation**: the core self-marketing operation.
  - Model: FedEx's "Relax. You sent it FedEx." The *feature* is reliable overnight delivery; the *benefit* advertised is peace of mind — a feeling.
  - How: take a transferable-skill statement (a feature) and ask what emotional state it creates in your boss. "I am good at negotiating prices that satisfy both seller and customer" (feature) → "I create satisfaction" / "I will make your life easier" (benefit).
  - Why your job is easier than an ad agency's: a deodorant brand must model many consumers' buying patterns and emotional motives. Your principal customer has **one** motive — she wants to save and make more money. Translate every feature into a bottom-line factor and you're on solid ground.

- **The 12-Point Prelaunch Checklist**: the performance platform self-marketing has to stand on. Answer each with a brutally honest yes or no; where you can't say yes, list the steps to get there and take them *before* you talk to your boss.
  1. I really know my job in depth. 2. I know and do it so well it would be very hard to replace me. 3. I am open to change; I embrace new technology and improved procedures. 4. I look for better ways to do things and bring them into my work. 5. I volunteer more than the average worker. 6. I am a team player. 7. I look for extra work that benefits the company and interests me. 8. I consistently demonstrate interest in what I do. 9. I offer constructive suggestions for improvement and innovation. 10. I am reliable. 11. I complete assigned projects on time. 12. I show up where and when I'm expected.

- **Five Reasons to Create Your Own Position**: (1) you see a genuine, perhaps pressing need for it; (2) available positions don't satisfy your needs and aspirations; (3) available positions don't use your unique transferable skills; (4) none of them is your dream job — and why shouldn't you have a shot at it?; (5) you want a position that is uniquely yours. Ownership carries substantial risk but offers creative latitude: *it is one thing to make yourself indispensable — better still to create a job that is indispensable.*

- **The Outside-Consultant Method (creating your own job)**: imagine you were hired from outside to recommend improvements to your own department, and proceed accordingly.
  1. Consider how the department could be improved by adding a position or replacing an existing one with a new one.
  2. Work out the recommendations in detail.
  3. Decide what facts and figures support them, then gather that data.
  4. Draw up scenarios demonstrating the benefits of the new position.
  5. Combine scenarios + data + your own performance record so you present as the ideal candidate.
  - Even if the company can't actually create the role, these five steps identify new projects and expanded responsibility, and market your work ethic — putting you at the head of the line when something does open. "No positive effort is wasted."

- **Let the Boss Own the Idea**: the selling technique for a new or redefined role.
  - How: don't proclaim the need for a new position. Explain your expanded role and let *him* be the one to say "so you're describing a new position." Use inclusive pronouns — **we, us, our** — never *I, me, you*. Use "what if" language: *Wouldn't it be great if . . . ? What if we had X capability in-house . . . ? If we had someone to do X, then we would be able to . . . / then we wouldn't have this problem in the future. This could be a great opportunity, if we . . .*
  - Also: connect what you want to something the boss has already said he wants, by date and detail.
  - Why: it is always easier to sell someone on his own idea. Sharing ownership costs you credit and buys you reality — "it is the reality you succeed in creating that finally matters."

## Key Concepts
- **Positioning** — how a product, service, or person is perceived by its best potential customer.
- **Feature vs. benefit** — what the product *is* vs. how it makes the customer *feel*.
- **Internal vs. external customers** — colleagues and your boss vs. actual clients; you produce for both.
- **Lateral move** — a change at roughly the same compensation made for a non-salary strategic gain.
- **Serial lateral moves** — a career killer; a habit of same-salary moves signals unreliability.
- **The status quo — comfortably fatal** — the drift that makes learning how you actually feel seem dangerous.

## Mental Models
- **Look for open doors and walk through; when a door starts closing on your future, don't shoulder it forever.** Let it close — as long as you're ready to open another.
- **What does your boss want? The impossible: something entirely new that's worked well in the past.** You are a known quantity and a proven asset — redefine yourself with new goals and you can actually deliver that impossible commodity.
- **You are a producer *and* a marketer.** Consistently produce excellence — there's no faking it — but don't depend on the work speaking for itself. The rare company that neglects marketing rarely survives.
- **The future is your strongest ally in the toughest times.** Its value isn't the *hope* things improve; it's the reason to take steps that actually improve them. And the future belongs to your boss and company too — persuade her you have one together.
- **Careers move in more than one dimension.** Up is not the only strategically viable direction. A lateral move can buy survival, intellectual satisfaction, or better growth potential; a downward move can unlock a track that offers more later.
- **Do the work for the position you want long before you get it.**

## Anti-patterns
- **Presenting a proposal as a fix-it for something your boss wrecked.** Steer clear of criticism and negativity; you must not look like you're mopping up after him.
- **Announcing a brand-new position outright.** Some bright people have a knee-jerk negative reaction to anything radically new. Ease the transition by framing it as a logical, natural extension of your current responsibilities — many managers prize continuity.
- **Claiming credit for the idea.** Natural, and costly. Your objective is to rise and grow, not to get a pat on the back.
- **Passive value-presentation** — "I offer X, Y, and Z. Take it or leave it." Never stop thinking like a marketer; always position.
- **A lateral move without a strategic reason.** Weigh future gains against current risks; and know that a boss may read a move for something intangible as a sign you don't like or respect her.
- **Building outside your current department first.** The most feasible route to a new position runs straight through the department where you have firsthand experience and a quantifiable track record. Build on what you have.

## Worked Example

**A five-part achievement story (customer service rep), compressed:**
> *Goal* — deliver exceptional service to a client in urgent need. *Problem* — ABC Widgets called in a total panic: they'd improperly set password coding and could execute no transactions for their customers; a phone-based software roadblock also raises real security-breach risk. *Solution, step by step* — I refused to let their panic infect my judgment; assured them we'd resolve it together and that I'd stay on the case; took out the security manual checklist and got all confirmations that I was dealing only with authorized people before touching the password problem; when they got defensive, I apologized for having to check for a breach, explained why, reviewed the value they received from it, assured them I didn't think anyone was a hacker, and complimented their own internal security — noting that combined with ours it made a tough shield that was a genuine selling advantage for them. *Outcome* — got around the roadblock, got ABC running, preserved full security both sides; their general manager called to thank me personally. *Evaluation* — I had the online tools to bypass protocol and felt real pressure to, and didn't, because I could communicate the protocol's strategic value.
>
> **Four lessons extracted:** (1) With a frustrated customer, express empathy and even apologize for the circumstance — "I'm sorry this has happened" is not accepting responsibility for a problem you didn't cause, but it lowers frustration a long way. (2) Nothing aggravates a customer more than "I'm following the rules / company policy / just doing what I'm told." Follow the rules *and* explain the value they represent. (3) Be firm, but assure the customer you are not accusing anyone of wrongdoing. (4) Turn the negative into a positive — the protocol was a value-add and competitive edge for ABC's own customers.

**Feature converted to benefit, spoken aloud:**
> "I am good at negotiating prices that satisfy both the seller and the customer. What I'm saying is that I don't just make the sale, I make every sale a really good sale, a sale that creates satisfaction, which means repeat business over the long term. You can rely on me to add to the bottom line this quarter, as well as next quarter and, in fact, quarter after quarter."

**Handing the boss ownership:**
> "This reminds me of what you said back in February, about more closely coordinating Service with Sales so that we don't miss out on opportunities to sell accessories. I've been reaching out to Service, as you know, but we really need to develop a dedicated coordinator position to achieve the consistency of connection that we had talked about early in the year."

**Opening line for the meeting:** "I'd like to speak with you about how I can make a bigger, more valuable contribution to our department. I need your take on some ideas I've been working out."

## Key Takeaways
1. Write at least three goal → obstacle → step-by-step solution → outcome → quantified evaluation stories; map each to your Ch 3 skill lists.
2. The I like / I don't like fold captures everything the skills inventory misses — environment, people, time, prestige — and items legitimately appear in both columns.
3. Of Benefit / Target / Competition, benefit is the only dimension you fully control, so that's where self-marketing happens.
4. Translate every skill feature into an emotional benefit, then into a bottom-line factor — your boss has one motive: save and make money.
5. Marketing works wonders, not miracles; pass the 12-point prelaunch checklist honestly before you sell.
6. If no position fits, design one using the outside-consultant method — and even a rejected proposal markets your work ethic.
7. Sell the new role with inclusive pronouns and "what if" language, and let the boss claim the idea. The reality you create matters more than credit for the proposal.
8. Consider lateral and even downward moves for strong strategic reasons; avoid serial lateral moves, which read as unreliability.
