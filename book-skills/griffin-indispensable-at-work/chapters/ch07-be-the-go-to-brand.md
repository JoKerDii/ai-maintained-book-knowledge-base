# Chapter 7: Be the "Go-To" Brand

## Core Idea
Selling is the business of all business and the job of everyone who works — what you sell is your value. Branding is what sets your merchandise apart once competition appears down the street. This chapter is twelve tactics for branding yourself indispensable.

## Frameworks Introduced — The Twelve Branding Tactics

**1. Be the Eternally Brand-New Brand.** Marketing's most powerful word isn't *free* — it's **new**. Because you're a living, thinking human rather than a day-old loaf of bread, you can brand your experienced self as new nearly every day.
- *Offer the latest*: come up with fresh approaches to routine issues; keep up with developments in the field; be the office's chief source of news — read company and industry publications, cruise the web, share immediately.
- *Deliver well-seasoned newness* (for veterans): age discrimination is illegal but older workers still fight the perception of being "grandfathered in" or short-timers. Confound the cliché by advocating innovation while still trading on what experience bought you — genuine know-how, the wisdom of having tried and evaluated many approaches, seasoned judgment, and a deep network inside the company, industry, and customer base. Revive bonds with longtime advocates; take younger coworkers to lunch, be seen with them, treat them as equals.
- *Get hired every day*: treat each day as an employment interview. Recall what got you hired — excitement, passion, a promise to deliver a great product — and relive those feelings daily.

**2. Be the Problem-Solver Brand.** *Problem solver* is the one position that can never be filled redundantly; no organization has too many, and virtually every one wants more. When you encounter a problem, resist passing it along — take initiative. Even failing to fully resolve it, the effort is appreciated. Seek chronic recurrent problems and clogged routines, build solutions, promote them to your boss, and establish ongoing credentials.

**3. Be Low-Maintenance.** The single most important corollary to #2. Most employees are fired not for inability, missing skills, or falling below productivity minimums, but for being **high-maintenance** — requiring constant supervision, or complaining about cubicle size and location, air-conditioning, slow computers, and holding forth endlessly on irrelevant matters. The language of business is money, but most firing decisions are formulated subjectively. No boss will say it aloud, but she is almost certainly thinking: *I fired him because I just don't like him.*

**4. Be the Clutch Brand.** Time, more than money, is business's most chronic and acute shortage — which is why *deadline* sounds so dire: **work that is late is value that is lost.** Be the player who brings it in on time, on budget, and with success. Every missed deadline gives bosses feelings they don't want to have, and sooner or later they think about shedding the source.
- If you miss one: give an **explanation, not an excuse** — including a promise it won't repeat *and a plan* for how you'll prevent it. Then move heaven and earth to keep it: work late, call in favors. "As hard as you may be on yourself, your bosses can always be harder."

**5. Be the Customer-Satisfaction Brand.** Work as if your professional life depended on every customer — it does. **External customers** are clients and consumers; **internal customers** are the subordinates, colleagues, and bosses you work with daily, and they expect value all of the time, not some of it. Growing your internal customer base raises visibility and extends your network.
- *Follow-up*: respond to calls and emails promptly and fully; take ownership — you cannot let go of a question without answering it or a problem without solving it, or finding someone who can. **If customers want you, you are by definition indispensable.**
- *Ask questions*: questions are not a sign of ignorance, they're a mark of intelligence. Don't guess at needs you can simply ask about, and when you lack an answer, route to someone who has it.
- *Extra-mile service*: physical absence is no excuse in the age of email and smartphones. Arrange a designated pinch hitter for extended absences — and if the customer still calls you, don't just redirect, make the connection yourself, ideally by conference call.
- *Smile*: deliver service as if serving were a privilege rather than a burden.

**6. Be the Feel-Good Brand.** The squeaky wheel does *not* get the grease at work. Whining is egocentric, and your myriad felt injustices will never become a popular cause.
- *Bottle it up*: chuck the daytime-TV advice about never bottling feelings. Try to avoid a single negative observation — not about your boss, not the fluorescent hum, not a solid week of rain. Be busy and look busy.
- *The war face*: Patton's famous scowl didn't come naturally — he called it his "war face" and practiced it in the mirror. Your war face is a smile. Put the work mask on before leaving home and wear it religiously. Once people start asking "What's wrong?", you might as well head for the exit.
- *Never walk through the open door to complain*, however genial and sincere the invitation.
- *There is no refuge in the quality of your work*: HR research consistently shows most people would rather work with a marginally incompetent but always friendly colleague than a super-capable whiner. Negativity is contagious; so is a can-do presence.

**7. Be the Popular Brand.** Isolation is job- and career-killing: if you're not perceived as part of the community, no one will regret showing you the door. Don't make refusing the after-work beer your knee-jerk response.
- *Steer clear of egomania*: a solid ego commands respect; an egomaniac — whose only topic is himself and only tune his own horn — signals he has no room for the company your welfare depends on. The surest defense: make your conversation far more questions than declarative statements.
- *Give, don't take, credit*: taking credit for others' ideas earns hatred, knives in your back, and — being fundamentally dishonest — eventually percolates up to management as grounds for dismissal. Be the cheerleader instead.
- *Network internally*, not only outside; more connections means more visibility, and visibility keeps you off the expendable list. Learn informally from the most experienced and respected people; formal mentorships often grow out of that.
- *Build respect, not just affection*: respected people get sought out for advice and mentoring, which cements their place and makes everyone, management included, want them to stay.

**8. Be the Loyal and Dedicated Brand.** Never broadcast that you work first and last for a paycheck, even if you do. Four moves: (1) work as long as the job takes, even after others leave — be **task oriented, not time defined**; (2) offer help, and lots of it; (3) when help is accepted, take ownership — the problem is yours until it's solved; (4) take on more work, volunteer.

**9. Be the High-Value Brand.** Savvy consumers shop for value, not price; savvy employers view human capital as return on investment. Your employer is trading the price of a paycheck for the value you provide and will not long tolerate an imbalance unfavorable to her.
- Show you're hungry — crave more work, volunteer, invent genuinely productive work when your projects are done.
- Focus on what you can do well — **superb execution is more important than sheer volume**; doing a lot of work badly is disastrous.
- Increase productivity measurably: longer hours, but also smarter and more efficient ones.
- Show up on time — in fact ten or fifteen minutes early.
- Brand yourself a workhorse; the newer you are, the more this matters.
- Immerse yourself in the business: suppliers, customers, competition — and become the source everyone consults. *("We can't lose Joe. He's the guy who knows everything.")*
- Keep pad and paper handy; take notes when the boss or senior people speak.

**10. Be the High-Sales Brand.** Promote your company outside — conventions, cocktail parties, Little League games, golf. Promote your department inside. Promote yourself always: document achievements and pass them up; ask genuinely satisfied clients to tell your boss so; let the numbers do your boasting; share measurable results with the people who can advance and reward you.

**11. Be the Bottom-Line Brand.** In soft economies nickels and dimes matter and management counts them. Employers want value for the value they invest, measured both in value generated and value saved.
- *Look for deals*: research new technology; propose a low-cost internship partnership with a community college or high school; compare Vendor A against Vendor B on printer ink; streamline a routine procedure.
- *Mine new revenue*: the real gold is revenue streams needing little or no investment — e.g., revamping routine service contacts as sales opportunities.
- *Do more with less*: practice creative austerity. "Guerilla marketing" — cheap or free channels to reach customers — stretches the ad budget; propose social media and other free publicity as leverage on existing paid programs.

**12. Be the All-Purpose Brand.** Compose a menu of everything you bring; identify which items are uniquely yours. Those specialties are the core of your brand — play them up, build on them, grow out from them.
- *Get uncomfortable*: push past your job description. Doing more may save your employer a hire. Don't become a jack-of-all-trades and master of none, but every team needs a **utility player**, and layoffs have magnified their value.
- *Learn*: expand expertise, multiply qualifications, make your job as big as it can be — and make sure your bosses recognize how big it's become. Investigate company learning programs and tuition assistance.
- *Demonstrate flexibility*: **your commitment should be to your firm, not to the position you happen to occupy.** Reinvent yourself using your transferable skills — an administrator boosts tech skills; a techie broadens into administration.

## Defending Your Brands
- **Pull in your horns.** Least offensive is always most desirable. Keep political and religious opinions to yourself, appear politically neutral, and don't ally with any faction. *If you connect too closely with your boss, he will drag you down if he gets fired.* Cooperate and collaborate, but never forget your deepest loyalty is first and foremost to yourself.
- **Leave gossip to the tabloids.** Idle chatter makes you look like a goof-off; refuse to be either the source or the vessel. The tales you tell have an ugly habit of coming back to bite.
- **Emails are strictly business.** Company emails are company property. You don't control who reads them or how they're used. Never send personal email on company equipment, and write nothing you wouldn't want your boss to read — deleting doesn't obliterate, since most enterprise networks keep a master file. Your words are deathless and available to management.
- **Office romance.** Most people do meet partners at work, so total abstinence is unrealistic — but: ask whether the attraction is mutual affection or perceived career gain; if you're senior, ask whether they'd say yes if you weren't in a position of power; learn your company's policy (most midsize-and-larger firms explicitly forbid harassment, but only ~6% have written fraternization policies, of which almost none ban romance between single consenting adults, though about a third define public displays of affection as inappropriate); be discreet regardless; know that after more than a couple of dates the entire office *will* find out, so agree in advance whom you'll tell and what you'll say. Anything other than two fully consenting adults isn't romance — it's sexual harassment, a career killer and grounds for civil and possibly criminal action.
- **Take a salary cut gracefully.** Bitterness only makes your boss feel bad or angry. Say instead: "I know it's all for the good of the company. I'll do with less for now." But don't swallow your own smiling reply — it's much easier to apply for a job from a position of employment. **Take less now, but start looking for more.**

## Key Concepts
- **Brand** — a set of information, values, and expectations attached to a product that gives consumers confidence and loyalty.
- **High-maintenance employee** — the real reason most people get fired; a problem *creator*.
- **Clutch player** — the one who reliably makes or beats hard deadlines; by definition the indispensable one.
- **Utility player** — someone who goes beyond the expected job description; more valuable in downsizing eras.
- **Creative austerity / guerilla marketing** — doing more with less; cheap or free channels to reach customers.
- **Task oriented, not time defined** — measuring your day by the job done, not the clock.
- **War face / work mask** — a deliberately practiced expression worn as professional discipline.

## Mental Models
- **Everyone is in the selling business; your merchandise is your value.** Whichever of the 750 positions across 276 industries you hold, your job comes down to selling.
- **You are the ten-year-old with the only lemonade stand — until competition appears down the street.** Then you'd better set your merchandise apart. That's branding.
- **Features vs. benefits, applied to reliability.** Features are your skills; benefits are how applying them makes your boss feel. Being the clutch player manufactures the feeling bosses want to keep having.
- **The customer is not always right, but never make him feel stupid.** Guide and advise — knowingly selling the wrong thing creates no satisfaction — but resist demonstrating superior knowledge. Look for ways to say "you're right" or "you're right, but," so he hangs up remembering that he asked for something and you found a way to give it to him.
- **If customers — internal or external — consistently come to you, rely on you, and seek you out, you are indispensable by definition.**

## Anti-patterns
- **Passing the problem along.** Every handoff of a solvable problem forfeits a claim on the problem-solver brand.
- **Being a prima donna even while performing well.** Top performers still get branded as problem sources.
- **Excuses for missed deadlines.** An explanation carries a promise and a plan; an excuse carries neither.
- **Correcting a customer by contradicting his stated want.** ❌ "You may want it, but you can't have it. You'll blow out the panel."
- **Whining anywhere, including through the open-door policy.** Competence is not a refuge from it.
- **Egomania and credit-taking.** Both cost you the community whose regard keeps you employed.
- **Allying with a faction, or too closely with your boss.** His fall becomes your fall.
- **Personal email on company equipment.**
- **Piling the plate high and executing badly.** Volume without quality is disastrous.

## Worked Example

**Serving a customer who's wrong, without making him wrong:**
> **Customer:** What I want is the 250-watt model. I need the power.
> ❌ **You:** Well, you may want it, but you can't have it. You'll blow out the panel. I'll sell you the 150. That's what you need.
> ✔ **You:** Bill, the panel you have is rated at 150 and would be overloaded by the 250-watt model. If you really do need the extra power — and I can understand why you would want it — then we will have to upgrade your panel. I can give you a price on this, if you like.

The bad version targets his error and ignores what he actually said he wanted: *the power*. The good version names the constraint, validates the want, and offers a route to it.

**Routing a question you can't answer, without abandoning the customer:**
> "Tom, I'm not familiar with that version of the software upgrade, but I know just the person to ask about it. May I put you on hold, so that I can get Marie Williams on the line? She will be able to help us out."

**Covering an absence without a cold handoff:**
> "Christine, I'm out of the office, but Sam Johnson is on top of your business with us and will take care of you. May I place you on hold for a moment while I get him on the line?"

**Turning a service call into revenue (bottom-line brand):**
> "Mr. Smith, you might consider investing in a new widget module, which has a duty cycle of 10,000 hours instead of the 5,000-hour model you're using now. I know most customers think of this as heavy duty, so they settle for the 5,000-hour model, which is certainly less expensive, but when you factor in the cost of the service call and the downtime, you may decide the extra up-front cash is well worth it."

## Key Takeaways
1. *New* beats *free* as a branding word — and unlike merchandise, you can be new again every day.
2. Problem solver is the one role that can never be redundant; problem *creator* is the one that gets fired.
3. Most firings are subjective and come down to high maintenance, not incompetence.
4. Work that is late is value that is lost; if you miss a deadline, give an explanation with a plan, never an excuse.
5. Internal customers are as real as external ones, and expanding your internal base buys visibility and network.
6. Wear the smile as discipline — negativity is contagious, and competence is no refuge from a whiner's reputation.
7. Give credit generously; taking it earns enemies and eventually reaches management.
8. Commit to the firm, not to the position you occupy, and keep a utility player's flexibility.
9. Defend the brand: neutrality, no gossip, business-only email, discretion, and a graceful "yes" to a pay cut — while quietly looking for more.
