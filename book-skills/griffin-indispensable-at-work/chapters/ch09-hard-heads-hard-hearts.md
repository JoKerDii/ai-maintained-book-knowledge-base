# Chapter 9: Hard Heads, Hard Hearts, and Hard Asses

## Core Idea
Some workplace conflict isn't created by problem issues but by problem *people*. Most fall into five types, each calling for a different response. Your goal is never to change the other person — it's to create the conditions that let you work productively with him, which earns you a coveted place as the in-house snake charmer.

## Frameworks Introduced — The Five Difficult Types

**1. The Bully.** Range runs from blustering, bellowing, berating hard cases to subtler operators who intimidate without abrasive language. In all cases the objective is to make you feel inadequate and inferior.
- *Recognizing the subtle version*: you present a project expecting debate on the merits, and the bully opens with "Are you **serious** about this? This is a joke, right?" Two things are happening: (a) **poisoning the well** — the mock question makes it hard for anyone to support your project without revealing themselves as foolish, rendering the proposal toxic before anyone evaluates it; (b) intimidating you into withdrawing it, prompting you to pull in your head, arms, and legs like a turtle.
- *The key distinction*: being challenged and made to defend a project in detail is fair and part of your job. A verbal assault on **you** that bypasses the issue is not, and shouldn't stand unchallenged.
- *Why it works on you*: the bully is armed with nothing but empty words and is up against the facts you carry. And **the role of bully can never be a solo act** — a bully cannot be a bully without a victim. Refuse the role and he's just someone making an unpleasant noise.
- *Six tactics*: (1) avoid dealing with or talking to the bully as much as possible — there's no rule you must confront her on every occasion; minimize all business with her. (2) Use email rather than phone or face-to-face. (3) When the show starts, step aside silently — maintain eye contact, don't interrupt; at the first opening, intrude with facts and ignore the tirade. **Don't** say "calm down" or "there's no need for that kind of language." (4) Don't argue, shout, or threaten — and don't back down. Just assert and reassert. (5) Whatever you feel, show no fear; show as little emotion as possible — bland, dead calm. (6) Let her vent, then repeat the facts.
- *The strategy in one line*: **the bully wants a quick decisive emotional skirmish; deliver a slow war of attrition.** Fail to stoke the emotional furnace and the fire dies.

**2. The Passive Aggressor.** Passively obstructionist resistance: procrastinating, sulking, claiming helplessness. May come across as easygoing, even shy or meek, but they are keen saboteurs. The single word is **unreliable** — and you can rely on that unreliability. Assign the task, secure the promise, return at the deadline, and it is undone. Confronted, she says "Don't worry. I'm on it." It still won't get done.
- *Handling*: if you supervise, avoid assigning mission-critical tasks; if possible avoid teaming up. If you have no choice, resign yourself to monitoring and micromanaging.
  - Assigning: draw up a timetable for **every stage** and specify all requirements, emphasizing outcome criteria.
  - Teamed: take charge early; put everything in writing, including the division of tasks and the timetable.
  - Keep tabs — obtain **facts and figures, not assurances and reassurances**.
  - Send tickler emails reminding of every deadline and requirement.
  - Repeat instructions, objectives, and goals as often as necessary. You will be exasperated; suck it up and express no frustration.
- *Principle*: address tangible behavior and its outcomes; control them with schedules, ongoing monitoring, and repetition.

**3. The Chronic Complainer.** Distinguish two kinds: productive complainers who get on your nerves but get the job done — count yourself lucky and let them whine — and those who channel all productive energy into nonproductive whining, using it to dodge work.
- *The two standard complaint shapes*: (1) "I can't do such-and-such because it (a) won't work or (b) can't be done." (2) "I don't have the (a) time, (b) equipment, (c) personnel, or (d) training to do such-and-such."
- *The seven-step protocol*:
  1. Determine whether the complaint is based on reality — you may have a gut feeling you're dealing with a work-dodger, but evaluate the merit first. Maybe the assignment really is impossible; maybe the department really does lack the tools.
  2. Once you determine it's evasion, **cease all discussion of the merits**.
  3. Express no sympathy for the complainer or the complaints.
  4. Explain and emphasize the importance of doing the assignment or solving the problem.
  5. Say that it is **necessary** to do or solve it.
  6. Repeat step 5.
  7. The hard one: **give him permission to fail.** Tell him to tackle it while assuring him you'll evaluate the results and offer help; explain that you understand the outcome may not be perfect and you'll work with him to make it right. Yes, you're agreeing to take on extra work — it may be your only hope of getting usable work out of him.

**4. The Schemer.** Uses gossip and rumor with genuine malicious intent to damage you and advance themselves; the skilled ones attack with total stealth. Even the indispensable aren't immune, so prevention beats cure.
- *Prevention (five measures)*: cultivate good corporate karma — Machiavelli said it's better to be feared than loved, but in the modern workplace **best of all is to be liked, by everyone**; make that your mission and practice empathy. Engage coworkers on a fully human level — they're people, not cogs; talk about their lives, ambitions, interests, dreams, and spend more time listening than talking. Assemble a coterie of allies by networking internally and externally — and the time to do this is **all** of the time, not once you're under attack. Never engage in malicious gossip yourself. Keep your chin up and your nose down: stay busy, look busy, perform with excellence, make yourself unmistakably valuable through what you produce.
- *If you find a shiv in your back*: pull it out fast. No law says you must confront the schemer; focus on damage control and repair — respond to false rumors with the truth, because facts, issues, and outcomes speak louder than secondhand information and opinion.
- *The boss judgment call*: going to your boss can help if you can present it as one team member to another, framing it as a problem that hurts the team. But the downside is real — even if she intervenes against your antagonist, you may look incapable of handling it yourself. Fair or not, you'll be tagged as part of a problem, and you want to be identified solidly and exclusively as **part of the solution**.
- *Confronting directly (six steps)*: (1) ask for a meeting, ideally outside the office over coffee or lunch — the one venue to avoid is an impromptu hallway confrontation; make it relaxed but planned. (2) Broach the *issues*, not the person. (3) Leave the "gotcha!" attitude in your desk drawer; let the schemer struggle with the issues. (4) If he folds, ask for his cooperation in stopping the loose talk. (5) If she does have real issues, discuss them without anger — maybe there's something of value in what she says. (6) Reshape future behavior and end looking forward. **Do not tell the schemer her rumor-mongering made you feel bad — that hands her a victory.**

**5. The Poacher.** Credit grabbing and idea stealing — no scheme is more common. The poacher listens to your casual discussion of an idea, may even pick your brain in a seemingly collegial way, then presents it or a close cousin as his own.
- *The bind*: teamwork and collaboration are the hallmarks of modern business, so you have to share. Intellectual ownership and intellectual sharing are two sides of the same coin — the coin that buys job security and advancement. So share, but protect.
- *Three protections*: (1) commit the idea to writing **before** discussing it with anyone; date it; store an original on a hard drive and on backup, including a portable drive you fully control if security policy permits. (2) Distribute the dated idea marked as a draft you're seeking input on, with a deadline for comments and a date for the final draft. (3) Choose collaborative partners carefully — don't broadcast to all and sundry; every recipient should know the names of the other recipients, and all should be cautioned not to share further.
- *If it happens anyway*: the poacher often presents your idea in a group meeting, in your presence, betting you'll say nothing. See to it that he loses the bet — and if you're the bystander, it's in your best interest to make sure credit goes where it's due.

## Key Concepts
- **Poisoning the well** — the rhetorical move of making an idea toxic before anyone evaluates it.
- **In-house snake charmer** — the reputation you earn by consistently creating satisfaction in difficult people.
- **Reliable unreliability** — the one thing you can count on from a passive aggressor.
- **Permission to fail** — the concession that unblocks an evasive complainer, at the cost of extra work for you.
- **Corporate karma** — the prevention layer against schemers (Ch 5).
- **Damage control and repair** — the default response to a stealth attack, in preference to confrontation.

## Mental Models
- **Your goal is not to change the other person.** It's to create the conditions that let you work productively with him.
- **A bully cannot be a bully without a victim.** The role is not a solo act; declining it ends the performance.
- **Don't move to the field the bully chose.** Attacking you rather than your idea is an attempt to relocate the battle. An illegitimate question only *seems* to demand an answer — blow past it with facts, which render the personal remarks exactly what they are: not right, not wrong, just irrelevant.
- **If a bully had real substance to back himself, he wouldn't resort to bullying.**
- **Better to be liked than feared.** Machiavelli's calculus doesn't survive the modern workplace.
- **Feelings come and go; results are what everybody in the organization is looking for.** Formulate a strategy and decide what results you want, rather than grimly anticipating an onslaught of bad feelings.

## Anti-patterns
- **Asking a bully to calm down, or admonishing his language.** Both engage the emotion and stoke the furnace.
- **Arguing, shouting, or threatening** — and equally, backing down. Assert and reassert instead.
- **Accepting a passive aggressor's assurances** in place of facts and figures, or letting your exasperation show.
- **Debating the merits with an evasive complainer** once you've established the complaint is evasion.
- **Waiting until you're under attack to build allies.**
- **Engaging in malicious gossip yourself.**
- **An impromptu hallway confrontation with a schemer** — or a "gotcha!" posture in a planned one.
- **Telling the schemer she hurt your feelings.** That's the victory she was after.
- **Discussing an idea before you've written and dated it.**
- **Saying nothing when your idea is presented as someone else's** — which is precisely the bet the poacher is placing.

## Worked Example

**Refusing the bully's field:**
> **Bully:** Are you *serious* about this?
> **You:** The approach I'm proposing will save us XX percent in overhead costs.

The question isn't answered because it's illegitimate. Persuasive facts leave the personal remark stranded as irrelevant.

**Assert and reassert:**
> **You:** Ted, I need your sales report this afternoon.
> **Bully:** Listen, I don't have time for that paperwork right now. I mean, how can you expect me to drop everything for busywork?
> **You:** I understand, but I still need that report this afternoon.

**Opening the schemer conversation on issues, not the person:**
> "I've been hearing a buzz to the effect that you have identified some problems with the XYZ project I'm working on. I'm at a loss as to what these could be. Can you share your insight? Help me out?"
> *If he folds* — "I don't know what you've heard, but I haven't told anybody about any problems" — take it at face value and recruit him: "I'm sure glad to hear that. Do you have any idea who's spreading these rumors? I'd like to put a stop to them. I hope you'll help."
> *Closing, forward-looking*: "Thanks for talking to me, Marilyn. In the future, I hope you'll feel that you can come straight to me if you have a suggestion or a problem with something I've done."

**Reclaiming a poached idea, in the meeting, in front of everyone:**
> **You:** Ben, isn't this the concept I presented to you on Monday? I sent it to George at that time, too.
> *If he denies it, persist politely:* "It *isn't* my concept? But how is what you're presenting different from what we discussed on Monday?"
>
> And if you are the bystander George: "Ben, that sounds like the idea you, Sarah, and I talked about last week. It's Sarah's idea, isn't it?"

## Key Takeaways
1. Five types cover most difficult people: bully, passive aggressor, chronic complainer, schemer, poacher — each with its own protocol.
2. Against a bully: ignore the illegitimate question, deploy facts, show no emotion, never back down, and turn an emotional skirmish into a war of attrition.
3. Against a passive aggressor: stage-by-stage timetables, everything in writing, tickler emails, facts not assurances, and no visible frustration.
4. Against a chronic complainer: verify the complaint first, then stop debating merits, state necessity twice, and grant permission to fail.
5. Against a schemer: prevent with karma, human engagement, and standing allies; respond with truth and damage control; weigh carefully before involving your boss.
6. Against a poacher: date your idea in writing before discussing it, control the distribution list, and speak up immediately when it's presented as someone else's.
7. Difficult people are a fact of business life where livelihoods are at stake and resources are limited. Play for results, not for feelings.
