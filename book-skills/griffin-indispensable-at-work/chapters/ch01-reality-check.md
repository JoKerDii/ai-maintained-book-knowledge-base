# Chapter 1: Reality Check

## Core Idea
Before you can argue for your value, you must know—factually—where your company, your industry, and your own job actually stand. Bad information is the enemy; the conversation with your boss is a job interview, so never walk into it with only questions.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Seven Signs Your Company Is in Trouble**: read the handwriting on the wall before management tells you.
  - When to use: quarterly, or any time the mood shifts. Don't wait for an announcement.
  - How — watch for: (1) star players leaving while back-benchers stay; (2) the company's strategy stops making sense to you (viable strategies are explainable without weasel words); (3) the company lags the rest of the industry; (4) missing target numbers becomes routine, not occasional; (5) layoffs repeat until they're normal; (6) cash consistently flows out faster than in; (7) management stops communicating meaningfully — jargon, doublespeak, technobabble. "Words are supposed to reveal, not conceal."
  - Why it works: two or three top performers leaving is "like the first rocks in a landslide" — good people want to work alongside good people, so their exit triggers more exits while the less able stay and hire other also-rans.

- **The Six Signs the Bull's-Eye Is on Your Back**: distinguishing "the ship is sinking" from "you're being thrown overboard."
  - When to use: when the company looks fine but your position feels off.
  - How — watch for: (1) you've lost your boss or mentor, and the replacement arrived with an entourage; (2) you lose the space race (office → cube, or your cube moves to the basement); (3) your boss starts a paper trail — things once said in a friendly chat are now put in writing for HR; (4) you're out of the loop (memos fly past, meetings held without you); (5) you're given the short end — scut work, or missions doomed to fail; (6) your company has been merged or sold, creating "redundancies."

- **The Three-Front Research Protocol**: gather answers, not questions, before any career conversation.
  - When to use: before asking your boss "How am I doing?" or requesting a raise, promotion, or reassignment.
  - How — Front 1 (**your company**): read the memos and newsletters you never read; re-read your own job description and recent performance evaluations; read the website's About Us, press releases, "From the CEO," and Investor Relations/Annual Report; web-search the company name; access whatever sales figures you're legitimately authorized to see (never break into anything); track the stock's last 3–6 months. Front 2 (**your industry and marketplace**): search industry keywords, scan trade publications for trends, search the names of your boss and his bosses for promotions/moves/retirements, read competitors' ads, analyze whether your market is growing, contracting, or stable. Front 3 (**yourself**): Chapters 2–4.

## Key Concepts
- **Reading the writing on the wall** — interpreting structural signals about company health before management discloses them.
- **The freeze-out** — being excluded from memos and meetings as a precursor to termination.
- **The paper trail** — a boss documenting in writing what used to be spoken, to satisfy HR before firing you.
- **Redundancy** — the merger/acquisition euphemism that reliably precedes layoffs.
- **Lifeboat building** — job-searching while still employed, before you need it.

## Mental Models
- **Think of the "How am I doing?" talk as a job interview.** You've had one before — you prepared for it. Prepare for this one identically. Interviewers appreciate your questions but what they're really looking for is answers.
- **Use "always be looking for a job" as a permanent posture, not a panic response.** It builds a lifeboat before the ship sinks, may find you something better than what you have, and gives you a live read on the employment market in your field.
- **Treat an industry problem as your personal opportunity.** New government regulations burdening the industry? Offer the willingness and expertise to help your firm comply. The problem for them becomes the reason you're indispensable.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming management is just too clever for you.** If you can't understand — or can't explain to someone else — your company's strategy, the likeliest explanation is that the strategy is bad, not that you are slow.
- **Talking to your boss cold.** Walking in with nothing but questions makes you a problem to be solved rather than a solver of problems.
- **Accessing information you're not authorized to see.** Gather what's legitimately available. Don't rifle confidential files.
- **Confining research to your own firm.** Your company doesn't operate in a vacuum; a healthy company in a dying market is still a risk.

## Worked Example
You notice two of the best engineers left last quarter, the CEO's memos have gone vague, and you're suddenly not on the Thursday planning invite. Rather than asking your boss "Am I okay?", you spend a week preparing: you re-read your job description and last two evaluations, read the last six press releases and the "From the CEO" page (finding "Acme to Scale Back Widget Division"), web-search the industry and learn that new compliance rules land next year, and note that no one in your department has compliance experience. You now walk in with a proposal — you'll take on compliance readiness for the division — instead of a question about your security. The research changed the meeting from a plea into a pitch.

## Key Takeaways
1. You cannot know where to go, much less how to get there, until you determine realistically where you and your firm stand.
2. Seven company-level signs and six personal signs give you warning long before an announcement does.
3. Prepare for a conversation with your boss exactly as you prepared for the job interview that got you hired — with answers.
4. Always be looking for a job, even when secure; it builds a lifeboat, surfaces better options, and reads the market for you.
5. Whatever else your boss wants from you, what she most wants is for you to solve the problems she has, not create fresh ones.
6. Industry problems are candidate opportunities: the thing that threatens the firm can be the thing that makes you valuable.
