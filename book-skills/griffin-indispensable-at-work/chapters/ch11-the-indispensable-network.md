# Chapter 11: The Indispensable Network

## Core Idea
Most people got their job through networking and then stopped networking — a career-stunting mistake. Maintaining and expanding contacts *after* you're hired keeps you current, gives you people to call, sets you up as a source of authoritative counsel, and carries the corporate banner farther than anyone else's.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Networking Audit**: ask half a dozen coworkers how they got their jobs and at least four will tell a networking story — but ask whether they're *still* active networkers and no more than two will say yes. When something is proven to work, common sense says keep doing it.

- **Be a Friend (four practices)** — effective networking begins with nothing more than behaving like one; it needn't be sleazy self-promotion or a robotic business-card ritual (though *do* exchange cards).
  1. Whenever you communicate with a contact — client, colleague, vendor, whoever — ask how they're doing and show genuine interest in their life and concerns. It may feel awkward or even fake at first; get accustomed to it and you'll be surprised how genuinely interested you actually are.
  2. **Stay in touch.** A big part of networking is just letting people know what you're up to. Consider circulating an occasional personal "newsletter" — including what you have to offer *them*.
  3. **Build a database of important days** — key contacts' birthdays and anniversaries. Send cards, celebratory emails, or make a friendly call. Season's greetings are always welcome.
  4. **Maintain as well as expand.** Don't let growing the network cause you to neglect who's already in it.
  - The payoff: invest time and interest in people and they'll very likely return the investment with interest.

- **Getting Into the Zone (before you work a room)**: mingling productively requires energy, so first jettison the thoughts that sap it — that you don't know enough to talk intelligently with higher-ups, that impressing a major industry figure is futile because hundreds are trying, that you're just not a "people person." Say them, set them aside, forget them. Then latch onto the positives:
  - Don't worry about talking about yourself — get others to talk about **themselves**, which is what most people like to do anyway. Ask questions, starting with the obvious: "What do you do at ABC Company?"
  - Showing interest in who someone is makes them feel good; interest is flattering, and everyone relishes flattery. Make a person feel good and he reciprocates with sincere interest in you.

- **Rethinking "a roomful of strangers"**: at any professional meeting you'll likely run into people you know at least slightly, even if only from the last meeting — and that slender relationship is enough to open a conversation. Even a genuine first meeting isn't a *total* stranger: same industry means shared interests, you're both probably trying to get ahead, and at minimum you share one mission — **getting through an awkward meeting with a stranger.**

- **Breaking the Ice (two moves)**:
  1. **Take the initiative.** Approach, introduce yourself, and share information aimed at revealing a common interest: "This is my first time at one of these meetings. Are you new, too?" If yes, you have something in common. If no, you can ask for advice: "What presentations are usually the most useful?"
  2. **Devote most of the conversation to listening.** Pick up their interests and concerns and make those the subject — that's what develops and holds attention.

- **The Rehearsed Ten-Second Self-Introduction**: spontaneity is generally overrated. Come prepared with something thoroughly rehearsed — the first couple of seconds are your name, then what you do, then a question. *"Hi, I'm Rebecca Smith, sales rep with ABC Widgets. Who are you and what interests you today?"*

- **The Social Media Rules (four)**: social media can put your network on steroids in sheer head count, but is never a substitute for face-to-face.
  - Use it to **expand** your network.
  - Use it to **keep in touch** with people you've met personally.
  - Use it to **identify** people you should try to meet face to face.
  - However much you use it, **keep making important contacts in person.**

- **The Dunbar Caveat**: anthropologist Robin Dunbar theorized in the 1990s that the human brain is physically incapable of truly knowing more than about 150 people. So a thousand LinkedIn connections are not a thousand relationships — but the other ~850 aren't meaningless either. Their value is indirect: when a contact asks whether you know about XYZ, you may not, but you may be able to say, "I know someone who does. Let me put you in touch with her."

- **The Connection-Request Filter**: beware growing any network indiscriminately. When you connect with someone, that person gains access to your business relationships. If you don't know the person, don't know someone connected to them, and they're not with a company you're interested in, consider declining.
  - The governing question: **why abandon in the social networking arena the criteria and standards you live by face to face?** In real life you wouldn't recommend someone to valued contacts without knowing them significantly. Protect your reputation and relationships in the digital realm as vigilantly as in real life.

- **Twitter as a Networking Tool — avoid being boring**:
  - Make at least some conversation relevant to something other than yourself or your business; if you're connected to many businesspeople the stream turns generic as everyone writes about their job.
  - Just because you *can* update every thought and move doesn't mean you should.
  - Make posts personal and special — family, coworkers, charitable work, the nonprofits your company supports. Make them real and you create real relationships.
  - The best posters include a **URL**: the short post tells you what it's about and offers the option of a deeper dive.
  - Learn **hash tags** (a word or two joined, preceded by #, representing a subject or event). Tag your posts from an event so your network gets a glimpse of it unfolding. **Provide high-value information — news they can use — and your value as an influential member of the community rises.**
  - **Post to be reposted.** Recipients can pass your material to a larger population; useful, compelling information gets forwarded, which heightens your visibility across the business community.

- **The Little-Known Art of Internal Networking**: rare that businesspeople consider networking inside their own walls. Everyone in the organization — receptionist to CEO — is a nexus of relationships and potentially valuable connections.
  - Reaching out: make it a habit to eat in the break room; gather a group for lunch; take a deep breath and ask your boss to lunch; organize a company softball team or other *fun* special-interest group; organize a company picnic.
  - The point is to create interaction **outside the office**, so you get to know coworkers on a human level and they get to know you.

- **To Expand Your World, Shrink It** — tactics for large organizations where out-of-office gatherings are hard to arrange:
  - Work with HR to organize a company-wide outing.
  - Propose a company directory with headshots and bios — could go on the company website at virtually no cost.
  - Work with others to organize dedicated company-wide networking opportunities.
  - Create or contribute to a departmental or company newsletter, online or print, that regularly spotlights individual employees.
  - Payoff: take the lead in bringing your department or company together into a genuine community and you become **the hub of the corporate wheel**.

## Key Concepts
- **Networking by another name** — "asking around," "contacting your friends," "talking to people in the business."
- **Dunbar's number** — ~150, the ceiling on people you can truly know; everything beyond is indirect value.
- **Internal networking** — the neglected half; connections inside your own company, including the next cubicle.
- **Hub of the corporate wheel** — what you become by convening the community rather than joining it.
- **Social inventory** — the church, synagogue, or mosque; PTA/PTO; service organization; sports club you already belong to, all usable venues.

## Mental Models
- **Get to know colleagues as people, not as holders of a job title** — and let them know you the same way.
- **The more connected you are to your industry, company, and department, the less isolated you are — and avoiding isolation is essential to both advancement and survival.**
- **Digital networking amplifies; it does not replace.** Head count is not relationship count.
- **You are never in a room of total strangers.** Shared industry, shared ambition, and a shared awkward moment are all openings.
- **Spontaneity is overrated.** Rehearse the ten seconds you'll need most.

## Anti-patterns
- **Shutting down your job-hunting network once employed.** The single most common failure in this chapter.
- **Declining invitations.** Accept lunches, office events, parties, conventions, and professional get-togethers every time you can — some on your own dime if the company won't pay.
- **Being a spectator at gatherings.** Talk to people; carry business cards and pass them out generously.
- **Growing a network indiscriminately**, accepting connection requests from people you don't know and can't vouch for.
- **Applying looser standards online than you would in person.**
- **Confining networking to outside your employer**, or even to outside your department.
- **Broadcasting only about yourself and your work**, which makes your stream generic and forgettable.

## Worked Example

**Opening a conversation with someone you half-recognize:**
> "Hi, I'm George Perkins from ABC Company. I've seen you at these meetings before, but I've never had a chance to introduce myself. Tell me, what do you do?"

**Opening with a genuine stranger — the reveal-a-commonality move, which works on either answer:**
> "This is my first time at one of these meetings. Are you new, too?"
> — *Yes* → you have something in common.
> — *No* → pivot to asking advice: "What presentations are usually the most useful?"

**The rehearsed ten-second introduction:**
> "Hi, I'm Rebecca Smith, sales rep with ABC Widgets. Who are you and what interests you today?"

Name → role → question, in that order, with the question handing the conversation to them.

**Converting a weak-tie network into value you couldn't otherwise deliver:**
> **Contact:** Do you know anything about XYZ?
> **You:** I don't — but I know someone who does. Let me put you in touch with her.

This is the entire practical payoff of the ~850 connections beyond your Dunbar 150.

## Key Takeaways
1. Networking got most people their job; almost nobody keeps doing it, which is exactly the gap you exploit.
2. Effective networking is behaving like a friend: ask after people, stay in touch, remember their dates, and maintain as well as expand.
3. Get into the zone by discarding the energy-sapping thoughts, then get others talking about themselves.
4. Prepare and rehearse a ten-second self-introduction; spontaneity is overrated.
5. Use social media to expand, maintain, and target — never as a substitute for face-to-face.
6. Apply your real-life vetting standards to connection requests; a connection gains access to your relationships.
7. Post high-value, personal, linkable material worth forwarding, not a stream of self-updates.
8. Network internally — everyone from receptionist to CEO — and create out-of-office occasions where none exist.
9. Convene the community rather than merely joining it, and you become the hub of the corporate wheel.
