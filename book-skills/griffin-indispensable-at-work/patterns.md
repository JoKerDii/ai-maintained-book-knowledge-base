# Patterns & Techniques — Be Indispensable at Work

## The Three-Front Research Protocol
**When to use**: before any conversation with your boss about your future — raise, promotion, reassignment, or "how am I doing?"
**How**: (1) *Company* — unread memos and newsletters, your own job description and recent evaluations, the website's About Us / press releases / From the CEO / Investor Relations, a web search on the company name, legitimately available sales figures, 3–6 months of stock performance. (2) *Industry* — keyword searches, trade publications for trends, web searches on your boss and his bosses for moves and retirements, competitors' ads, whether the market is growing or contracting. (3) *Yourself* — the achievement inventory and skills dig.
**Trade-offs**: takes a week you'd rather spend working; converts the meeting from a plea into a pitch, which is the whole return.

## Speak the Language of Business
**When to use**: any description of yourself, your work, or your value.
**How**: write the raw activity list without editing, then pass over it again attaching a dollar figure or percentage to each line. Nouns and verbs beat adjectives and adverbs; nouns and verbs linked to numbers beat everything.
**Trade-offs**: some real contributions resist quantification — attach the nearest proxy rather than dropping them.

## The Verb Conversion
**When to use**: whenever you're about to claim a trait or a job title.
**How**: adjective → verb ("I am persuasive" → "I persuade"), then title → function by choosing People, Data, or Things ("sales rep" → "I persuade people").
**Trade-offs**: none; this is strictly additive precision.

## The Three-Sheet Dig
**When to use**: to surface transferable skills you don't know you have.
**How**: three sheets headed PEOPLE / DATA / THINGS, each with "I am good at . . ."; target 25+ items per sheet with no editing; then winnow to your 8–10 highest-level skills and elaborate each as skill + object + benefit ("I am good at negotiating deals that please the buyer as well as the seller").
**Trade-offs**: the first pass feels wasteful; the buried items only appear because you didn't censor.

## The Transferable Skills Ladder
**When to use**: deciding which skill to claim and which to develop next.
**How**: place your claim on the People ladder — following directions < communicating < persuading < negotiating < coaching/mentoring. Claim *persuading* and you keep a sales job; *negotiating* opens account executive; *mentoring* reads as VP material.
**Trade-offs**: overclaiming a rung you can't demonstrate is worse than accurately claiming a lower one.

## The Five-Part Achievement Story
**When to use**: when raw inventory hasn't produced enough evidence; before any conversation requiring concrete proof.
**How**: write at least three, each following goal → obstacle → step-by-step solution → outcome with significance → objective evaluation quantified in dollars. Then map each story to your skill lists.
**Trade-offs**: slower than list-making; it's the only method that reliably surfaces skills you didn't know you had.

## Feature → Benefit Translation
**When to use**: self-marketing to a boss or any employer.
**How**: state the skill (feature), ask what emotional state it creates in the customer, then reduce to a bottom-line factor. "I negotiate prices that satisfy both sides" → "I create satisfaction" → "repeat business, quarter after quarter."
**Trade-offs**: your job is easier than an ad agency's — your customer has one motive, to save and make money.

## The Outside-Consultant Method
**When to use**: when no existing position fits, and you want to create one.
**How**: imagine you were hired externally to recommend improvements. (1) Consider what position should be added or replaced; (2) work out recommendations in detail; (3) decide what facts and figures support them and gather the data; (4) draw up benefit scenarios; (5) combine with your own performance record so you present as the ideal candidate.
**Trade-offs**: the company may be unable to act — but the exercise still markets your work ethic and identifies projects. No positive effort is wasted.

## Let the Boss Own the Idea
**When to use**: selling a new or redefined role.
**How**: never proclaim a new position; describe the expanded role and let *him* name it. Use *we / us / our*, never *I / me / you*. Use "what if" language. Connect the proposal to something he already said he wanted, by date and detail.
**Trade-offs**: you forfeit credit. You buy the reality — which is what actually matters to your career.

## The Helping Vocabulary
**When to use**: continuously, in every interaction.
**How**: move the conversation from *I* and *you* to *we*, *us*, *our*, then layer on collaboration verbs. "You've really got a problem" → "*We've* really got a problem" → "What should *we* do about it?"
**Trade-offs**: none; this is the cheapest rapport instrument in the book.

## Asking for Help (in preference to offering it)
**When to use**: to build rapport faster than an offer of help does.
**How**: identify the best source first (who does what job, who has influence and respect, who's in the loop, who do others ask, who decides), then calibrate urgency: "Tom, I need your help" / "…when's a good time to talk?" / "…mind if I pick your brain?" Never apologize for asking; never belittle yourself; thank specifically.
**Trade-offs**: feels like taking; it's actually giving the opportunity to be helpful.

## The 12 Rules of Constructive Criticism
**When to use**: any time you're about to correct someone.
**How**: never criticize a person, only an issue/action/outcome; confirm it's worth criticizing and fixable; ask permission; choose a private place and time; cite specifics; bring alternatives; use an informal tone, never command language; temper with praise; one issue at a time; follow up with positive feedback; no threats — but no sugarcoating either.
**Trade-offs**: slower than blurting. It's the difference between a correction and an enemy.

## FIDO
**When to use**: as the first filter on any irritation or anger.
**How**: Forget It, Drive On. Act only when a *situation* — never a person — is damaging your work life or the organization's productivity.
**Trade-offs**: some real problems get deferred; most annoyances are transitory and acting on them enshrines them with destructive permanence.

## The Four-Component Apology
**When to use**: as soon as you've erred; never wait to be asked.
**How**: (1) say you're sorry; (2) own the error; (3) express empathy for their feelings; (4) offer a remedy, shifting from *I/you* to *we/us*. Keep the tone sincere, not dramatic — the apology is about their feelings, not yours.
**Trade-offs**: none. It eases your guilt while giving the other person the chance to forgive.

## The Trigger Object
**When to use**: preemptively, while calm, so it's available when conflict erupts.
**How**: choose an object always reliably on your desk (or a golfer's counter for your pocket, per Shapiro's *Precision Nirvana*). Say out loud that it is your signal to stop, listen silently, and think before responding to the issue rather than the personality.
**Trade-offs**: nothing is special about the object; it works only because you committed to it in advance.

## Slap a Label on It
**When to use**: when emotion is fogging a dispute.
**How**: define the substantive issues, name each one, list them — then make yourself the public scribe: "Let's take an inventory of exactly what issues are in play. Alice, why don't you go first?" Enforce turns without interruption.
**Trade-offs**: costs meeting time; carefully describing a problem does roughly half the work of solving it.

## The Six Questions Before You Engage
**When to use**: deciding whether a conflict is worth fighting.
**How**: Is the outcome worth the energy? Worth risking goodwill? Has the other side already decided, and is it reviewable? Can you live with them winning? Does letting them win buy future bargaining chips? Does this have to be solved now?
**Trade-offs**: the exception is a team about to choose a destructive course — that battle is always worth fighting.

## Exploit Agreement, Transform Disagreement
**When to use**: converting a stalled dispute into progress.
**How**: build first in the areas everyone agrees on so everyone gains a stake, then split the remainder into what's working and what isn't, and name an owner for the path between. "We agree the project is feasible, but we need more data. Harry, can you get us the additional data?"
**Trade-offs**: none — but it is valueless without the action plan and follow-through.

## The Action Plan + Executive Follow-Through
**When to use**: the moment any meeting produces agreement.
**How**: list the tasks; assign each to a defined owner; set objectives, measurable performance criteria, and timetables; get management buy-in. Then write the summarizing memo yourself, monitor within the deadlines, and encourage, cajole, nag, and goad as required.
**Trade-offs**: it's unglamorous work no one assigned you. An *executive* is someone who executes.

## The Bully Protocol
**When to use**: under a personal attack that bypasses the merits.
**How**: ignore the illegitimate question, answer with facts; minimize contact and prefer email; maintain eye contact, don't interrupt, don't say "calm down"; assert and reassert without arguing or backing down; show as little emotion as possible.
**Trade-offs**: the bully wants a quick emotional skirmish. Deliver a slow war of attrition and the fire dies.

## The Passive Aggressor Protocol
**When to use**: with anyone whose reliability you can rely on being absent.
**How**: stage-by-stage timetables with outcome criteria; take charge early and put the division of tasks in writing; demand facts and figures, not assurances; send tickler emails; repeat instructions as often as necessary and express no frustration.
**Trade-offs**: it is micromanagement, and it costs you time you shouldn't have to spend.

## The Chronic Complainer Protocol
**When to use**: when whining is being used to dodge work.
**How**: verify the complaint has no merit *first*; then cease all discussion of merits; express no sympathy; state the assignment's importance; state that it is necessary; state it again; finally, grant permission to fail — promise to evaluate results and help make it right.
**Trade-offs**: step seven means agreeing to take on extra work. It may be the only route to usable output.

## The Schemer Protocol
**When to use**: prevention always; confrontation only when damage control fails.
**How**: prevent with corporate karma, genuine human engagement, standing allies built before you need them, and never gossiping yourself. Respond to rumors with truth. If confronting: a planned meeting outside the office (never a hallway), broach issues not the person, no "gotcha," recruit their help stopping the talk, end looking forward — and never say the rumors hurt your feelings.
**Trade-offs**: involving your boss may work, but risks tagging you as part of a problem rather than the solution.

## The Poacher Protection
**When to use**: before discussing any idea with anyone.
**How**: write and date the idea first, store it on a drive you control; distribute it marked as a draft with a comment deadline and a final-draft date; choose recipients carefully and let each know who else received it. If poached in a meeting, speak up immediately and persist politely.
**Trade-offs**: slows down casual idea-sharing, which collaboration requires; the dated draft is the cheapest insurance available.

## Sell Your Influence
**When to use**: when the danger signs appear and you feel yourself being pushed out of the loop.
**How**: pick one proposal; identify a powerful person as a lever; sell the benefit *to that person*; shift from "my concept" to "our concept" as buy-in starts; recruit your critics rather than deflecting them.
**Trade-offs**: greater exposure at the moment you feel most like hiding — which is exactly the point.

## The Two-Stage Career Change
**When to use**: when your target career differs from your current one in both title and field.
**How**: change one component to take a job; gain experience; change the other later. Either route works, and the first step is often easiest without leaving your current company.
**Trade-offs**: slower than a single leap, and far more likely to succeed.

## The Informational Interview
**When to use**: after your immediate subsistence needs are handled, before applying anywhere.
**How**: identify the career → the companies → their product → the individuals producing it. Contact them explicitly *not* as a job applicant. Ask: how did you get started; what do you like most; what do you dislike most; what are the industry's prospects; who else should I talk to? Cap at ten minutes unless extended. Always send a thank-you email.
**Trade-offs**: no job comes of any single one. Enough of them broadcast your name across an industry before you apply.
