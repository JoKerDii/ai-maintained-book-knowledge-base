# Chapter 8: You Are Here

## Core Idea
The harder you try to "use time well," the more the present becomes a mere corridor to a better future that never arrives — because to *use* time is to treat it instrumentally, as a means to an end. Burkeman argues that life is nothing but a succession of present moments, each valuable in itself, and that the cure is not to try harder to "be present" but to notice you were never anywhere but here.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **De-instrumentalize the moment**: Weigh the present-moment value of an activity alongside its future payoff, instead of letting future outcomes automatically win.
  - When to use: Whenever you catch yourself doing something *only* because it sets up a later result (parenting, work, leisure, relationships).
  - How: (1) Name the future outcome you're chasing. (2) Ask "Is this also worth doing right now, even if it produced no future benefit?" (3) Let intrinsic value count in the balance — don't let it always be overridden by "for later."
- **Treat it as the last time (Sam Harris)**: Approach experiences with the reverence you'd show if it were the final instance — because for everything, eventually, there is one.
  - When to use: Recurring experiences you take for granted — picking up your child, a swim in the ocean, a conversation with an old friend.
  - How: Recognize you almost never *know* which time is the last; therefore extend that final-time reverence to ordinary instances now.

## Key Concepts
- **Instrumentalizing time**: Treating every present moment purely as a means to a future end, so the "real" value of life is always located somewhere you haven't yet reached.
- **The "when-I-finally" mind**: The belief that fulfillment begins only after some specific accomplishment ("when I finally get my workload under control..."); since the present is treated only as a path, it never satisfies, and a new postponement always appears.
- **The causal catastrophe (Adam Gopnik)**: Judging the rightness of how you raise a child solely by the adult it produces — which strips childhood of intrinsic value by reducing it to a training ground for adulthood. Generalizes to any present treated only as preparation.
- **Purposiveness (Keynes)**: The compulsion to push the meaning of one's actions forward into the future ("jam tomorrow and never jam today"), ultimately a bid for a spurious immortality and a refusal to die.
- **The billable-hour ethos (Cathleen Kaveny)**: Conceiving of time, and thus yourself, as a commodity sold in sixty-minute chunks; any hour not sold feels wasted, and non-commodifiable activities (a child's school play) start to feel unaffordable.
- **Capitalism as instrumentalizing machine**: An economic system that converts the earth, your time, and your abilities into "resources" for future profit — partly explaining why successful, wealthy people are often miserable.
- **Intrinsic value of the present**: A child's purpose is to be a child, not to grow up (Stoppard's Herzen: "Nature... pours the whole of itself into each moment... Later is too late"). Moments are worth living for their own sake.
- **Absent in the present**: The self-defeating strain of *trying* to live in the moment, which is itself a covert instrumentalism — using now as a means to an enriching now.

## Mental Models
- Use **"is this also worth doing now?"** when an activity is justified purely by its future return — the future shouldn't get automatic veto power over the present.
- Use **the donkey-and-carrot image (Alan Watts)** when you notice life as an endless on-ramp — nursery school readies you for kindergarten, college for the business world; the carrot hangs from your own collar, so you never arrive.
- Use **"you are already in the moment"** when you feel you're failing at mindfulness — your very worry about not being present is itself a present-moment thought; there is no outside of now to escape to.
- Use **the fisherman parable** when ambition promises that grinding now buys leisure later — sometimes the future state you're sacrificing for is the life you could simply live today.

## Anti-patterns
- **Optimizing childhood (or any present) for outcomes**: Reading parenting how-tos to engineer the "best" future adult turns a living person into a tool for assuaging your own anxiety — and the evidence for the competing methods barely exists anyway.
- **The "when-I-finally" deferral**: Postponing fulfillment until a milestone guarantees it never comes; the mind simply relocates the finish line.
- **Trying hard to "be here now"**: Effortful presence (or savoring, or scheduled extra sex à la the Carnegie Mellon study) drains the experience of flavor — like trying too hard to fall asleep. It's instrumentalism wearing a mindfulness costume.
- **Living for a frictionless future**: Believing meaning lies in a coming golden era lets you dodge the truth that the moment of truth is always now — and you collaborate in this to feel in control.

## Worked Example
Burkeman's son is born, and his first instinct is to *optimize*: he buys stacks of how-to books, splitting into "Baby Trainers" (strict schedules) versus "Natural Parents" (co-sleeping, baby-wearing) — two camps that, he later learns, have almost no credible evidence behind them, and both obsessively justify their methods by the future adult they'll produce. He realizes his real motive in seeking the advice was that this was his stance toward all of life: spend every day striving for future outcomes so that, someday, life would finally run smoothly. Worse, he sees he was using his son as a means to his own future peace of mind. The turn comes when he notices his son is "sheer presence" — he'll be zero years old for only one year. Watching the tiny fist close around his finger *without* checking it against developmental milestones becomes the whole point. The future still matters (vaccinations, preschools), but it can't be the only thing that counts.

## Key Takeaways
1. Notice when an activity is justified *only* by its future payoff, and deliberately weigh whether it's worth doing right now too.
2. Catch the "when-I-finally" thought in the act — fulfillment that waits on a milestone keeps moving the milestone.
3. Stop optimizing the present (your work, your kids, your leisure) as raw material for a hypothetical better future; that very habit is how people miss their lives.
4. Don't strain to "be present." You are already, inescapably, in the moment — the work is realizing you had no other option, not manufacturing a state.
5. Watch for the billable-hour reflex in yourself: if an experience can't be tallied or commodified, you may be treating it as an indulgence you "can't afford."
6. Extend last-time reverence to ordinary recurring moments, since you rarely know which one is the last.

## Connects To
- **Ch 1–2 (finitude, the efficiency trap)**: "Using time well" is the productivity drive turned inward on every moment; instrumentalizing time is the efficiency trap at the scale of a single life.
- **Ch 9 (rediscovering rest)**: The push to make leisure "pay off" later is the same instrumentalism; both chapters argue for activities done as ends in themselves, "atelic" rather than telic.
- **Stoic "amor fati" / Buddhist non-striving**: Herzen's "life's bounty is in its flow" and Jay Jennifer Matthews's "the life of this moment has no outside" echo accepting and inhabiting reality rather than mining it for future gain.
- **Hedonic treadmill**: The "when-I-finally" mind is the psychological mechanism behind why attained goals reset expectations instead of delivering lasting fulfillment.
