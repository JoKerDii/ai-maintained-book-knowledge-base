# Chapter 9: Rediscovering Rest

## Core Idea
We have inherited a "deeply bizarre" idea that leisure is only worthwhile if it makes us more productive later—turning rest into another item on the to-do list. To actually inhabit your four thousand weeks, you must reclaim leisure as an end in itself, doing some things purely for the doing, with no payoff.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Atelic activities (Kieran Setiya)**: Activities whose value isn't derived from their *telos* (ultimate aim). They have "no outcome whose achievement exhausts them"—you can stop doing them but never *complete* them (a country walk, listening to a favorite song, an evening of conversation).
  - When to use: When your relationship with time has become almost entirely instrumental and the present moment has lost its meaning (Setiya felt this as a midlife emptiness from a project-driven, telic life).
  - How: Deliberately seed your days with activities where the only thing you want from them is the doing itself. Don't aim to get them "done"; there is no later point at which you'll have accomplished all the walking you meant to do.

- **Rules for Rest (the Sabbath principle)**: Rest is not what happens by default when you stop working; you need structures—ideally socially reinforced ones—that *make* rest happen.
  - When to use: Whenever willpower alone fails to produce genuine downtime (the always-open shops, the laptop at home).
  - How: Adopt exacting, intentional rules (the Jewish/Puritan Sabbath required advance preparation). A self-imposed "digital sabbath" helps but is weaker because it lacks the social sanction that makes a rule easy to keep.

## Key Concepts

- **Instrumentalized leisure**: Justifying time off only by its usefulness for something else ("Relax! You'll Be More Productive"), which makes leisure feel like a chore—"work in the worst sense."
- **The Decline of Pleasure (Walter Kerr, 1962)**: We are "compelled to read for profit, party for contacts… gamble for charity," etc.—even fun is harnessed to an external purpose.
- **True leisure (Aristotle)**: For the ancients, leisure was not a means but *the* end to which other things were means—the highest virtue, chosen for its own sake. *Negotium* (business) literally means "not-leisure."
- **Idleness aversion / pathological productivity**: A widespread inability to rest; getting antsy when not being productive. Danielle Steel writing in 20-hour days is an extreme case—work as "refuge" from difficult emotions.
- **The Protestant work ethic (Max Weber)**: Calvinist anxiety over predestination made relentless work a way to prove (to self and others) that one belonged to the elect; idleness became evidence one might already be damned.
- **Atelic vs. telic**: Telic activities exist to be *done* and to produce outcomes (publishing a paper to get tenure); atelic ones exist only in the present doing.
- **The hobbyist as subversive**: In an age of instrumentalization, doing something for its own sake—offering no profit or productivity payoff—is a quiet act of resistance. A good hobby should feel slightly embarrassing.
- **"On the receiving end" (Walter Brueggemann)**: The Sabbath as the claim that whatever you've done by week's end might be *enough*—nothing more needed to justify your existence.

## Mental Models

- **Use atelic activities when** your days feel hollowed out by ceaseless future-aiming—deliberately do things that cannot be completed or "won."
- **Use Sabbath-style rules when** you keep failing to stop—rest needs scaffolding (habit + social sanction), not just intention.
- **Use the "embarrassing hobby" test when** choosing a pastime—if it would never earn money or acclaim (Rod Stewart's model railway, beginner surfing), that's a sign you're doing it for love, not status.
- **Expect discomfort, not delight, when** you first truly rest. Per John Gray, idleness is alien to an age where nothing means anything "unless it leads to something else"; the discomfort is a sign you should keep going, not stop.

## Anti-patterns

- **Resting only to recharge for work**: Treating rest as recuperation for further productivity drains it of intrinsic value and converts free time into a chore.
- **The "side hustle" reflex**: It feels more respectable to monetize a hobby than to do it purely for pleasure—precisely because we can't tolerate value with no future payoff.
- **Optimizing leisure**: Training endlessly for a 10K but never just going for a run; backpacking to "stockpile experiences" rather than to explore. The goal contaminates the experience.
- **Punishing rest**: SoulCycle/CrossFit-style "leisure" as self-flagellation—expunging the guilt of laziness rather than actually relaxing.
- **Relying on willpower alone**: A digital sabbath with no social reinforcement is fragile and prone to the same strain as forcing yourself to be "present."

## Worked Example
Setiya, approaching forty, felt a creeping emptiness. Tracing it, he saw a life packed with telic activities: he published papers to earn tenure, sought tenure for security and reputation, taught to advance those ends. Every act pointed at a future outcome, so the present kept evaporating—and as mortality came into view, the absurdity of always postponing fulfillment became unbearable. The remedy he found was the atelic walk: just walking, a skill he hasn't improved since age four, with no destination and no way to "finish." Contrast Schopenhauer's pendulum—we swing between the pain of not-yet-having and the boredom of having-attained. The atelic activity escapes the pendulum entirely, because "there is no more to going for a walk than what you are doing right now."

## Key Takeaways
1. Spend at least some leisure "wastefully"—focused solely on the pleasure of the experience—because that is the *only* way not to waste it.
2. Build rules and rituals for rest; don't expect it to occur by default. Borrow the Sabbath's intentionality even without the religion.
3. Cultivate atelic activities and hobbies you cannot complete and need not be good at—mediocrity is a feature, since it frees you from "using time well."
4. When real rest first feels uncomfortable rather than blissful, take that as confirmation you're doing it right.
5. Accept "this is it": your days aren't climbing toward a future state of invulnerable happiness, so show up for the brief time you have *now*.
