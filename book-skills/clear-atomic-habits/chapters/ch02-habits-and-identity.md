# Chapter 2: How Your Habits Shape Your Identity (and Vice Versa)

## Core Idea
Lasting change is identity change. The most durable habits flow from who you believe you are, not what you want to achieve. Every action is a vote for a type of person.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three Layers of Behavior Change** (concentric onion): Outcomes (what you get) → Processes (what you do) → Identity (what you believe). Most people change from the outside in; durable change works from the inside out.
  - When to use: diagnosing why a habit won't stick.
  - How: shift the target from outcome-based ("I want to be skinny") to identity-based ("I am the kind of person who doesn't miss workouts").
- **Identity-Based Habits**: focus on who you wish to become; let identity drive behavior.
- **Two-Step Process to Change Identity**: (1) Decide the type of person you want to be; (2) Prove it to yourself with small wins.
  - How: work backward from a desired result — "Who is the type of person who could get this outcome?" — then accumulate evidence.

## Key Concepts
- **Identity**: from Latin *essentitas* (being) + *identidem* (repeatedly) → "repeated beingness." You are your repeated actions.
- **Votes for an identity**: each habit is a piece of evidence ("maybe this is who I am"); you need a majority, not unanimity.
- **Identity conflict**: the biggest barrier to change — a habit that clashes with your self-image ("that's not who I am") won't last.

## Mental Models
- "Every action you take is a vote for the type of person you wish to become."
- Reframe goals as identities: not "run a marathon" but "become a runner"; not "read a book" but "become a reader."
- Your habits are how you *embody* an identity — proof, not aspiration.

## Anti-patterns
- **"No thanks, I'm trying to quit."** — still identifies as a smoker. Contrast: "No thanks, I'm not a smoker" (identity shift).
- **Clinging to a fixed identity** ("I'm bad at math," "I'm always late") — self-image sabotages change; progress requires unlearning.

## Worked Example
The 100-pound weight-loss friend used one identity question as an all-day compass: "What would a healthy person do?" — walk or cab? burrito or salad? By acting like a healthy person long enough, she became one. Contrast Brian Clark: he stopped nail-biting not through willpower but after a manicure made him *proud* of his nails — pride in an identity locked in the behavior.

## Key Takeaways
1. Decide who you want to be, then let each small win be evidence.
2. The goal is not the outcome; it's becoming the person for whom that outcome is natural.
3. You don't need perfection — just a majority of votes for the right identity.
4. Continuously edit and expand your identity; don't over-attach to one version.
