# Chapter 11: Walk Slowly, but Never Backward (3rd Law — Make It Easy)

## Core Idea
Habits form through repetition (frequency), not through time passed or planning. Stop preparing to act and start accumulating reps — you only build a habit by performing it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Motion vs. Action**: *Motion* is planning, strategizing, learning — it feels like progress but produces none. *Action* is the behavior that delivers the outcome.
  - When to use: catching yourself researching/preparing instead of doing.
  - How: notice when you're "in motion" (reading about, planning, outlining) and convert it into a rep of the actual habit.
- **Frequency, Not Time**: a habit's automaticity depends on how many times you've performed it, not how many days you've had it.
  - How: focus on getting the reps in; don't ask "how long?" ask "how many?"

## Key Concepts
- **Motion**: preparing to act (often a form of procrastination that feels productive because it avoids failure).
- **Action**: the repetition that actually forms the habit and produces results.
- **Habit line / automaticity curve**: performance and automaticity rise with repetitions until a behavior becomes automatic — the exact number varies, so count reps not days.

## Mental Models
- "If motion doesn't lead to results, why do we do it? Because it lets us feel like we're making progress without running the risk of failure."
- Repetition is the price of automaticity — walk slowly (small reps) but never backward (never stop).

## Anti-patterns
- **Chronic motion**: endlessly planning the perfect workout program, reading twenty productivity books, outlining a business you never start.
- Waiting to feel ready; readiness is a byproduct of reps, not a prerequisite.

## Worked Example
The photography-class study: a professor split students into a "quantity" group (graded purely on the number of photos submitted — 100 photos = an A) and a "quality" group (graded on a single perfect photo). The best photos all came from the quantity group. By shooting constantly, they experimented, learned from mistakes, and improved — while the quality group theorized about perfection and produced little. Reps beat planning.

## Key Takeaways
1. It's the reps that build the habit, not the time elapsed.
2. Distinguish motion from action; convert planning into doing.
3. Don't count days to mastery — count repetitions.
