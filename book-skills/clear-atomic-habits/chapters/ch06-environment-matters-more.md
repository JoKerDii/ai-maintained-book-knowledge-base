# Chapter 6: Motivation Is Overrated; Environment Often Matters More (1st Law)

## Core Idea
Behavior is a function of the person *and* their environment. You act on whatever cues are most obvious around you, so redesign the space rather than relying on willpower.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Environment Design**: make the cues of good habits obvious and visible in your surroundings.
  - When to use: any habit you want automatic without daily effort.
  - How: put the cue in your line of sight (fruit bowl on the counter, guitar stand in the living room, water bottle on your desk).
- **One Space, One Use**: give each context a single, clear association so cues don't compete.
  - How: don't work, eat, and relax in the same spot; "every habit should have a home." When contexts blur, the strongest cue wins.

## Key Concepts
- **Cue is king for initiation**: the most obvious/available cue usually determines behavior — vision dominates (~half the brain's sensory resources).
- **Context as cue**: habits attach to whole contexts (a room, a situation), not just single triggers — which is why new environments make new habits easier.
- **"Environment is the invisible hand that shapes human behavior."**

## Mental Models
- You don't have to be the victim of your environment — you can be its architect.
- To build a habit in a crowded context, do it in a fresh context where no competing cues exist.

## Anti-patterns
- Relying on self-control to resist cues you keep in plain sight (junk food on the counter, phone on the desk).
- Mixing many activities in one space until the room cues nothing in particular.

## Worked Example
A supermarket sells more of whatever it makes most visible: end-cap displays and eye-level shelves drive purchases; the ~10% highest-margin items get prime placement. Apply the same to yourself — want to drink more water? Fill several bottles each morning and place them where you'll be all day. Want to practice guitar? Keep it on a stand in the middle of the room, not in a closet.

## Key Takeaways
1. Design your environment so good cues are obvious and bad cues are hidden.
2. Make each space a single, dedicated context to keep cues clean.
3. A new environment is the easiest place to install a new habit.
