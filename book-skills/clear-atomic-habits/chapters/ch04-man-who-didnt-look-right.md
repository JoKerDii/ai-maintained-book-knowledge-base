# Chapter 4: The Man Who Didn't Look Right (1st Law — Make It Obvious)

## Core Idea
You can't change a habit you're not aware of. With enough repetition, behavior becomes non-conscious; the first step of change is bringing the invisible cues of your habits into the light.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Pointing-and-Calling**: say your actions aloud to raise them from automatic to conscious (Japanese railway safety system — cuts errors up to ~85%, accidents ~30%).
  - When to use: catching an automatic bad habit in the act.
  - How: name the action and its consequence out loud — "I'm about to eat this cookie, but I don't need it. It will cause me to gain weight."
- **Habits Scorecard**: list your daily habits and mark each as good (+), bad (–), or neutral (=) relative to your desired identity.
  - When to use: building awareness before trying to change anything.
  - How: write every habit in a typical day → score each against the person you want to become → no judgment yet, just awareness.

## Key Concepts
- **Non-conscious behavior**: expert habits run without deliberate thought; cues get so ingrained we stop noticing them.
- **Awareness precedes change**: you must recognize a cue before you can respond to it differently.

## Mental Models
- The problem isn't always motivation — often it's that the behavior is invisible to you.
- Score a habit by outcome relative to *identity*: does this move me toward or away from who I want to be?

## Anti-patterns
- Trying to break a habit you can't consciously catch — the cue fires and the response completes before you notice.

## Worked Example
The chapter opens with a paramedic-style tale of someone who intuitively sensed a person "didn't look right" and was about to have a heart attack — expertise so internalized it reads as intuition. The paired practice: pointing-and-calling on Japanese trains, where operators physically point at signals and speak them aloud, dramatically reducing mistakes by forcing a conscious check on an otherwise automatic action.

## Key Takeaways
1. Repetition makes behavior automatic and therefore invisible; awareness must come first.
2. Use the Habits Scorecard to inventory and honestly rate your current habits.
3. Point-and-call to interrupt automatic bad habits at the moment of the cue.
