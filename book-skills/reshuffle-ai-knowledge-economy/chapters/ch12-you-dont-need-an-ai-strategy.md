# Chapter 12: You Don't Need an AI Strategy — Where to play, how to win

## Core Idea
Companies don't need an AI strategy; they need a strategy for the conditions AI creates. AI changes coordination (which redraws where to play) and control (which redefines how to win) — so start from the system that is emerging, not from the tasks you already perform.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Where to play / how to win, restated for flux**: When the playing field is in constant flux, choosing where to play means betting not just on new positions but on a new structure of the playing field — a new system. How to win is less about internal excellence in capabilities and more about establishing control points in the external playing field.
  - When to use: Whenever someone in the room asks for "our AI strategy."
  - How: Zoom out to the whole system of work, then work back in. Ask in this order: Has the competitive landscape itself changed? Has the basis of value creation moved? Does the business model still make sense? What new capabilities does this new game require? Which roles and workflows still matter, and which have lost their edge? Only then bring in AI — as a way to win the new game, not improve the old one.
  - Why it works: AI multiplies force, and force applied in the wrong direction only accelerates failure. Failure mode: asking "what are we doing now, and how can AI automate it?" — optimizing what exists instead of designing what should be.
- **Coordination determines where to play; control determines how to win**: New coordination technologies (container, barcode, AI) expand the scope of economic activity and make new strategic positions viable. Control is then won by identifying the constraints the new system creates and building the capacity to resolve them better than anyone else. Winning doesn't come from owning what's scarce, but from coordinating what's stuck.
  - When to use: Mapping a technology shift onto strategic choice.
  - How: Ask "what system is emerging, and what role can we play at its center?" — then find the second-order constraints (Singapore's was trade risk, not logistics) and become the party that resolves them.
- **Four strategic postures toward flux**: Reactive optimizers use AI to speed up today's tasks without asking whether they're still valuable (agribusinesses predicting yield better). Anticipators — the Gretzkys — sense value migration early but sell insight on top of old workflows (precision agriculture). Logic shifters — the Currys — change how and where decisions get made, restructuring the organizational system (John Deere moving the decision from farmer into the machine). Field reshapers restructure the entire playing field and ecosystem (Climate Corp connecting seed selection, inputs, risk, and carbon markets).
  - When to use: Auditing your own AI initiatives; naming which posture each one actually reflects.
  - How: The deeper the reimagining of coordination, the greater the shift in who holds power and the more enduring the advantage.

## Key Concepts
- **Framing error / misframing**: Treating AI as a toolkit for faster execution of familiar tasks rather than a catalyst for reimagining the system of work.
- **Technological solutionism**: The seductive belief that better tools alone fix systemic problems; it offers an illusion of control and good headlines.
- **Three traps of instability**: Chasing transient advantage; substituting execution speed for direction; a perpetual cycle of experimentation with no committed direction — all improve odds *within* the current system and tether you to its logic.
- **Motion vs. direction**: Speed isn't the same as direction; Yahoo had agility at scale and raced the wrong way.
- **Control point (external)**: A position in the playing field others must rely on, built from resolving the system's constraints rather than owning scarce assets.
- **Second-order constraint**: The risk or friction a new system creates once its first-order coordination problem is solved (Singapore's governance and trust layer).
- **Behavior graph**: TikTok's AI-driven alternative to the social graph — what you engage with determines what you see — which dissolved the cold start problem and the incumbents' moat.
- **Cold start problem**: The constraint TikTok designed around; Facebook needed your friends to join, TikTok needed only your first few swipes.
- **Tiger-proofing**: Redesigning the playing field itself in response to one player's dominance — the strongest strategic position available.

## Mental Models
- Use "zoom out to the system, then work back in toward the centre" whenever the ground is shifting — starting at the task is starting inside out.
- Think of asking for an AI strategy as requesting a prescription before the diagnosis.
- Think of the Gretzky posture as the floor, not the ceiling: anticipating the puck still assumes the game's logic holds; Curry changed the logic and Tiger changed the field.
- Use scarcity to explain yesterday's advantage and constraint-resolution to build tomorrow's — AI commoditizes scarce expertise at near-zero marginal cost.
- If the strategy begins and ends with efficiency, you don't get a shot at the new playing field.

## Anti-patterns
- **Starting inside out**: "What are we doing now, and how can AI automate it?" optimizes what exists rather than designing what should be.
- **Assembling strategy from scattered experiments**: Like assembling puzzle pieces without knowing the picture; optimizing tasks in isolation rarely transforms anything.
- **Confusing executional agility for strategy**: Yahoo trained thousands in Scrum and still misread the structural shift; it became faster at solving the wrong problems.
- **Retreating to the tasks AI hasn't commoditized yet**: A reactive game against a shrinking pocket, with intensifying competition and diminishing returns.
- **Misreading where to play**: Anchor in a fading system and you will inevitably misread how to win within it (Chegg: 99% of market value gone in under two years).

## Worked Example
Singapore's rise is usually explained by geography — sitting astride the Strait of Malacca — with "build the best port" as the how-to-win. That reads the old playing field. Containerization had solved fragmented trade's coordination problem, and Singapore's real where-to-play was the newly viable landscape of reliable cross-border trade. Its how-to-win came from resolving that system's constraints. First, coordination: reliable trade needed air, sea, road, and rail stitched together, so Singapore rebuilt the port as an integrated logistics hub with synchronized multimodal scheduling and integrated customs — a control point in intermodal transport. Second, a second-order constraint: risk. In a region marked by corruption, delays, and regulatory uncertainty, its location was arguably a liability. Transparent customs, strict anti-corruption enforcement, rule-based dispute resolution, and regulatory clarity made it the trust layer for regional trade. Had it merely automated container handling and celebrated turnaround times, it would have missed the system-level opportunity entirely.

## Key Takeaways
1. Replace "what is our AI strategy?" with "where do we play and how do we win under the conditions AI creates?"
2. Run the diagnostic outward-in: has the landscape changed, has the basis of value creation moved, does the business model still hold, what capabilities does the new game need, which roles and workflows have lost their edge — then, and only then, apply AI.
3. Use coordination shifts to choose where to play; ask what system is emerging and what role you can play at its center.
4. Win by resolving the new system's constraints better than anyone else, including the second-order ones like risk and trust.
5. Name the posture behind every AI initiative — reactive optimizer, anticipator, logic shifter, field reshaper — and push it deeper.
6. Don't mistake speed or experimentation for direction; both tether you to the old system's logic.
7. The strongest position isn't anticipating the game or even changing its logic — it's forcing a change in the structure of the playing field.
