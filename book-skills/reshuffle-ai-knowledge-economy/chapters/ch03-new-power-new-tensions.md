# Chapter 3: New Power, New Tensions — Why tools that promise progress also concentrate control

## Core Idea
Technology grows the pie and re-slices it at the same time: whoever controls the mechanisms of coordination captures a disproportionate share of the new value. AI is distinctive because it enables coordination without requiring consensus or enforced integration — and because it learns continuously, compounding that control.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Five Factors of Coordination Power (representation, decision-making, execution, composition, governance)**: Complex systems are sliced not by controlling every point but by designing the mechanisms of coordination. Representation power is the power to define what is seen and measured; decision power is the ability to choose what happens; execution power is the right to determine who carries out what action; composition power sets how the parts fit together (including how a job is bundled); governance power decides what counts as good performance and on whose terms actors participate.
  - When to use: to diagnose who actually holds power in any industry, platform, or job after a technology lands — and to locate where your own leverage sits.
  - How: for the system in question, ask (1) whose map of reality is authoritative? (2) who decides? (3) who executes and under whose trigger? (4) who defines the bundle of tasks/parts? (5) who sets the metrics and rules of participation. The actor holding the most of these captures the growing pie.
  - Why it works: value in complex systems cannot be allocated by top-down command (Gosplan's nails and one-size shoes); it must be shaped by designing relationships and interdependencies that keep functioning after the lines are drawn.

- **The Four Quadrants of Value Distribution (pie growth x pie slicing)**: Luddite fallacy (fixed pie, jobs lost); techno-optimist fantasy (growing pie, shared by all); task-based misdirection (fixed pie, jobs merely reshaped as humans supervise AI); and rebundling (growing pie, unevenly sliced) — the only quadrant that treats AI as the engine of a new system.
  - When to use: to place any AI argument you encounter and check which assumption it is smuggling in.
  - How: ask two questions of the claim — does it assume the pie is fixed, and does it assume the slices follow the growth? Three of four quadrants run on task-centric framing.
  - Failure mode: living in the bottom half ("will jobs vanish? will productivity rise?") means never asking who defines the new rules and who holds the knife.

- **Three Levels of Tension**: AI generates tensions at the task level (workers vs. their tools), the organizational system level (centralization vs. decentralization of decision power), and the competitive ecosystem level (capability users vs. tool providers, firms vs. customers, big platforms vs. AI-empowered individuals).
  - When to use: when scoping the impact of an AI deployment beyond headcount.
  - How: map each level separately; solutions that optimize one level (labor policy, profit, customer delight) reliably damage another.

- **Three Orders of Effect**: first-order automation effects, cascading coordination effects, and accelerating learning effects, where AI shapes the system's behavior and the changed behavior further trains AI.

## Key Concepts
- **Representation power**: the power to define what is seen and measured; the starting point of coordination, because systems can only act on what they can see.
- **Coordination without consensus**: AI's ability to align actors without prior agreement on standards or forced systems integration — unlike the shipping container or Walmart's barcode mandates.
- **Barcode-native architecture**: a system rebuilt around a new technology's logic rather than bolting the technology onto the inherited architecture.
- **Size vs. scale**: size is store count; scale is coordinated size, where each added unit strengthens the whole.
- **Algorithmic management**: coordination systems that assign, price, and evaluate work, rewarding only what they can represent.
- **Representation determines reward**: if the platform cannot measure it, it does not get compensated.
- **Learning loop**: AI's continuous feedback cycle — unlike a paper map or a barcode, the representation itself updates from use and in turn shapes behavior.
- **Reshuffle**: the emergence of new winners and losers not despite a growing pie but because of it.

## Mental Models
- Think of AI as a coordination technology first and an automation technology second — the barcode did not win by scanning faster, it won by re-architecting who decides.
- Use the paper map vs. Google Maps contrast when explaining why AI differs from prior coordination tech: fixed representation vs. one that learns from and steers the actors it represents.
- Think of a platform's metrics as the boundary of your economic existence: judgment, care, and reliability under pressure that the system cannot see are effectively unpaid.
- Use "grow the pie" and "slice the pie" as two separate questions; conflating them is what makes both the doomer and the optimist wrong.

## Anti-patterns
- **Adopting the tool into the old architecture**: Kmart captured the same barcode data as Walmart but kept fragmented purchasing; Kodak went digital, Barnes & Noble had an e-reader. Technology creates no advantage; coordination does.
- **The "better tools" fallacy**: attributing dominance to superior technology or execution rather than to the reorganization of the system and its power relationships.
- **Top-down slicing**: Gosplan-style targets produce nails made to weight and shoes in one size — precise lines drawn over interdependencies nobody redesigned.
- **Judging AI's impact by tasks alone**: task framing hides the ecosystem tensions where the real reallocation of power happens.

## Worked Example
The Climate Corporation started as weather prediction for farmers, then built a digital representation of the farm from satellite imagery, weather, soil moisture, and yield history. Farmers gained real diagnostics — nutrient deficiency and disease risk, better irrigation calls — so they opted in without any contractual mandate. That representation, though, defined what "good farming" meant on Climate's terms. Growers outside the platform, and traditional cooperatives, lost ground; insurers and equipment partners who could act on the new map gained. After Monsanto (now Bayer) acquired Climate, recommendations were tuned toward Bayer's own product portfolio. Seed suppliers, insurers, and machinery makers began building to Climate's standards, and each new participant made the platform harder to avoid. Its drought and yield forecasts now influence agricultural insurance pricing. Climate moved from a tool that helps farmers decide to a system that defines what counts as good practice — coordination power without any traditional market power.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask not "does this tool make us faster?" but "does this tool change who decides?"
2. Audit any system across the five factors — representation, decision, execution, composition, governance — before assuming where power sits.
3. Build a representation others must consult; whoever holds the authoritative map of reality sets the frame for everything downstream.
4. Distinguish size from scale: added units only create power when they feed a shared coordination layer.
5. Deliver value before integration. AI-enabled coordination attracts participants by giving them something first, then deepening lock-in as they opt in.
6. Expect new tensions, not just new efficiency — between workers and tools, headquarters and edges, firms and their tool providers, producers and customers.
7. Watch your tool providers: Uber depended on Google Maps and later faced Waymo; AI vendors to accounting, legal, and consulting firms are on the same path.
