# Chapter 10: Designing for Indecision — How companies turn customer confusion into competitive moats in the age of AI

## Core Idea
When choice is abundant and journeys are fragmented, guidance is scarce: whoever absorbs the customer's decision friction wins the right to serve them, converts that into a control point, and uses it to rebundle the ecosystem on their own terms.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Simplify decisions → establish a control point → rebundle the ecosystem**: Three sequential principles. Simplifying decisions gives you the right to play, not the right to win. Owning the high-friction, high-anxiety decision creates a control point. The control point then lets you reorganize complementary services and tax partners for access.
  - When to use: Competing in a fragmented, high-choice category, especially against a cheaper or more convenient rival.
  - How: Find the step in the journey where customers are most overwhelmed; own it with expertise, tooling, or data; use that foothold to integrate adjacent steps (selection, installation, usage, support); then set the rules of engagement for partners who need access to those customers.
  - Why it works: A control point is created by solving a high-friction decision — reducing complexity earns trust, locks in customers, and creates leverage over adjacent players. Failure mode: winning one step but never integrating the journey, so the moment stays a favor rather than a position.
- **Three types of control points in the customer journey**: Relationship-based (trust-based human relationships, e.g. salon stylists, local car dealers); workflow-based (embedded in critical processes, e.g. Figma owning design collaboration); intelligence-based (guiding decisions through data and recommendations, e.g. Google Maps, TripAdvisor).
  - When to use: Choosing where to plant your own control point, or diagnosing which incumbent control point AI can dismantle.
  - How: AI is the lever for the intelligence-based type — deploy it first as support inside an existing relationship or workflow, accumulate the usage data, then let the intelligence layer become the center of gravity.
- **Complexity x risk: how to deploy AI assistance**: Complex + low risk (choosing a recipe) → AI can face the consumer directly. Complex + high risk (finance, health, diagnosis) → embed AI in expert workflows so the human catches errors and carries the liability for the final recommendation.
  - When to use: Deciding whether AI assistance goes direct-to-consumer or through intermediaries.
  - How: Route through intermediaries while accuracy matures; shift to direct as reliability rises and risk falls.
- **Direct vs. derived demand**: Direct demand is the primary, emotional, aspirational need (beauty, confidence, a home); derived demand is the product bought to satisfy it (foundation, a mortgage). Companies that own direct demand dictate terms to those competing in derived demand.

## Key Concepts
- **Decision friction**: The cognitive effort a customer spends evaluating options — the thing a decision-support provider absorbs to earn trust.
- **Decision support**: Helping customers make confident, informed, context-aware choices; a durable source of competitive advantage in a high-choice world.
- **Right to serve**: The contested prize; whoever wins it disintermediates other relationships in the ecosystem.
- **Right to rebundle**: The license, earned via a control point, to reassemble adjacent steps and partner capabilities around the customer.
- **Customer journey fragmentation**: The smartphone-era scattering of decision-making across reviews, video, social feeds, and stores, which intensifies competition for the customer.
- **Showrooming**: Customers evaluating in-store then buying cheaper online — a threat convertible into an advantage once you hold the decision moment.
- **Price parity vs. price competition**: Price competition is what you reach for when locked out of the journey and it trains customers to see you as a commodity; parity is how you monetize a journey you're already in.
- **Platform tax**: Premium placement fees, revenue sharing, and training subsidies extracted from partners who need access to your influence over customer decisions.
- **Trojan horse AI assistant**: A manufacturer's AI that first helps resellers, then accumulates their customer data and shifts control upstream.
- **Credential wallet**: Learner-owned, portable store of micro-credentials and outcomes that makes lifelong-learning coordination possible.

## Mental Models
- Use decision support as your entry point when the category is complex and overwhelming — abundance of choice is your opening, not your enemy.
- Think of a control point as the high-anxiety moment in a journey, not as a channel or a piece of shelf space.
- Use intermediaries as a liability buffer when the AI isn't yet reliable enough to be wrong in public.
- Think of D2C as a channel play and an ecosystem as a system play: no single brand can outcompete an ecosystem.

## Anti-patterns
- **Racing to the bottom on price**: A desperate attempt to buy into a journey you don't own; it commoditizes you (Circuit City, RadioShack cutting staff and margins).
- **Over-engineering the journey**: Stitching together discrete steps instead of designing environments where the desired action happens naturally; rebundling works best when it doesn't feel engineered.
- **Misreading an ecosystem play as a channel play**: The client who read Sephora as retail and answered with D2C, then burned cash on customer acquisition while Glossier came back to Sephora's shelves.
- **Deploying AI direct-to-consumer on high-risk decisions**: No expert catches the error; the fallout is immediate, viral, and trust once lost is hard to regain.
- **Treating decision support as the whole answer**: Beneath every decision sits a coordination problem; without it, even a smart assistant navigates with a broken map.

## Worked Example
Best Buy in 2012 lost $1.7 billion in a quarter and looked next in line after Circuit City. Amazon was undercutting on price and showrooming was turning stores into free display space. Instead of matching prices, Best Buy noticed the one thing e-commerce couldn't do: help a shopper who cannot tell which OLED refresh rate or smart-home system is right for them. It reinvested in staff training and turned stores into decision-support hubs staffed by advisors — exactly as rivals were replacing experienced workers with cheaper ones. It then closed the loop with a price-parity guarantee: once you decided in-store, you paid no more than elsewhere, so conversion cost almost nothing extra. Owning both the decision and the transaction made brands like Apple and Samsung dependent on it; they subsidized product training and paid for premium placement. Best Buy set display, explanation, and selling rules, and its stock rose roughly 10x from the 2012 lows.

## Key Takeaways
1. Find the highest-friction decision in your customer's journey and own it before you compete on price or features.
2. Simplifying decisions only earns the right to play; convert it into a control point or a rival will.
3. Once you hold the decision, use parity — not discounting — to capture the transaction.
4. Rebundle outward from the control point: selection, installation, usage, support, then partner access.
5. Charge partners for access to your influence over decisions; that's where the margin sits.
6. Choose your AI deployment mode by complexity x risk; put experts in the loop while accuracy matures, and let liability sit with them.
7. Compete for direct demand; owning it lets you dictate terms to everyone selling derived demand.
8. Pick your control-point type deliberately — relationship, workflow, or intelligence — and use AI to convert fragmented relationships into a centralized intelligence layer.
