# Glossary — Reshuffle (Sangeet Paul Choudary)

Terms as the author defines them. Chapter attribution in parentheses.

## A
**Above-the-Algorithm vs. Below-the-Algorithm** — Above: workers who use or build algorithmic systems for leverage. Below: workers managed by them, with little autonomy or differentiation. (Ch 3)
**Accessibility** — How easy it is for a customer to adopt and pay for a solution. (Ch 9)
**Agentic Execution** — Autonomous goal-driven agents that monitor systems, detect intent, and act across workflows without human prompting. (Ch 6)
**AI as a Tool vs. AI as an Engine** — Tool: optimizes tasks inside the existing model; doesn't change the basis of competition. Engine: drives core system performance and defines how the business operates. (Ch 12)
**AI as Coordination Infrastructure** — AI's transformational role is not automation but extending coordination into previously uncoordinated domains by structuring ambiguity and translating tacit cues. (Ch 6)
**AI-Native Firm** — An organization designed from the ground up around AI in core workflows and knowledge distribution, unlike incumbents layering AI on old systems. (Ch 6)
**Algorithmic Management** — Using algorithms to allocate, supervise, and evaluate work; compresses wages, standardizes output, strips decision control from workers. (Ch 3)
**Assistive Execution** — AI-supported workflows where humans still decide; the bridge to agentic workflows. (Ch 6)
**Autonomy-Coordination Trade-off** — The assumption that more team autonomy means less alignment. AI dissolves the trade-off. (Ch 6)

## B
**Barcode-Native Architecture** — An organization built to fully exploit a coordination technology's data (Walmart with barcodes), not merely to adopt it. (Ch 1)
**Behavior Graph** — Preferences inferred from observed actions; replaces the social graph in AI-native experiences (TikTok). (Ch 10, 12)
**Building Block** — A discrete, modular capability that can be rented, reused, and recombined into a larger system. (Ch 7)
**Building Block Economy** — An economy of rentable modular capabilities; success depends on how well you rebundle them and manage constraints between them. (Ch 7)

## C
**Clockspeed** — The relative pace of innovation across parts of a value chain; AI tools evolve faster than the businesses adopting them. (Ch 8)
**Cold Start Problem** — Participants won't join until value is visible, but value needs participants. (Ch 11, 12)
**Collapse of Differentiation** — When AI makes everyone's output look alike, expertise stops commanding a premium. (Ch 4)
**Composable Business** — A business that assembles third-party capabilities rather than building them. (Ch 7)
**Composition Power** — Control over how components and actors plug into a system. (Ch 1)
**Constraints** — System bottlenecks limiting performance: scarcity-based, risk-based, or coordination-based. (Ch 5)
**Constraint Management** — Identifying and designing around system limits to secure consistent performance and defensibility. (Ch 9, 12)
**Contextual Value** — The value a role holds because it resolves a critical constraint in a specific system — not because the skill is rare. (Ch 5)
**Control Point** — A chokehold others must operate through to create or deliver value; power by indispensability, not ownership. (Ch 11, 12)
**Coordination** — Aligning independent agents toward a shared outcome under fragmentation, ambiguity, or misaligned incentives. (Ch 1)
**Coordination Gap** — The space between what algorithms can coordinate (codified workflows) and what economic activity actually requires (judgment, context, tacit knowledge). (Ch 1)
**Coordination Paradox** — Narrow local automation fragments the organization and raises, not lowers, coordination costs. (Ch 6)
**Coordination Tax** — The hidden, exponentially scaling cost of keeping teams aligned: meetings, messaging, searching, manual handoffs. (Ch 6)
**Coordination Technology** — Technology that aligns fragmented system parts — barcodes, containers, and now AI. (Ch 1)
**Coordination Without Consensus** — AI-enabled coordination where actors need no prior agreement on formats or rules; AI interprets diverse inputs into shared understanding. (Ch 11)
**Curation** — Elevating relevant signal from noise and shaping context and narrative; rises in value as information commoditizes. (Ch 5)
**Curiosity** — The disciplined ability to frame high-leverage questions; an economic asset when answers are cheap. (Ch 5)

## D
**Data Flywheel** — More usage → more data → better system → more usage. (Ch 11)
**Decision Friction** — The cognitive burden or uncertainty that stops customers choosing confidently. (Ch 10)
**Dependence vs. Dominance** — Ecosystem control now comes from being depended on, not from ownership or lock-in. (Ch 11)
**Demand-Side / Supply-Side Control Point** — Power from controlling customer decisions vs. power from coordinating how participants deliver. (Ch 10, 11)

## E
**Economic Collapse of the Task** — A task still gets done but no longer commands a premium because AI made it cheap. (Ch 2, 4)
**Economic vs. Contextual Displacement** — A task can become cheaper (economic) or lose importance in the workflow (contextual). Either devalues the job. (Ch 4)
**Economic Visibility** — Whether the system recognizes and rewards a role's contribution; contextual value must be made visible to be captured. (Ch 5)
**Ecosystem Flywheel** — AI coordination attracts participants → more data → better coordination → more participants. (Ch 11)
**Engine** — The component that determines system performance; not replaceable without re-architecting. (Ch 8)
**Execution Bias** — Acting fast because execution is cheap, at the expense of strategic intent. (Ch 12)
**Exponential Systems** — One coordination breakthrough (containers, APIs) triggers layers of new capabilities, restructuring industries rather than just speeding outputs. (Ch 1)

## F–H
**Framing Error** — Solving a problem with an obsolete mental model while the underlying structure has changed. (Ch 2)
**Governance Power** — The ability to set and enforce rules of participation. (Ch 3, 11)
**Human-in-the-Loop Illusion** — Believing humans stay central by supervising AI, while in fact being reduced to support roles in systems that make the decisions. (Ch 3)

## I–L
**Intrinsic / Economic / Contextual Value** — A task can be meaningful, paid for, or workflow-critical; these come apart. (Ch 4)
**Judgment** — Making accountable decisions under uncertainty; gains value as AI outputs turn probabilistic. (Ch 5)
**Knowledge as Capital** — Knowledge as a rentable, scalable, recombinable asset rather than labor. (Ch 4, 7)
**Learning Loop** — AI improves from use, so unlike barcodes it changes the system as it learns from it. (Ch 1)
**Leverage** — Amplifying output without proportional input, via rented capabilities, AI, and platform orchestration. (Ch 7)
**Local Optimization, Systemic Fragility** — Isolated improvements in one part increase misalignment with the rest. (Ch 8)

## M–P
**Modularity** — Discrete interchangeable components combinable into larger systems. (Ch 7)
**Narrative Control** — Power to shape meaning and action through framing and curation. (Ch 3, 5)
**Network Capital** — Distributed audience reach that enables outsized impact without infrastructure. (Ch 5)
**Ownership of Outcomes** — Accepting liability, which demands representation (visibility), integration (decision-making), and execution (delivery). (Ch 9)
**Performance Leverage** — Leverage from owning a superior critical component (engine). (Ch 8)
**Performance-Based Lock-In** — Dependence created by superior component performance, not contracts. (Ch 8)
**Pie-Slicing vs. Pie-Growing** — Technology grows the pie but also reshapes who slices it; AI concentrates coordination power. (Ch 3)
**Platform Tax** — Fees or concessions an orchestrator extracts for access to decision moments or customers. (Ch 10)
**Positive Constraint** — A deliberately chosen boundary or design rule that improves coordination and creates differentiation, preserving judgment or diversity that pure speed would sacrifice. (Ch 9, 12)

## R
**Rebundling** — Reassembling unbundled tasks and capabilities into new roles, workflows, organizations, or industry architectures around new constraints. (Ch 5, 6, 7)
**Reliability** — How consistently a solution performs in the field under real-world constraints. (Ch 9)
**Reliability Leverage** — Leverage from taking responsibility for consistent outcomes in unpredictable environments. (Ch 8, 9)
**Representation Power** — The power to define what is visible and measurable, and thus to set the frame for decisions. (Ch 3, 11)
**Reskilling Fallacy** — Believing new skills alone protect you when the system no longer needs human execution of the task. (Ch 4, 5)
**Right to Coordinate** — The earned ability to organize an ecosystem by removing friction in both decisions and execution; requires deep integration, not a nicer UI. (Ch 11)
**Risk Absorption** — Internalizing technical, financial, or operational failure risk so the customer doesn't bear it. (Ch 9)

## S
**Scarcity- / Risk- / Coordination-Based Constraints** — The three constraint types that generate role value: limited access; high-consequence errors; misaligned moving parts. (Ch 5)
**Showrooming** — Customers explore in-store and buy online. (Ch 10)
**Skill Premium** — Extra income from a rare or difficult skill; shrinks when AI lets less-skilled workers match output. (Ch 4)
**Skill-Biased Technological Change** — The claim that technology rewards the highly skilled; breaks down when AI narrows the high/low performance gap. (Ch 4)
**Solution** — A complete offering delivering reliable outcomes by managing constraints, integrating complements, and absorbing risk. (Ch 8, 9)
**Solution Bundle** — Tools, services, financing, and guarantees that together make performance predictable. (Ch 9)
**Standardization Risk** — The more standardized and measurable the work, the easier for tools and insurers to absorb its performance and liability. (Ch 9)
**System Builder** — A firm that reengineers the system around a tool — financing, operations, integration, risk — not just the tool. (Ch 9)
**System-Aware Adaptation** — Tracking where constraints move as systems evolve and repositioning to solve new frictions, instead of chasing skills. (Ch 5)
**System-Centric vs. Task-Centric View** — Analyzing how technology reshapes the architecture of work systems vs. evaluating task-by-task substitution. (Ch 2)

## T–W
**Tacit Knowledge** — Expertise in intuition and experience, hard to codify but essential under uncertainty. (Ch 5, 6)
**Three-Circle Model** — The task-level job nested inside the organizational system, nested inside the competitive ecosystem — AI reshapes all three. (Ch 2)
**Tool Provider vs. Solution Provider** — Supplies functional components vs. integrates and is accountable for outcomes. (Ch 8)
**Typist Effect** — Job obsolescence caused by system redesign rather than task automation: the task survives, the logic that justified the job does not. (Ch 2)
**Unbundling** — Breaking a job, product, or workflow into components once old constraints are removed; separates expertise from labor. (Ch 4)
**Unified Representation / Decision / Execution** — AI produces a shared view, aligned decisions, and coordinated follow-through across actors using different tools. (Ch 6, 11)
**Vertical Encroachment** — A tool provider moving into the solution layer and absorbing the customer experience. (Ch 8)
**Vibe Coding** — Karpathy's term for outputs that look impressive but lack rigor; symptom of performative execution. (Ch 12)
**Wrapper** — A solution provider reduced to an interface over someone else's engine: controls appearance, not substance. (Ch 8)
**Work- / Results- / Outcomes-as-a-Service** — Paid for operational delivery, for measurable results, or for customer-specific strategic outcomes — each step deepens integration and liability. (Ch 9)
