# Chapter 14: Commitment

## Core Idea
Commitment — the fifth and final stage of trust building (not selling) — is ensuring the client fully understands what solving the problem will require and is genuinely willing to do it. It is emotional obligation, not just an action plan, and trust deepens through the advisor's candor about risks, barriers, and true requirements.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Commitment as emotional state, not just action**: The dictionary's two meanings — (1) a pledge to *do* something, and (2) the state of *being* obligated or emotionally impelled. Trust lives in the second.
  - When to use: After the problem is framed and the vision is clear.
  - How, the commitment conversations: What will get in the way? What do we intend to do about it? Who's brought into the loop? Who does what part? What information do we need? When do we check in? What are the key deadlines?
- **"Buckling in for the ride"**: Testing the client's resolve openly.
  - How: "Let me test your resolve for committing to this course of action. Let's make sure we understand what will be required of each of us and where contingencies may arise. Let me play devil's advocate and try to convince you *not* to do all the things we just agreed to do."
- **Managing Expectations checklist**: 1) articulate what *we* will and won't do; 2) articulate what the *client* will and won't do; 3) define analysis boundaries; 4) check off-limits areas/people; 5) identify working arrangements; 6) agree communication methods and frequency; 7–9) decide who gets which reports, how often, how used; 10) set milestones/progress reviews; 11) decide how success is measured, at the end and during.
- **Joint Commitment**: Trust-based commitment differs from action planning by being *joint* and *personal* — two-sided and irreplaceable, not parallel workload division.
  - How: Link the client's tasks to their other divisions/people, propose deepening content and personal linkages, and tie in the client's own personal growth.

## Key Concepts
- **Commitment vs. opinion**: Without it, advice is just "You should do this!" / "Yeah, I should! Thanks! Good-bye!"
- **Self-protection in candor**: If clients aren't committed to do what it takes, they won't benefit and you'll have failed — even if everything you did was right.
- **Two reasons clients commit**: Pain/energy around a topic, or captivation by something new and appealing — pain relief drives roughly 80% of commitment.
- **Clients commit to slightly less than they could**: A defense mechanism — they need room to maneuver for the unexpected, which always happens.
- **"We-not-me" orientation**: Showing your full work plan, offering choices (thorough vs. quick-and-dirty), being open about challenges — all signal joint ownership.
- **As-is to to-be**: Commitment connects the abstract envisioned future to concrete "who, what, when."

## Mental Models
- Think of "no problem, leave it to us" not as confidence but as a red flag — it's often read as arrogance or secrecy ("What's he hiding?").
- When commitment stalls, back up one or two steps in the trust process — resistance usually means an earlier stage (often envisioning) was inadequate.
- Use "who, what, when" as the most powerful guide to generating concrete commitment.
- Treat managing expectations' picayune details as trust-builders — they prove you're serving the client the way they wish to be served.

## Anti-patterns
- **Omitting risks and pitfalls**: Skipping discussion of risks, uncertainties, and downsides — especially while winning the work — leaves the client feeling you were less than open when foreseeable pitfalls hit.
- **Over-promising to get the work**: Saying yes to work completable only with great personal pain. "It's not worth it" (repeated three times for emphasis).
- **Parallel, impersonal commitment**: An action split where the advisor could be swapped for anyone else adds no unique value.
- **Confusing commitment with closing the sale**: Commitment is a trust-building stage about the client's resolve, not a contract signature.

## Worked Example
**The 700-store retailer** shows backing up to envisioning to unblock commitment. Every strategy discussion started with one global vision statement and ended with someone objecting that store #327 didn't fit. Charlie's team proposed a basic segmentation of three store types — but still couldn't get commitment even to noncontroversial implications. The breakthrough: assign each store to one of the three types and run a composite profit-and-loss statement. Suddenly results were quantified; all but a dozen stores categorized cleanly, statistically isolating the historic disagreement. The P&Ls were radically different — the most profitable group had been the most shunned (highest shrinkage). Commitment blossomed; actions in real estate and merchandising followed. The lesson: the client needed more envisioning, and a familiar concrete descriptor (store income statements) let them envision an alternate reality — unblocking commitment. **When commitment is in doubt, back up one or two steps.**

**Joint vs. parallel commitment (Myra, Joe, Andy).** In the low-trust version, advisor Andy simply accepts a task split and offers background material — replaceable by anyone. In the trust-based version, Andy proposes involving colleague Judy for a complementary banking perspective, offers to connect Joe's metrics work to Bill Y. at the client's ABC division, and reminds Myra of her own goal to delegate and spend more time externally ("Isn't this a case in point?"). The commitment becomes two-sided, personal, and irreplaceable.

## Key Takeaways
1. Commitment is the final trust-building stage — emotional obligation to do what it takes, not just closing a sale.
2. Lay out all risks, barriers, and true requirements candidly up front; candor is the trust-builder and your self-protection.
3. Pain/energy drives ~80% of client commitment; know whether you're offering pain relief or inspiration.
4. Manage expectations in exhaustive detail (both sides' roles, boundaries, reports, milestones, success measures) — the picayune details prove you're serving them their way.
5. Make commitment joint and personal, not parallel — connect it to other work, people, and the client's own growth.
6. When commitment stalls, back up one or two steps (often to envisioning); name and claim fear or complacency.
