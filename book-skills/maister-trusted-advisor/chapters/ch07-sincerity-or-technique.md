# Chapter 7: Sincerity or Technique?

## Core Idea
Techniques and sincerity are not opposites — clients can only observe sincerity through external behaviors, so caring and technique work together. But occasional tactics inconsistent with how you normally behave will be spotted as phony; the only durable path is to genuinely care (or to consistently act your way into caring). "The trick of earning trust is to avoid all tricks."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Inside-Out and Outside-In (work from both ends to the middle)**: You can start from caring (inside) and let action-behaviors fall on fertile ground, *or* start from new external behaviors (outside) that open you to fresh information and focus on the client. Both are valid; "we are generally better off working from both ends to the middle."
  - When to use: When wrestling with whether to adopt trust behaviors you don't yet feel.
  - How: If you already care, practice the behaviors that show it. If not fully there, adopt caring behaviors consistently — "you can act your way into right thinking better than you can think your way into right acting."
- **Four Options When You Don't Care for a Client**: (1) Keep serving, don't build the relationship; (2) Keep serving, apply trust techniques without sincerity; (3) Pass the client to a colleague; (4) Resign the account.
  - When to use: After genuinely trying and failing to connect with a client.
  - How: Option 1 makes everyone a loser (drop it); Option 2 is worth trying for a while but not forever; Option 3 is attractive when possible (a non-fit for you may be a dream client for a colleague); Option 4 is the ultimate step — "Reputation before revenue!"
- **Three Pieces of Advice for the Junior/Team Member** (who can't resign or reassign): (1) Check your motives — have you truly tried everything, or are you avoiding some other unpleasantness? (2) Put it in perspective — if not life-, career-, or client-threatening, how long must you live with it? (3) If real and material, raise it directly with the senior engagement manager.

## Key Concepts
- **Sincerity is inferred from behavior**: Sincerity is about intentions, assumed to come from within — but clients infer it only from external behaviors (attention paid, interest shown, advance work, empathetic listening).
- **"Manipulate" without being manipulative**: Consciously using a technique isn't manipulation if you genuinely care; the loaded words are needlessly loaded.
- **Consistency is the test**: What fails is *occasional* tactics inconsistent with your normal behavior. If you always act caringly, the distinction between "faking it" and genuine becomes academic — indistinguishable from the real thing.
- **You fool yourself too**: "When I fool the people, I find, I fool myself as well" — faking has costs to the faker.
- **No win-lose in relationships**: There are only win-wins and lose-loses; if the fit fails for one party, it ultimately fails for both — like couples who divorce.
- **Client or friend?**: You need not befriend every client, but you *do* have to care to be a trusted advisor (a mere vendor doesn't). It's unprofessional *not* to show interest.
- **Socializing vs. sociable**: Trust isn't earned on golf courses or at dinners; socializing is optional, but being *sociable* — a window into the client as a person — is necessary.
- **Reputation before revenue**: Serving clients you're not engaged with damages your reputation longer than the cash penalty of walking away.

## Mental Models
- Treat sincerity and technique as complementary, not opposed — "start from within, or without? The only right answer is yes to both."
- Use "fake it till you make it" *only* as a permanent commitment: there's no point faking unless you'll keep it up for the whole relationship — at which point it's real.
- Apply the "common denominator" check: "I was amazed at how many fools I ran into until I noticed the common denominator: me."
- Bet on the journey, not the destination: clients (like romantic partners) detect whether you want them as a means to an end or as a fellow-voyager.

## Anti-patterns
- **Occasional inconsistent tactics**: Isolated caring gestures that clash with your normal behavior are read as phony, insincere, and clumsy — worse than doing nothing.
- **Option 1 (serve but don't connect)**: Forfeits all trust benefits and yields an unfulfilling work life — everyone loses.
- **Faking it indefinitely with no intent to make it real**: "We can imagine nothing worse than to spend our whole lives faking it."
- **False friendship**: Clients see through friendships built on golf, football, and small talk; interest must be genuine.
- **Bailing when times get tough**: The hallmark of trusted advisors is that they *don't* bail out during the rough, cyclical patches of a relationship.

## Worked Example
**Jim and the Polaroid camera.** Jim, a self-made man who teaches a business-school course he doesn't need the money for, brings a Polaroid camera and a white sheet to the first class each semester and photographs all 45 students. He promises to memorize every face, name, undergraduate school, and company by the next class — and he stresses he is *not* naturally good with names. He spends the week working through flash cards. At the next class everyone shows up to see if he can do it. He does; he dazzles; his no-show and dropout rates are zero. Is it premeditated? Absolutely. Is it sincere or manipulative? The authors call those needlessly loaded words: Jim is consciously using a visible technique *and* is plainly sincere — why else spend hours of valuable time memorizing names for a course he teaches for little pay? The lesson: technique and caring are not in conflict. (Contrast: **Rob and Arnold** — after Arnold left the firm, Rob liked the successors and did good work, but "his heart really wasn't in it"; the relationship gradually weakened. Without true feeling, tactics lose their power — even for someone who teaches this material.)

## Key Takeaways
1. Sincerity is inferred from behavior — so caring and technique reinforce each other, not compete.
2. Work from both ends to the middle: if you care, show it; if you don't yet, act your way toward it — consistently.
3. Consistency is everything; occasional out-of-character tactics get spotted and backfire.
4. To be a trusted advisor you genuinely have to care; a vendor doesn't. It's unprofessional *not* to show interest.
5. When you can't connect, use the four options — but avoid Option 1, and remember "Reputation before revenue."
6. Relationships are win-win or lose-lose; a bad fit ultimately fails both parties, so name it honestly and kindly.
7. Be sociable, not necessarily socializing; bet on the long-term journey and don't bail when times get tough.
