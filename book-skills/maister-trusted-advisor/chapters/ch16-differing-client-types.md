# Chapter 16: Differing Client Types

## Core Idea
Clients are not interchangeable; effective advisors diagnose each client's motivation, personality, and organizational context, then adapt style (without faking) to deal with the person the way that person wants to be dealt with.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Guiding Principles for Differing Clients**: A set of pre-engagement disciplines — (1) work out in advance what's different about this client and about you in this situation; (2) ask the three questions; (3) rank the client's prevailing motivation; (4) find why you genuinely like this client; (5) use the trust equation to read them.
  - When to use: Before and during any client engagement, especially unfamiliar or recurring ones.
  - How: Take one lesson each from your most and least successful past clients into every visit; ask delicate advance questions (delicate topics, divided views, likely resistance, competing initiatives).
- **The Three Diagnostic Questions**: (1) What is the client's prevailing personal motivation? (2) What is their personality? (3) How does the state of their organization affect their worldview?
  - When to use: Sizing up any client before adapting your approach.
  - How: After answering, ask "How do I adapt my style to deal with this person as he/she likes to be dealt with?"
- **Four Motivational Drives**: The need to excel; the need to take action/achieve results; the need to understand and analyze before deciding; the need to drive consensus.
  - When to use: Tailoring the conversation's target outcome.
  - How: Rank which comes first for the client and aim the interaction at excellence, action, analysis, or consensus accordingly.
- **Nine Difficult Client Archetypes**: Named behavioral patterns with a scripted diagnosis and response for each.
  - When to use: When a client's surface behavior is throwing you off.
  - How: Match the pattern, read the underlying need, respond in the client's language.

## Key Concepts
- **Entry factors**: For many clients, credibility and reliability must be satisfied first before they'll engage on intimacy and self-orientation.
- **Leading the client**: Clients comfortable only in the objective realm may need to be led toward intimacy/self-orientation; not leading isn't the same as their not needing to go there.
- **Good acting (not lying)**: Focusing on the one facet of your own personality the role needs and suppressing the others — find the genuine part of you that can empathize.
- **Taking the measure of the organization**: The same individual can behave very differently depending on organizational "wind"; role changes can look like personality changes.
- **Structured envisioning fit**: Works both for deductive/skeptical/critical thinkers (firms up benefits) and for creative blue-sky thinkers (frees imagination).
- **Naming and claiming**: Openly naming an unspoken issue in a private conversation — the core tool for the "Let Me Handle That" and "Oh, By the Way" clients.

## Mental Models
- Use "while every kettle may have a cover, one size doesn't fit all" — match personality and pace, don't standardize.
- Think of a difficult client's surface content as a mask over a deeper need (to be understood, to feel special, to control, to feel secure).
- Use "if nothing about them reminds you of someone you like, that's a big hint this isn't the right client for you" as a self-selection filter.
- Think of political-obsessed clients by making politics a *content issue* — discuss it openly with agenda, pros, and cons.

## Anti-patterns
- **Overgeneralizing that all clients are alike**: Tempting, dangerous, and wrong; it blinds you to the differences that matter.
- **Assuming familiarity means you're briefed (the David retreat error)**: Skipping advance diagnostic questions because you "know" the firm — then getting blindsided by new divisions or divisions of opinion.
- **Calling a client's bluff (the "You're the Expert, Dummy" trap)**: Embarrasses both of you; instead give back value as a set of hypotheses.
- **Forcing an inarticulate client into your verbal comfort zone**: The "Just Like, You Know" client needs written options to rank, not more open-ended questions.

## Worked Example
The "Just the Facts, Ma'am" client (Type 1). He says: "Just give me the facts. Answer when asked. Don't sell me. Why is the price so damn high? I'm the boss here." The mistake is taking the content at face value and getting defensive about price. Diagnosis: this is a plea to be understood, sometimes masking a fear of being wrong or wronged. Response (clarify and confirm): speak in his language, affirm his values, then behave that way and re-check occasionally. Answer: "OK, I get it. You don't want to mess around with niceties. You want to get straight to the point, don't want to waste time, want me to have content every time we talk. That's the kind of person you are. Have I got it?" The move is to affirm and confirm the person, not argue the price.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose motivation, personality, and organizational context before adapting your approach.
2. It's the advisor's professional obligation — not the client's — to surface delicate topics and divisions in advance.
3. Adapt authentically: find the true part of yourself that fits the moment; never fake or misrepresent.
4. Difficult behaviors are masks over deeper needs; respond to the need, in the client's language, not to the surface content.
5. Use the trust equation to read which factors each client weights, and lead objective-realm clients toward intimacy.
6. If you can find nothing to genuinely like about a client, that's a signal to invest your trusted-advisor energy elsewhere.
