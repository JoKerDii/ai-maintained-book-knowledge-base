# Chapter 12: Framing the Issue

## Core Idea
Framing — the third stage — is crystallizing a client's complex issues and emotions into a clear problem definition that gives insight and a fresh way of thinking. It is the hardest, most rewarding stage because it fuses the rational and emotional, and an accurate problem statement is more than halfway to the solution.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rational Framing**: Distilling a complex set of issues down to a few critical variables.
  - When to use: To break the paralysis of too much complexity, which the mind can't process until someone simplifies.
  - How: Generate a list, draw a diagram, sketch a process, or apply a formal model. The value isn't a proprietary paradigm — it's leading *this* client with *this* problem to see the relevance of an old or new paradigm.
- **Emotional Framing**: Surfacing the hidden emotional or political "gut" issue that has stagnated a relationship, when the logjam can't be solved by rational insight.
  - When to use: When conversation stops flowing freely and stagnates ("the elephant in the parlor" — things everyone knows but no one says).
  - How: Take the personal risk to name the hidden emotion; remember you're framing the *client's* emotions, not your own.
- **Naming and Claiming**: The breakthrough of speaking what was previously "unsayable."
  - How, three factors: 1) acknowledge the difficulty of raising the issue; 2) accept responsibility for raising it; 3) directly state the issue itself.
- **Responsibility-taking caveats ("the Columbo technique")**: Judicious caveats that reduce personal risk while raising a hard issue.
  - How: String together caveats about how hard the issue is to raise until you've slightly overcompensated for the perceived risk, then say the thing that must be said. E.g., "It's probably just me, but…", "You've probably thought of this already, but…", "I'm not sure if I'm being inappropriate in bringing this up, but…"

## Key Concepts
- **Framing = crystallizing**: Identifying and enunciating the essence of the issues — usually something hidden, critical, fundamental, or all three.
- **Middle step**: The most brilliant frame falls on deaf ears if you haven't earned the right by engaging and listening first.
- **Framing and Blaming**: Most initial frames (especially clients') are laden with blame ("I can't get marketing to listen to me"); blame is a defense mechanism and just another form of self-orientation.
- **Blame-free problem statement**: By telling the truth and eliminating blame, the advisor maneuvers toward full ownership of a statement that can be acted upon and transcended.
- **Move from blame to responsibility**: The essence of emotional framing — and, paradoxically, naming-and-claiming reduces personal risk rather than increasing it.

## Mental Models
- Think of emotional framing as dynamiting a clogged stream — there are echoes of it in psychotherapy and confession, bringing forth what was left unsaid.
- Use "add more caveats" as a risk dial — feel more risk? add more caveats until you've slightly overshot the perceived risk.
- Treat the client's emotional signals (red face, white knuckles) as objective facts, not judgments about you.
- Don't over-protect proprietary models — there are few great truths; effectiveness lies in making an old paradigm relevant to this client.

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing clients buy your brilliance**: Strategy consultants over-rely on rational framing (their comfort zone) and miss the emotional/political logjam.
- **Covering up the client's emotion**: Joe's lost sale — when the CEO said sadly, "Joe, what are we gonna do about all those people?", Joe joked it away ("no problem… outplacement counselors… out of here in no time"). The meeting ended inconclusively, the restructuring stalled for years, and the eventual layoffs were 2–3x larger.
- **Suppressing the elephant**: Letting hidden emotional issues burst out only in stolen hallway conversations during coffee breaks.
- **Framing loaded with blame**: Problem statements that assign fault are not usable frames.

## Worked Example
**Ellen's Story** demonstrates naming and claiming. Ellen, an accounting-firm partner, had to deliver bad news to her client, the controller. As she began, "the client's face was getting red, and his knuckles were getting white." Instead of panicking ("There goes the account") and running for cover, she paused, took a deep breath, and said, "You look a little angry." Then she waited silently. The client shouted, "No, I'm not angry! … Well, I mean, not at you; I'm angry at our people… you shouldn't have to be the one to bring this news… I'm glad you've pointed it out." By treating the emotional signals as facts about the client rather than judgments about herself, Ellen surfaced the truth, let the client vent, and moved toward a joint solution — while deepening the bond. Had she gone with her own fears, she would have left wrongly believing the anger was directed at her.

David's executive-committee dialogue shows emotional framing of a political issue: noticing whispered side conversations and people shifting in their seats around enforcing a new partner standard, David called on a whisperer — "Fred, I'm concerned that we are not getting all of the issues out on the table" — surfacing the real, unspoken fear of confronting the firm's biggest rainmaker.

## Key Takeaways
1. Framing crystallizes complex issues and emotions into an insightful problem definition — often more than halfway to the solution.
2. There are two framings: rational (distill to few variables) and emotional (surface the hidden gut issue); the emotional is harder and more valuable.
3. Naming and claiming = acknowledge difficulty + accept responsibility + state the issue; it reduces, not increases, personal risk.
4. Use responsibility-taking (Columbo) caveats to dial down the risk of raising hard topics.
5. Eliminate blame — it's a form of self-orientation that blocks a usable, actionable frame.
6. You must earn the right to frame by first engaging and listening.
