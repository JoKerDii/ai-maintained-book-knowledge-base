# Chapter 3: Earning Trust

## Core Idea
Trust must be earned and deserved, not requested — you give before you get, keeping your self-interest in check. Trust has definable characteristics (it grows, is both rational and emotional, is two-way, entails risk, differs for client and advisor, and is personal) that tell you how to build it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Six Characteristics of Trust**: Trust (1) **grows** rather than appears — it accumulates over time, no quick fix; (2) is both **rational and emotional** — competence is necessary but not sufficient; (3) is a **two-way relationship** — the client must reciprocate, you can't force it; (4) is intrinsically about **perceived risk** — no risk, no trust; (5) is **different for the client than the advisor** — one trusts, one is trusted (like ballroom dancing, one leads, one follows); (6) is **personal** — we trust people, not institutions.
  - When to use: To diagnose why a relationship isn't building trust and to set realistic expectations.
  - How: Check each characteristic — Are you giving it time? Addressing emotion, not just logic? Selecting a client who will reciprocate? Willing to take a personal risk?
- **The Give-to-Get Principle**: Provide evidence of trustworthiness by giving (knowledge, help, honesty) freely and first, before being retained.
  - When to use: Early in a prospect relationship, before you're hired.
  - How: Offer genuinely useful help even when it lies outside your billable scope.

## Key Concepts
- **Earned and deserved**: Saying "Trust me!" backfires — it raises defenses; you must *do* something that gives the other person evidence.
- **Perceived risk**: If party B *couldn't* and *wouldn't* do otherwise, the relationship is about probabilities, not trust; the choice not to violate trust is what makes it special.
- **Rational + emotional**: Content-free advisors are rejected, but pure technical competence is only half the story.
- **Two-way / can't force it**: You may have to *select* carefully whom to build trust with; unilateral effort never adds up to trust.
- **Institutional trust is an oxymoron**: Brand and reputation get you on the short list; only a person keeps you there.
- **"It's business; it IS personal"**: The inversion of *The Godfather* — trust lives in human interactions, not credentials or ads.
- **Caring**: The most critical signal — evidence that the client's interests matter to you as much as your own.

## Mental Models
- Think of trust like ballroom dancing: roles differ, someone leads and someone follows — ambiguity collapses it into two solo exercises.
- Use "give in order to get" — the fastest way to earn business is to be generous with knowledge before any fee is on the table.
- When a technique "feels risky," treat that fear as confirmation you're on the trust-building path: no risk, no trust.
- Being able to trust is necessary (not sufficient) for being trustworthy — if you can't trust, you probably can't be trusted.

## Anti-patterns
- **Selling yourself/your firm**: Talking about your founding date, offices, and fees signals self-interest and lowers confidence.
- **Fudging credentials to win business**: Sacrifices credibility instantly (see Charlie/sandpaper) — "who would trust such a person?"
- **Saying "Trust me!"**: Directly requesting trust raises defenses.
- **Barraging busy clients**: A good advisor knows when to bother a client and when to leave them alone.

## Worked Example
**Charlie and the sandpaper.** Newly promoted, Charlie brought a senior partner to a sales call with an abrasives manufacturer. The client asked what experience the firm had doing marketing studies for "industrial consumables." Charlie's mind went blank, then realized the man meant sandpaper — and panicked, because the firm had done no such studies. His trained-consultant instinct was to fudge: "Not exactly, but we've done studies for products quite similar…." Before he could speak, the senior partner leaned in and said flatly, "None that I can think of," paused, then asked, "Given that, is there anything else that you think it would be helpful to discuss?" The subtext: *I will answer truthfully even if it costs us your business.* The client was unconcerned, asked about relevant adjacent experience, and the firm won the work. Charlie learned two lessons: trust turns on keeping self-interest in check, and it can be won or lost in a millisecond. (The chapter pairs this with David's lawyer who faxed a free how-to-probate-a-will outline before being retained, dentist Andrew who *always* phoned that evening to check on pain, and Peter Biagetti, who told a client he couldn't imagine litigating against one's own mother — and settled the case on the courthouse steps, winning all the client's future business.)

## Key Takeaways
1. Give first, freely — evidence beats assertion; never say "Trust me!"
2. Keep self-interest visibly in check; honesty that risks the sale builds more trust than a polished pitch.
3. Expect trust to grow slowly through accumulated experiences — there is no quick fix.
4. Address both rational competence and emotional reassurance; competence alone is insufficient.
5. Trust is two-way — select clients who can reciprocate, and take the personal risk it requires.
6. Trust people, not institutions; demonstrate caring — proof that the client's interests matter as much as yours.
7. Learn the timing: know when to bother a client and when to leave them alone.
