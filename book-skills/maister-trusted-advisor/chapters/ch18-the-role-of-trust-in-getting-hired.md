# Chapter 18: The Role of Trust in Getting Hired

## Core Idea
Selling and serving are not opposites but flip sides of the same coin — acting like a trusted advisor. Reframing "selling" as "getting hired" (earning trust) makes the required behavior clear: demonstrate, don't assert, and begin serving before the contract is signed.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Service as Selling / Selling as Service**: The two converge. You sell by demonstrating you can be trusted (a serving act); you serve so well the client rehires and refers you (a selling act).
  - When to use: Whenever you feel uncomfortable "selling" or believe your work should speak for itself.
  - How: Reframe the task from "How do I push what we've got?" to "How do I convince this person to put their trust in me?"
- **Start the Work Immediately (the "beauty contest" move)**: In a competitive final pitch, use the allotted time as the first hours of the actual project rather than delivering a standard presentation.
  - When to use: Competitive selection where the good being bought is intangible and vague.
  - How: Show the buyer, with visceral impact, what it feels like to be in a working relationship together, thereby reducing their perceived risks.

## Key Concepts
- **Getting hired vs. selling**: A reframe that clarifies the activity — it's about earning trust, not pushing services.
- **Demonstrate, don't assert**: You sell by showing you have something to offer, not by claiming it.
- **Buyer's risks**: Financial, emotional, lost-time, embarrassment, and career risks — all reducible by showing how it will feel to work together.
- **"The devil you know"**: Why the incumbent with the relationship holds a major advantage in professional services.
- **Selling confidence, security, and ease**: The professions sell intangibles — "No client wants to buy air unless they can breathe it first."
- **Professional obligation to point out opportunities**: If you'd warn a client of impending harm, you must equally flag significant opportunities for improvement — that's "selling" recast as contributing ideas.

## Mental Models
- Think "no client wants to buy a painting without seeing it" — clients prefer to buy based on a sample, so give a taste of the work.
- Use "getting hired" instead of "selling" whenever selling feels distasteful.
- Think of leaving a client's opportunity unnoted as *unprofessional*, not as tactful restraint.

## Anti-patterns
- **Separating sales and service into different people/units with no bridge (the Dalton case)**: Specialists in lead-gen, closing, diagnostics, and delivery — none owning the ongoing relationship or spotting new opportunities — produced a low-retention machine that collapsed when new-client flow dried up.
- **Talking about serving instead of serving ("we won't show you anything until money changes hands")**: Withholding value until paid loses both the work and the relationship.
- **Believing you sell a limited stock of specialized knowledge**: This fear makes professionals refuse to take a point of view or go out on a limb, costing them the work.

## Worked Example
Dalton (a disguised consulting firm) built a brilliant four-step industrial sales process — separate units and reward systems for lead generation, closing, diagnostics, and delivery. It worked staggeringly well while new-client demand was high. But the delivery team saw its job purely as hitting the defined spec and budget; it viewed selling as a distraction and never looked for new client opportunities. Clients, who worked daily only with delivery, had no one skilled at surfacing follow-on work. The result was chronically low retention and thin margins — masked by growth until demand for the core service declined and prime clients ran out, at which point Dalton's fortunes collapsed. Lesson: even if sales and service live in different people, there must be a mechanism to relate them — someone must combine seeking new opportunities with excellence in delivery.

## Key Takeaways
1. Sales and service converge; both are expressions of acting like a caring, trusted advisor.
2. Reframe "selling" as "getting hired" — earning trust — and the right behaviors become obvious.
3. The best selling technique is to stop selling and commence serving; give the client a sample of the relationship.
4. Buyers face many risks in intangible purchases; demonstrating how it feels to work together reduces them all.
5. Flagging client opportunities is a professional obligation, not aggressive selling.
