# Chapter 13: Envisioning an Alternate Reality

## Core Idea
Envisioning — the fourth stage — is jointly imagining, in rich detail, a specific future state the client wants, *before* jumping to action or solutions. It is the most-often-neglected stage and the one not strictly necessary, yet it can add the greatest value by freeing people into a creative, energizing perspective.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Joint Envisioning**: Advisor and client together concretize a specific vision and choice among many possible future states.
  - When to use: After framing, resisting the temptation to leap straight to "what can we do about it?"
  - How, three questions: 1) For what are we *really* aiming here? 2) What will it look like when we get there? 3) How will we know we are there?
- **Grammar shift**: Replace "why don't we?" (action language) with "how would things be if…" (possibility language).
  - How: Use descriptive sentences; ask about benefits, end states, and outcomes, not tasks.
- **The United Research envisioning exercise**: A large-scale sales/diagnostic step built on leading questions.
  - How: "Could things be different around here? If so, could they be better? How? What would things look like in this better future? What would have to change? Where would the benefits show up?" As people detail the solved future, change feels real, possible, energizing ("Let's go for it") and comforting ("We can do this").

## Key Concepts
- **Envision ≠ solve**: Any problem has many solutions depending on the future the client wants; envisioning chooses the destination first.
- **Most-neglected stage**: Sales models often go straight from problem definition to action/solution; envisioning is skipped.
- **Benefits ≠ envisioning**: Focusing on benefits is parallel to envisioning but not the same thing.
- **Frees up people**: Envisioning pulls people out of the technical, high-risk problem-solving stance into a perspective of freedom and creativity.
- **Sound-bite visions**: "New Deal," "New Frontier," "Great Society," "I Have a Dream" — succinct articulations of an attainable goal that build energy and consensus.

## Mental Models
- Think of envisioning as choosing the destination before debating the route — with a clear destination, both parties have signed up for the benefits and outlined the eventual solution's specs.
- Treat "what can we do?" as a Pavlovian bell to resist — respond with "Hold on, we'll get there, but let's first talk about where we want to go and what we're really trying to achieve."
- Use joint focus on a mutually attractive future to break down barriers and build bridges, unencumbered by present problems.

## Anti-patterns
- **Slipping into action language directly from framing**: The strong temptation (reinforced by the client's own "well, what can we do about this?") to skip envisioning because our self-image as technical masters is on the line.
- **Blame/self-focus/judgment in the frame**: A frame that is all about you, loaded with speculative motive attributions, and judgmental cannot support useful envisioning.

## Worked Example
**Charlie and Mark.** Mark, a consultant, took on a risky assignment on a CEO's personal assurance of open access, then found the CEO and COO increasingly unavailable while pressure to deliver held. Mark's instinctive frame: "The client is being very unfair to me: I took on considerable risk based on his personal assurance… but he is not delivering on his part of the bargain." This was self-focused, full of speculative motive attributions, and judgmental. Reframing step: "I am upset because the client is not spending the time with me that I expected, thus jeopardizing the quality of the work" — still self-focused and blaming. Working further to take the client's view (which they had to hypothesize): "Things have gotten busy… he hasn't been able to provide the time he had hoped… And I find myself possibly producing lower quality than I promised. Neither of us is happy."

Then Mark envisioned with the CEO: "Look, Albert, before we both jump to even more commitments we might regret, can we take a minute and be sure we're in sync about what's at stake? What are we trying to accomplish? How will we know when we've got it right?" Together they generated a picture of the future: "If we settled on a common view of where we're going, we'd be able to table an issue until we had time to talk about it… We'd know a delay wasn't personal… We'd raise issues more quickly… We wouldn't interpret events solely as personal failures or successes… We'd have confidence in each other." By getting the client to envision the benefits of that future state, Mark obtained the cooperation he needed.

## Key Takeaways
1. Envisioning jointly concretizes a specific desired future *before* solving the problem.
2. Anchor it in three questions: what are we really aiming for, what will it look like, how will we know we're there.
3. It's the most-neglected stage but often adds the greatest value by freeing people into creativity.
4. Shift grammar from "why don't we?" to "how would things be if…"; describe benefits and end states.
5. Resist the Pavlovian pull of "what can we do?" — name the destination first, and the eventual solution's specs emerge with it.
