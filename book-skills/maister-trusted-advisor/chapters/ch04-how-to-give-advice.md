# Chapter 4: How to Give Advice

## Core Idea
Advice-giving is almost never a purely logical exercise — it is an emotional "duet" between advisor and client. Being *right* is not enough; an advisor's job is to be *helpful*, which means earning the right to be critical and guiding the client to reach the conclusion themselves.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Advice-Giving Process (4 steps)**: (1) Give them their options; (2) Give them an education about the options (enough depth to weigh each); (3) Give them a recommendation; (4) Let them choose.
  - When to use: Any consequential decision where you want buy-in and to preserve trust.
  - How: Avoid taking a stand too early; be a guide through reasoning, not a source of pre-formed verdicts. If asked to choose, say "If this were my business/money, I would do X."
- **Socratic Teaching (advisor as teacher)**: Move the client from point A (what they know/believe/do now, and why) to point B (deeper understanding) via questions, not assertions.
  - When to use: When the client needs to *own* the conclusion.
  - How: First deeply understand point A through questioning and listening; then build a step-by-step reasoning journey using questions like: Why do you think we have this problem? What options exist? What advantages/adverse consequences do you foresee? How would key players react? What benefits from this approach?
- **The "Chat with Mom or Dad" stance**: Advise as if convincing a respected parent — find words that convey the point with great respect and soften the implied critique, without avoiding the issue or rolling over.
  - When to use: Whenever delivering critique or unwelcome advice.
- **Hard-to-Soft Phrasing / turn assertions into questions**: Replace "You've got to do X" with "Let's go through the options together…"; replace "What are your problems?" with "What is most in need of improvement?"; replace "This is the best solution" with "My other clients usually do X for these reasons — do you think that applies here?"

## Key Concepts
- **Earn the right to be critical**: Every professional's job implies critique — but the person who hired you is often responsible for the current state of affairs; tread carefully.
- **Emotional duet**: Advice is co-performed; you must recognize, deal with, and respond to client emotions.
- **Client anxiety**: Clients hand their affairs to a practitioner of an "impenetrable art" and feel dependency/loss of control; they want reassurance, not extra worries.
- **Diffuse defensiveness**: Defensiveness is *always* present; prove you're trying to help, not to criticize.
- **Assume responsibility for mutual understanding**: The advisor owns whether the client understood — not the client.
- **Client politics are unavoidable**: Rarely is there a single client; others must be won over. If you can't handle politics, you can't advise effectively.
- **Advice-giving is a performing art**: (per Jeswald Salacuse) — customize to each client's preferred style.

## Mental Models
- Think of yourself as a teacher, not a lecturer: "a lecture gets ideas from the teacher's notes to the student's notes without passing through either mind."
- Use "advise Mom or Dad," not "explain to a child" — same expertise gap, opposite tone.
- Turn every assertion you can into a question — it keeps the conversation alive if the client disagrees.
- Pre-wire multi-party decisions: call each attendee one-on-one *before* the meeting to surface agendas; don't facilitate cold in real time.

## Anti-patterns
- **Being right but not helpful**: David was fired for a "very direct and candid" correct diagnosis — proving someone wrong is satisfying but unproductive.
- **"You've got to do X"**: Evokes resistance; if the client says "No, I don't," you've nowhere to go and you're now on opposite sides.
- **"Did everyone understand that?"**: Forces students/clients to confess weakness — silence looks like comprehension but hides failure. Use "Have *I* made myself clear?" instead.
- **Taking a stand too early**: Damages your credibility as a neutral guide if the client thinks you've already reached an inflexible conclusion.
- **One fixed consultative style**: "Take it or leave it" is pompous, patronizing, and arrogant.

## Worked Example
When David taught mathematical statistics, he paused while writing on the blackboard to ask, "Did everyone understand that?" Silence met him, so he assumed he was teaching well. At exam time, everybody failed. A colleague diagnosed it: his attitude was fine but his skill was weak. "Did everyone understand?" forces a student to confess weakness to say no, so no one does. The fix was to shift the question to "Have *I* made myself clear here?" — which makes it easy (if bruising to the ego) for someone to say "No, you haven't," and reveals confusion without threatening anyone. The principle: the successful advisor assumes responsibility for the proper mutual understanding. (The chapter pairs this with the "You've got to do X" vs. "Let's go through the options together" phrasing contrast, and the parent analogy: "You've every right to do that, Dad, but Sister has a few extra burdens…")

## Key Takeaways
1. Be helpful, not merely right — earn the right to be critical.
2. Treat advice as an emotional duet; diagnose and address the client's anxiety and defensiveness first.
3. Run the 4-step process: options → education → recommendation → let them choose.
4. Use Socratic questions to move the client from A to B so the conclusion feels like theirs.
5. Advise as you would a respected parent; turn assertions into questions.
6. Own the mutual understanding — phrase check-ins so it's safe to admit confusion.
7. Master client politics: pre-call each decision-maker one-on-one and summarize decisions to reduce ambiguity.
8. Customize your style to each client; never impose a single fixed approach.
