# Chapter 21: The Case of Cross-Selling

## Core Idea
Cross-selling feels hard because trust is personal and non-transferable: an institutional relationship cannot transmit trust to a new person or new service. The critical sales events happen between people who know each other best, yet involve strangers — so new relationships must be built almost from scratch, using trust-building skills.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three Cross-Selling Types (Expand / Broaden / Diversify)**: Mapping old/new client individuals against old/new services.
  - **Type 1 Expand**: New service to a current client individual (new player = firm's new content expert, Barbara).
  - **Type 2 Broaden**: Existing service to a new person in the client org (new player = the new client, Don).
  - **Type 3 Diversify**: New service to a new person — two new players from each side; hardest of all.
  - When to use: Diagnosing which cross-sell you face and how much trust-building it demands.
  - How: Identify who's new on each side; the more new relationships, the lower the starting trust and the more investment required.
- **Trust Equation as Cross-Sell Diagnostic**: T = (C + R + I) / S; baseline scores (new client ≈ 1.25, current client ≈ 5) reveal how far a clumsy cross-sell drops trust.
  - When to use: Scoring the trust impact of a proposed cross-sell approach before acting.
  - How: Rate each component; a "passing the business card" ask can drop below even the new-client baseline. Use it as a checklist to cover credibility, reliability, intimacy, and self-orientation.
- **Iterative Sequencing (internal sales first)**: Do the internal sale before the external meeting; iterate.
  - When to use: Type 3 Diversify (and complex cross-sells generally).
  - How: Talk to your internal expert (Barbara) first, get more from your client, redefine the problem, iterate; proceed to the full multi-person meeting only when you can no longer think of a good reason *not* to.

## Key Concepts
- **Trust is personal, not institutional, and not very transferable**: The core cross-selling constraint.
- **Making withdrawals, not deposits**: A crude ask ("here's her card") draws down trust instead of building it during the sale.
- **The professional-firm internal sale**: Persuading your colleague (Barbara) to invest — barriers include less "credit" for another's client, precious time, and risk of embarrassment if you've mis-scoped.
- **The existing client as seller**: In Broaden, the first client (Curt) is the most objective advocate; excluding him from selling to a colleague (Don) sends negative signals.
- **"What's in it for me?"**: The existing client's question — answerable with rational benefits, but believed only if the advisor genuinely believes the cross-sell serves the client.
- **The hidden relationship**: Every cross-sell recasts the original Amy–Curt relationship, asking Curt to believe many things at once about Amy's judgment and motives.
- **Time investment as proof**: Spending your own unpaid time is the best signal of low self-orientation and genuine commitment.

## Mental Models
- Think of cross-selling as "meeting your prospective in-laws" — they'll probably like you, but don't take it for granted; more than one relationship is at stake.
- Use "if it isn't true, don't say it; if you don't believe it, why should the client?" — belief precedes words when getting a client to sell.
- Think of the bigger issue as *the original advisor's* credibility/intimacy/self-orientation, not the new expert's trust level.
- Use naming and claiming to surface the unspoken emotional issues in each cross-sell.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Passing the Business Card"**: Amy hands Curt Barbara's card and asks him to call — an ask with little in return that drops trust below even a cold new-client baseline and leaves Curt squirming with no graceful exit.
- **"I Just Need an Intro"**: Asking the client to vouch for someone to a colleague, presuming to judge the colleague's needs — downside for the client, no visible upside, feels taken for granted.
- **Relying on subjective assurances ("Barbara's really great, I've known her forever")**: These only draw down trust; it's Amy's credibility at issue, not Barbara's.
- **Believing you must be as expert as Barbara to say anything**: The client doesn't expect Amy to master the new content — only to know enough about the business issue to say it's worth talking to an expert.

## Worked Example
Type 1 (Expand). Amy, the current advisor, wants to bring her colleague Barbara (a new-content expert) to her existing client Curt. Amy thinks she can clearly help; Curt thinks of Amy's firm as "people who do what they're doing for me," doesn't associate them with the new area, and doesn't know Barbara. In the clumsy "Passing the Business Card" version, Amy tells Curt there's too much at stake to be coy, praises Barbara, and offers her card — "Would you like me to have her call you, or would you prefer to call her?" Curt feels put on the spot, asked for something with little in return; the trust score plunges below the 1.25 new-client baseline, and he buries the request. The fix: Amy invests a bit of her own time educating Barbara about the client *before* approaching Curt — slightly above the norm, a visible small sacrifice. This lets Barbara judge whether the issue is real (raising Amy's credibility), shows Amy operating from more than short-term self-interest, and deepens Amy–Barbara intimacy. Cross-selling can be made to happen — but only through extensive attention to trust building.

## Key Takeaways
1. Trust is personal and non-transferable; institutional relationships cannot transmit it to new people or services.
2. Diagnose the cross-sell type (Expand / Broaden / Diversify); the more new relationships, the more trust-building required.
3. Clumsy asks make withdrawals from the trust bank — score the impact with the trust equation before acting.
4. In Broaden, put the existing client on the selling team; his absence signals something wrong.
5. Sequence Diversify iteratively with the internal sale first; hold the full meeting only when there's no good reason not to.
6. The decisive issue is the *original* advisor's credibility, intimacy, and self-orientation — and investing your own time is the strongest proof of genuine intent.
