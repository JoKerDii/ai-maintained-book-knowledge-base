# Chapter 20: Re-earning Trust Away from the Current Assignment

## Core Idea
Relationships must be continually re-earned outside the demands of paid work. Clients want an invested business partner who proactively helps them think — not a salesperson who only appears to pitch or "romance" them.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **What Clients Actually Want (the 10 suggestions)**: Make an impact (don't just be visible); do more "on spec"; help us think and develop strategy; lead our thinking about our future; jump on our new data for extra analysis; schedule offsite brainstorming; understand our business by sitting in on meetings; benchmark us against others; explain competitors' moves; discuss anything else we should be doing.
  - When to use: Designing relationship activity between engagements.
  - How: Invest serious (non-reimbursed) time getting to know the client's business and industry in depth; create settings for mutual discussion, not one-way pitches.
- **Relationship Plan vs. Sales Plan**: Clients readily perceive the difference; build the former.
  - When to use: Whenever tempted to draft a "sales plan" for a key client.
  - How: Emphasize investing your own unpaid time to be useful, not a sequence of asks.
- **Institutional Relationship Model (the Relationship Manager role)**: Build firm-to-client relationships through many people and consistent standards, coordinated by a relationship manager who *manages* rather than tries to build the whole relationship alone.
  - When to use: Large firms serving large "accounts."
  - How: The manager acts as the client's advocate to the firm, creates opportunities for colleagues to form new trust relationships, coaches and serves the team first, and treats it all as an investment with dedicated non-reimbursed budget.

## Key Concepts
- **Continually earn future business**: Relationships can't be taken for granted; clients want providers to re-earn, not coast.
- **"On spec" work**: Investing preliminary unpaid effort in new areas to demonstrate value and commitment.
- **Staying in touch**: "There's nothing more destructive to trust than to call only when we want something"; great advisors keep contact even with no live project.
- **"It's never over as long as you check in"**: Work's impact continues; re-establishing a lapsed relationship is harder over time but never too late.
- **Junior professionals as sensors**: Field and junior client people often know emerging issues first; junior provider staff who contact them are best positioned to surface new needs — the CEO is often last to know.
- **Serve the team before you need them**: Relationship managers do favors for their team first, on the principle that if they serve the team, the team serves the client.
- **Business partner, not false friend**: Clients don't want to be "romanced" with dinners and events; they want usefulness.

## Mental Models
- Think "relationship plan," never "sales plan" — clients can tell which one you're running.
- Use "it's never too late to reestablish a relationship" to overcome the fear of reaching out after silence.
- Think of the relationship manager's job as *managing* the relationship (orchestrating many contacts), not personally being the single point of trust.
- "The best way to get someone to cooperate is to do them a favor first" — serve your internal team proactively.

## Anti-patterns
- **Calling only when you want something**: The single most trust-destructive habit between engagements.
- **Substituting romance for usefulness**: Fancy dinners and sporting events read as filler; clients already have those and want substance.
- **Relationship manager acting as a salesperson**: Focusing on generating fees rather than strengthening the relationship makes them less accepted and less effective.
- **Relationship manager hoarding the relationship**: Trying to build all client trust personally instead of opening doors for colleagues — a common and limiting mistake.

## Worked Example
Rebecca's silence (referenced from Ch 15), inverted here into the institutional fix. Clients interviewed across professions and countries voiced the same complaints: "They only see us when they want to sell something"; "They never call to ask how our business is doing"; "We don't want to be romanced." The remedy a firm builds: a relationship manager who, rather than pitching, offers free internal seminars where new firm members demonstrate expertise and meet client personnel in a low-stress, non-selling setting; offers a colleague to attend a client's internal meeting for free; arranges for every relevant trade magazine, industry publication, and analyst report to be summarized and circulated so the whole team stays current on the client's world. Each move invests unpaid time, opens new personal trust relationships, and — studies confirm — links directly to profitability.

## Key Takeaways
1. Relationships are re-earned continuously through invested, unpaid time — not taken for granted.
2. Build a relationship plan, not a sales plan; clients easily tell the difference.
3. Stay in regular contact even with no active project; calling only when you want something destroys trust.
4. Institutional relationships require many contacts and consistent standards, orchestrated by a relationship manager who manages rather than monopolizes trust.
5. Junior people on both sides are the early-warning system for emerging client needs.
6. Serve your own team first; if you serve the team well, the team serves the client. Relationship management is an investment activity that pays off in fees and careers.
