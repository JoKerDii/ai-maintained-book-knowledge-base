# Chapter 10: Engagement

## Core Idea
Engagement is the first stage — the moment a client first senses that this particular person might help with a specific problem. Before a client will talk about a need, an initial connection must be earned; listening alone is not enough to make them open up.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Engagement precedes listening**: Something must come before listening — an initial connection that makes the client believe you're worth talking to *on this issue*.
  - When to use: At the start of any new or renewed relationship, before diagnostic questioning.
  - How: Bring something of value (an insight, a point of view), not just "what are your problems?" Pull a trigger in the client's mind.
- **Visibly trying to customize**: Do something a little different — customized to this specific client — to break habitual perceptions and demonstrate you're willing to enter their world.
  - How: Saddlebags/pizza boxes (Ch 3), presenting in the client's own web-site format, incorporating their products, even video or live performance — anything that signals "maybe these people aren't like all the others."
- **Understand wants, not needs**: (from Brooks & Travesano) Engage quickly around something genuinely meaningful to the client — you need only *understand* their wants, not yet deliver on them.
- **Urgency vs. Importance sequencing** (Covey): Choose topic AND timing by available time.
  - How: With limited time (pass-in-the-hall, five minutes), lay out the agenda, start urgent, end important. With more time, start important, save five minutes at the end for urgent. Either way it buys more client time.

## Key Concepts
- **Engagement = two beliefs**: The client must decide the issue is worth energy AND that you specifically are worth opening up to.
- **Nontrivial decision**: Putting an issue forth costs the client emotional energy and personal risk; they won't invest unless they foresee good results.
- **Re-engagement**: Existing clients grant an audience (they'll hear you out) but not necessarily engagement (opening up) — you must stay new, intriguing, and exciting without seeming predictable.
- **Content-driven vs. relationship-driven approaches**: Early relationships rely on expertise/content topics; later ones earn the right to raise competitive, career, and personal issues.
- **Six degrees of separation research**: Second-stage prospect research — do you know someone who knows someone who worked with this person?

## Mental Models
- Think of engagement as breaking through the clutter: advisors are self-focused (rehearsing lines, checking appearance, fine-tuning the proposal), so genuinely articulating the client's wants is striking enough to create engagement.
- Use "if you can't add value, postpone the meeting" — just having a meeting isn't enough; wait until you can add value.
- Talk to a new client as a *new* friend, not an old one — conversational, not transactional, but never falsely familiar.
- Don't hold back a topic for lack of time to shine — even an indication of caring can serve you well years later.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Just asking what your problems are"**: The CFO's complaint — advisors who come only to listen and bring nothing of value waste the client's time.
- **Purely transactional early interactions**: Focusing strictly on content gets you pegged as a technician, not an advisor.
- **Name-dropping**: Facts about where you've been only work if genuinely of interest to *them*.
- **Stale or overused topics**: Bringing a hot issue that meets blank stares because it isn't fresh or well-timed.

## Worked Example
A major consulting firm built its success on engaging new clients by earning trust first. Before *any* contact with a carefully selected target, it did a thorough industry analysis — not just collecting facts but developing genuine insights and a point of view. It would then approach the target: "We have some unique insights into your industry and we'd like to come and share them with you, for free. We don't pretend to know your business as well as you do, but we think we've got some information and views that are a little different. May we come and discuss them with you?" This embodies the Ch 5 principles — go first, illustrate, don't assert — earning sales by earning trust.

For re-engaging an existing client: "Susan, I've been looking at your Web site and those of your competitors, and I notice that many of your competitors are making some very significant moves. I certainly don't have the answers, but I do have some thoughts about how you might respond. Would you… like to get together to bat around some ideas? No charge, of course."

## Key Takeaways
1. Engagement, not listening, is the true first step — a connection must precede the client opening up.
2. Bring value (insight, a point of view) to the table; "tell me your problems" is not engagement.
3. Visibly customize to this specific client to break habitual perceptions.
4. Engage around the client's *wants* — you only need to understand them, not yet deliver.
5. For existing clients, get the topic AND timing right; sequence by urgency vs. importance and available time.
6. Do exhaustive, current homework; postpone the meeting if you can't add value.
