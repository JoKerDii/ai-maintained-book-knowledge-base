# Chapter 19: Building Trust on the Current Assignment

## Core Idea
Trust is built through many small, visible gestures during the engagement — not by disappearing, doing the work, and returning with a brilliant product. The client must *experience* your respect, progress, and reliability continuously.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Nine Drivers of Perceived Value** (from *Managing the Professional Service Firm*): Understanding, sense of control, sense of progress, access/availability, responsiveness, reliability, appreciation, sense of importance, respect.
  - When to use: Auditing whether the client is actually *experiencing* good service, not just receiving it.
  - How: For each driver, ask what visible action makes the client experience it (e.g., ask regularly for their opinion so they experience respect).
- **Five Categories of Trust-Building Tactics on the Assignment**: (1) Involve the client in the process; (2) make reports/presentations easy to pass on; (3) help the client use what you deliver; (4) make meetings more valuable; (5) be accessible and available.
  - When to use: Throughout delivery, chosen situationally.
  - How: Brainstorm with the client, give them tasks and options, get format instructions, provide reusable summaries, coach them on internal politics, set agendas and pre-reads, send same-day meeting summaries, brief your assistants on client names.
- **Small Commitments, Reliably Met**: Make many small promises ("I'll have that to you by 5 PM," "I'll call at noon") and keep them.
  - When to use: Continuously, to accumulate reliability.
  - How: Reliability is built on accumulated experiences, not elapsed time — engineer frequent, small, kept commitments.

## Key Concepts
- **Experiencing vs. having a quality**: It's not enough to respect the client; act so the client experiences the respect.
- **Deliver a small, fast result**: Respect comes from seeing performance, so produce a quick early win to evidence effort; if impossible, send a status report.
- **The courtship/honeymoon/doubt cycle**: After a short honeymoon, clients naturally begin wondering whether you're devoting enough time and whether the approach will work — pre-empt with visible progress.
- **The client's ever-changing mind**: Goals appropriate at the start may be inappropriate by the end; anticipate change rather than fighting it as conflict.
- **Barrier busters / "we-not-me"**: Anticipating who inside the client org will resist, and building defenses into the work product or its rollout.
- **No surprises**: Keep clients in the loop; an angry-but-forewarned client is far safer than an angry-and-surprised one.

## Mental Models
- Think of reliability as built on *accumulated experiences, not elapsed time* — engineer many small kept promises.
- Use "err on the side of more communication, not less; more people in the loop, not fewer."
- Think of every deliverable as something the client can use internally *without modification*.
- "Clients often forget the promises we keep but remember the promises we didn't fulfill" — under-promise on deadlines and fees.

## Anti-patterns
- **Compromising an individual's confidence**: Giving away secrets to appear "in the know" — people forgive not knowing but never forgive misused information.
- **Missing a client's unspoken concern (the format story)**: Charlie and Rob dismissed a client's format complaint as trivial since the content was right; the client concluded that if the format was unacceptable, so was the content — and the relationship died.
- **"I am sometimes wrong, but I am never in doubt" behavior**: Vehement certainty alienates the wider client organization faster than almost anything.
- **Blowing a deadline (even an arbitrary one) without warning**: It's the client's deadline unless they say otherwise; if you'll be late, forewarn or renegotiate rather than deliver late silently.
- **Refusing responsibility ("It wasn't my fault," "That wasn't my intention")**: Reads to clients as abdication and diversion of blame — an infraction of trust.

## Worked Example
The format dispute. Charlie and Rob had a small, tight-deadline piece for a major investment bank during a very busy period. The key client had a concern about the final product — not the content, which was right, but the format. Charlie and Rob pointed out that the content was correct and tried to make light of the fact that "only" the format was the issue. It didn't matter to the client: he decided that if the format wasn't acceptable, neither was the content, and the relationship went no further. The lesson (trust-destroyer #2): failing to pick up and honor a client's questions, uncertainties, or unhappiness — even about something you consider minor — can sink the whole relationship.

## Key Takeaways
1. Make trust visible through many small gestures; the client must experience the service, not just receive it.
2. Reliability accrues from accumulated small commitments kept, not from time passing.
3. Deliver a fast, small result early to evidence performance and earn respect; use status reports when quick results aren't possible.
4. Keep clients continuously in the loop — no dramatic reveals, no surprises, especially in semipublic forums.
5. Under-promise on deadlines and fees; broken promises are remembered far longer than kept ones.
6. Take responsibility for outcomes; anticipate the client's changing mind and internal resistance.
