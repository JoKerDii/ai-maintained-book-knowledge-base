# Chapter 17: An Inaccurate Map of Our Pain: What We Get Wrong About Mental Illness
*Part III: Rethinking Abnormal: Afflictions as Adaptations*

## Core Idea
Maté argues that mainstream psychiatry's biological emphasis — mental illness as a genetically dictated chemical imbalance — is a construct that has outrun its evidence, and that by keeping our gaze on effects rather than causes it gives us, in Johann Hari's phrase, an inaccurate map of our own pain.

> **How to hold this chapter.** This is Maté's position, and it diverges sharply from mainstream psychiatry, which continues to treat diagnostic categories and pharmacological treatment as clinically essential. Maté himself is explicit that he is **not anti-pharmacology**: he took Prozac himself with major benefit, has seen psychiatric medication be life-enhancing and lifesaving, and states that his objection is not that doctors prescribe but that too often prescribing is *all* they do. Nothing here should be read as guidance to stop or change medication — that is a decision for a person and their prescriber.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **"You have been injured" — reframing symptom as injury**: Replace the question *what disease does this person have* with *what happened to this person, and what is still happening.*
  - When to use: with any presentation of mental distress, especially one that has cycled through many diagnoses and drug trials without resolution.
  - How: (1) Take the diagnosis as shorthand, not explanation. (2) Ask the questions the intake form omits — Is there any reason you might feel distressed? What has been happening in your life? Is anything hurting you that we might change? (3) Ask about childhood adversity, which in Maté's account is routinely never asked. (4) Direct "prescription" toward what would promote healing of the wound, alongside whatever symptom relief is warranted.
  - Why it works: it restores agency. Biological determinism reinforces the story Darrell Hammond names — *you're the only one that has no power* — and leaves the person passively receiving treatment. Failure mode: swinging to the opposite error and implying the sufferer chose or caused their condition; Maté's stance is explicitly non-blaming.

- **Diagnosis as abstraction, not explanation**: No diagnosis ever explains anything. Diagnoses are professional shorthand — summaries of reported symptoms and observed behavior — sometimes helpful, always incomplete.
  - When to use: at the moment a label starts functioning as a causal account.
  - How: test for the circle. *Why does this person have mood swings? Because they have bipolar disorder. How do you know? Because they have mood swings.* (Lucy Johnstone). Then ask what the label reveals about the underlying events and dynamics — usually nothing.
  - Note the concession Maté makes: receiving a diagnosis can validate a lifetime of diffuse experience and be a genuine first step toward healing. He knows this firsthand. The dead end is only when diagnosis is mistaken for explanation.

- **The relational test (via oppositional defiant disorder)**: Some "disorders" are definitionally relational and therefore cannot be located inside one skull.
  - How: apply the ODD case — oppositionality requires an "anyone" to oppose. Lock yourself alone in a room and try to oppose somebody. Since a child develops in relationship, her behavior is intelligible only through the relational environment. Ask, then, why we diagnose the child rather than "diagnosing" and treating the family, school, community, and culture.

- **The medication-efficacy fallacy**: That a drug relieves a symptom reveals nothing about the symptom's origin.
  - How: run Maté's own test cases. If aspirin eases a headache, was the headache an inherited deficiency of acetylsalicylic acid? If bourbon relaxes you, is your nervous system suffering a DNA-dictated whiskey shortage? Benefit is real; it is not evidence of cause.

## Key Concepts
- **Construct**: A frame we develop to interpret a phenomenon — valid in some respects, erroneous in others, never objective. "Mentally ill" is one, as "sinful" was another; both say as much about the culture as about what is seen.
- **No measurable physical markers**: Unlike cancer or rheumatoid arthritis, no blood test, biopsy, scan, or radiograph confirms or rules out a psychiatric diagnosis; markers remain subjective and behavioral. The DSM-5 task force chair conceded in 2013 that the promised biomarkers remain "disappointingly distant."
- **Chemical-imbalance theory**: The serotonin/dopamine account of depression and schizophrenia. Maté cites Robert Whitaker's finding that source materials for the claim did not contain the finding — researchers described it to him as a metaphor.
- **The brain as a historical organ** (Bruce Perry): It stores our personal narrative, in chemistry and neural networks — so difficult experience naturally produces disturbed neurobiology, without the neurobiology being the source.
- **Birth-month effect**: A B.C. study of nearly a million schoolchildren found December-born kids 39 percent more likely to be diagnosed with ADHD than January-born classmates — eleven months less brain development, medicated as a genetic disorder.
- **Orchid children** (Tom Boyce): Exquisitely sensitive to environment — especially vulnerable under adversity, unusually vital and creative under nurture. Sensitivity, not disease, is what gets transmitted.
- **Sensitivity as gift and curse**: The same genes that potentiate suffering in a stressed environment potentiate resilience, insight, and creativity in a supportive one.
- **Relational connectedness as top predictor** (Perry): Current relational connectedness, then history of connectedness, predict present functioning more powerfully than formal ACE scores.
- **The conservative function of genetic explanation**: "It's all in the genes" — an explanation for the way things are that does not threaten the way things are (Menand). It absolves individuals, parents, and societies alike, at the cost of disempowering all of them.

## Mental Models
- **Use "the brain is a historical organ" when a scan shows an abnormality** — the finding is as likely a record of what happened as a cause of it.
- **Think of a diagnosis as a compressed summary, never a causal story** — useful for communication and access to care, useless as an answer to *why*.
- **When a childhood presentation looks disordered, widen the frame by one level** — from child to family, family to school, school to culture — and see whether the "disorder" resolves into an intelligible response.
- **Use "responsibility without guilt" as the stance** — if mental health is not dictated by genes, we are not their victims, and there is much we can do; that is good news, not an accusation.

## Anti-patterns
- **Circular diagnosis**: Naming the symptom cluster and calling it the cause. Explains nothing, forecloses inquiry.
- **Never asking about childhood adversity**: A man saw up to forty psychiatrists over thirty-five years and accumulated a stack of diagnoses; no one asked. The evidence linking childhood misfortune to later psychiatric disorder is, per Bentall, statistically about as strong as the smoking–lung cancer link.
- **Simplistic biological explanation as the comfortable fallback**: It costs little time or emotional energy, and it spares clinicians from facing their own shadow side. Patients participate too — looking at one's life is enormous work.
- **Genetic fundamentalism as an escape from accountability**: It shields families and societies from examining conditions they could change, and historically has served to justify inequality.
- **Pathologizing ordinary feeling in children** — while equally, dismissing the real rise in children's distress as mere overdiagnosis. Maté holds both: overdiagnosis is a genuine risk, *and* the world children are born into now is disrupting cognitive and emotional self-regulation for real.

## Worked Example
Maté, in his mid-forties, is in a state he records in his diary as having no energy for life — spending every free weekend enervated, passive, demoralized, depressed and depressing to be with. He goes on Prozac despite his own skepticism. Within days he is transformed: his wife notices his face soften; he greets mornings with vim instead of venom; he can feel and express tenderness where he had been cold and brittle. He marvels aloud that people can apparently feel like this normally.

Then comes the error he flags on himself. Converted, he becomes something of a Prozac booster in his practice, looking for pathology where there was only everyday unhappiness, telling patients with prescription pad ready: *you have a chemical imbalance, you're lacking serotonin.* He calls this uttering scientific nonfacts. The medication helped him; it did not thereby establish where his depression came from.

**What it demonstrates:** Hold two things at once — a drug can be genuinely, even profoundly, helpful, and its helpfulness proves nothing about origins. Confusing the two turns relief into a false explanatory story that then blocks the work of healing.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat every diagnosis as shorthand and then ask the question it skipped: what happened, and what is still happening?
2. Ask about childhood adversity as a matter of routine — in Maté's clinical experience it is the question most reliably never asked.
3. Never infer cause from treatment response.
4. When the "disorder" is relational by definition, examine the relationship, not the individual.
5. Distinguish sensitivity from pathology; the orchid child's vulnerability and her gifts run on the same wiring.
6. Refuse both poles — genetic fatalism and personal blame. Responsibility can be embraced without guilt.
7. Present this material as a contested position, not settled consensus, and never as a reason for anyone to alter medication outside a conversation with their prescriber.
