# Chapter 3: You Rattle My Brain: Our Highly Interpersonal Biology
*Part I: Our Interconnected Nature*

## Core Idea
The bodymind unity does not stop at the skin. Our physiology is shaped, from the outset and throughout life, by other bodies and minds and by the social order we live in — **our biology itself is interpersonal**. Human beings can be studied as self-contained organisms in a laboratory, but never in real life.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Interpersonal biology**: Maté's widening of Daniel Siegel's *interpersonal neurobiology* — dropping the "neuro-" prefix to place not just brain and nervous system but the entire mental-physical makeup under the interpersonal banner.
  - When to use: whenever a physiological finding is being explained solely by what is happening inside one body.
  - How: (1) Identify the physiological outcome (inflammation, stress-hormone level, blood pressure, immune function, symptom flare). (2) Map the close relational field: intimate partner, parents, children, household emotional atmosphere. (3) Map the wider field: job strain and rank, employment security, income, neighborhood, social status relative to others, discrimination, loneliness, and how stable the social order itself currently is. (4) Ask which of those are modifiable and by whom. (5) Refuse to stop at the individual when the pattern is population-wide.
  - Why it works: the brain is the central organ of a supersystem reaching every tissue — vessel calibre, intestinal contraction, heartbeat, bone-marrow immune-cell manufacture, hormonal secretion, kidney function. Emotions act on nerves, nerves on hormones, hormones on immunity, immunity on brain, brain on gut and back again — and each person's version of that loop acts on everyone else's. Failure mode: read fatalistically ("my circumstances have already written my body"), it disempowers; the point is that relational fields are among the most changeable variables in medicine.
- **The biopsychosocial approach** (George Engel): Medicine's "crippling flaw" is that it does not include the patient *as a person*, though a person is the prime object of study.
  - How: hold emotions and physiology as a single dynamic process unfolding in a context of relationships, from the intimate to the cultural — and treat that context as part of the case, not as background colour.

## Key Concepts
- **Interpersonal biology**: The whole mental-physical organism as constituted and continually reshaped by relationships and environment.
- **Biopsychosocial**: Engel's framing — the unity of emotion and physiology, understood only within a relational and cultural context.
- **Ego (as used here)**: Not conceit, but the internally perceived separate self we invoke hundreds of times a day. A healthy ego is convinced of its separateness — reasonably so; the error is losing sight of the equally real other half.
- **Interbeing** (Thich Nhat Hanh): "There are no separate entities, only manifestations that rely on each other to be possible." Not mysticism — a scientist conversant with the evidence would simply nod.
- **Interdependent co-arising**: The Buddha's formulation — this is because that is. A leaf is both a discrete thing *and* a process of sun, rain, soil, and minerals.
- **The status gradient**: In the British civil service studies, lower rank predicted death from heart disease better than smoking, cholesterol, or hypertension.
- **Loneliness as a lethal exposure**: Across 300,000+ participants, the effect of deficient relationships is comparable to smoking and alcohol, and exceeds physical inactivity and obesity.
- **"All my relations"**: The Indigenous greeting naming the individual's multidimensional bond with people, ancestors, land, and creatures — and heard by Maté in the communities Canada has harmed most.

## Mental Models
- Think of the wood-wide web: trees of the same species are communal, form alliances across species, and signal each other electrically and chemically. Apparent isolates are nodes.
- Use "who else is in this physiology?" as a clinical question. A child's asthma inflammation tracks the parents' emotional state; a couple's hostility during conflict shows up in their stress hormones and immune function — identically for newlyweds and septuagenarians.
- When you see an illness clustered by race, class, rank, or employment status, ask Maté's question directly: is this individual pathology or the manifestation of a social malaise? Racial discrimination predicts adult-onset asthma in Black American women — the airway is individual, the cause is not.
- Treat marriage-and-mortality findings as relational, not institutional: married people outlive their single contemporaries, but unhappily married people fare worse in well-being than the unmarried. It is not the status; it is the relationship.
- Note the asymmetries — marriage's protective effect was roughly five times greater for men than women, which is a fact about gender roles in this culture, not about biology.

## Anti-patterns
- **Methodological individualism in medicine**: Focusing on the organism and its internal processes is not wrong as such — it simply misses the pivotal influence of the mental, emotional, social, and natural environments people actually live in.
- **Treating patients as bags of chemicals and organs**: Siegel's description of his own medical training — teachers who approached patients and students alike as if they had no subjective inner core.
- **Filing interconnection under "spirituality"**: Relegating these observations to mystical belief is a way of not having to act on the evidence.
- **Locating a socially produced illness inside the sufferer**: The most consequential form of the "normal that isn't healthy."

## Worked Example
A review of the literature on childhood asthma finds it "consistently shown" that parents in an unfavorable mental state — depression, anxiety, stress, chronic irritation — predict poorer status for the child's asthma. The child's lung inflammation is directly affected by the mother's or father's emotions. Separately, in a large cohort of Black American women, experiences of racial discrimination are associated with adult onset of the same disease.

Two different inflamed airways. In one, the relevant exposure sits across the breakfast table; in the other, it is diffused through an entire society. In neither case does the pathology reside solely in the patient.

**What it demonstrates:** Where the inflammation *is* and where it *comes from* are different questions — and only the second one tells you what to change.

## Key Takeaways
1. When taking a history, ask who the patient lives with and what the emotional weather in that home is — especially with children, whose physiology is most porous to caregivers' states.
2. Screen for job strain, workplace rank, and employment insecurity as you would for a risk factor, because that is what they are.
3. Treat loneliness as a first-order medical exposure, particularly in older patients separated from pleasure, connection, and support.
4. When a disease clusters by race or class, name discrimination and deprivation as exposures rather than treating the cluster as unexplained.
5. Resist the reflex to treat interconnection as sentiment — the evidence is ordinary epidemiology and physiology.
6. Remember the direction runs both ways: your own state as clinician, parent, or partner is an input into someone else's biology.
