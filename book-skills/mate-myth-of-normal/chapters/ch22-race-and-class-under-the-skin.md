# Chapter 22: The Assaulted Sense of Self: How Race and Class Get Under the Skin
*Part IV: The Toxicities of Our Culture*

## Core Idea
Race and economic status are not background variables in health — they are exposures with traceable biological pathways. Racism and poverty become physically embedded in the body through the same stress mechanisms this book has followed throughout, producing measurable differences in inflammation, telomere length, blood pressure, brain development, and mortality. Maté's insistence, echoing the research he cites: it is racism, not race, that harms — race in physiological or genetic terms does not exist.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Biological embedding (Clyde Hertzman)**: the principle that social environments and experiences "get under the skin early in life," shaping biology and development. Hertzman meant *under the skin* literally — what life events do to skin, nervous system, and viscera.
  - When to use: whenever a health disparity between groups is being explained by genetics or by behavior.
  - How: identify the social exposure; identify the stress-mediated pathway (inflammatory gene expression, telomere shortening, HPA dysregulation, blood pressure, airway narrowing, blood sugar); check whether the disparity persists after controlling for income and behavior. If it does, the exposure is the social position itself.
  - Why it works: it converts a moral or political claim into a mechanistic one that can be measured. Hertzman's list of outcomes with early-life social determinants includes heart disease, diabetes, obesity, depression, substance abuse, school success, premature mortality, disability at retirement, accelerated aging, and memory loss.
  - Failure mode: reading embedding as destiny. The point of naming a mechanism is that mechanisms are interruptible — at the level of the exposure.

- **The assaulted sense of self (Kenneth Hardy)**: a state in which "the soul of one's being gets perpetually punctured" — when one's definition of self is defined by someone else, when the sense of self is defined by what one is *not* rather than by what one is. Hardy: "Who I am thus becomes a response to how I am defined; it's always in response to something else."
  - When to use: to name the internal dimension of racism that overt-discrimination measures miss.
  - How: attend to internalized bias alongside external events. Maté cites a study of Black American men finding that overt experiences of racism *and* the assaulted sense of self, including internalized racial bias, "operate jointly to accelerate biological aging."
  - Why it matters clinically: Hardy's answer to why he keeps raising race — "If I don't bring it up, I have all these physiological things inside" — locates emotional suppression, and its biological harms, among racism's injuries.

- **Intersectionality as a health framework**: race, class, and gender are treated separately in these chapters for exposition only. Maté is explicit that treating them as independent operators is a fallacy — for many individuals they intersect so thoroughly that what is a function of which cannot be teased out.
  - How to apply: look for compounding. Maté cites biomarker data in which poor people scored worse than the economically advantaged in both races, *but non-poor Blacks had a greater probability of high scores than poor whites* — and differences were most pronounced among non-poor Black women compared with non-poor white women.

- **The social gradient (Michael Marmot)**: the linking of social rank with health across *all* strata, not just the poorest.
  - How: assess relative position, not just absolute deprivation. Marmot's Whitehall studies found British civil servants' rank correlated with risk for heart disease, cancer, and mental health diagnoses — the further down the ladder, the higher the risk, independent of smoking or blood pressure, and among a cohort with economic security and respectable middle-class employment.
  - Why it works: status confers degrees of control, and lack of control is a known trigger for physiological stress. Richard Wilkinson's aside is worth keeping: "It is easier to vacate contaminated buildings than to change social structures."

## Key Concepts
- **Biological embedding**: social experience shaping physiology, especially early in life.
- **Assaulted sense of self**: identity constituted in response to another's hostile definition of you.
- **"Race is the child of racism, not the father" (Ta-Nehisi Coates)**: the concept of race emerges from the racist's distorted imagination; the word did not exist meaningfully until the late eighteenth century, arising with European capitalism's subjugation of peoples elsewhere.
- **Othering as an antidote to self-doubt**: Maté's psychological account — if I don't feel good about myself, I can at least feel superior to somebody. He cites Sartre on the anti-Semite as a man afraid not of Jews but of himself.
- **Sociogenic brain damage (Ashley Montagu, 1974)**: the claim, since supported by imaging, that stressed environments including poverty interfere with brain development — reduced cortical surface area, smaller hippocampi and amygdalae, impaired adolescent serotonin function. One scientist calls poverty a "neurotoxin."
- **Social determinants of health**: research Maté cites attributes at most ~25% of population health to health care and a full 50% to social and economic environments — a figure he considers a serious underestimate.
- **"Medicine is a social science, and politics nothing else but medicine on a large scale" (Rudolf Virchow, 1848)**: the historical anchor for the whole chapter.
- **Generational trauma and armoring (Eboni Webb)**: "That assault itself is trauma, but the trauma is also how we have had to armor ourselves" — including the instruction to children not to cry at school.

## Mental Models
- **Use "racism, not race" as the causal formulation.** A review of multiple studies Maté cites concludes it is racism, not race itself, that threatens the lives of African American women and infants. This is not a softening — it makes the cause specific and therefore addressable.
- **Think of survival defenses as chronically activated stress apparatus.** Webb's account of raising children where "racism can be life-threatening" describes a life lived out of survival defenses — which is, physiologically, lifelong stress activation with the consequences this book has catalogued.
- **When a disparity persists across class lines, the exposure is not economic.** Maté's examples: breast cancer risk for Black women cutting across class; Black maternal mortality at three to four times the non-Hispanic white rate and infant mortality at least double, holding across education and socioeconomic status.
- **Read Helen Knott's grandmother's posture as physiology, not shyness.** Entering a grocery store, her shoulders hunch, her head faces the ground, she avoids eye contact — a woman who is her family's matriarch outside such spaces. Knott calls it "an almost unconscious calculation of safety, almost at all times." That calculation has a metabolic cost.

## Anti-patterns
- **Attributing group health disparities to genetics**: Maté states flatly that it is no outcome of genetic destiny that Indigenous people in Canada suffer more illness and die earlier. This is his standing objection to genetic determinism, applied to populations.
- **Clinical advice that ignores material conditions**: Gary Bloch's examples — "take this antibiotic on a full stomach," said to a woman for whom three meals is a joke; diabetes medication refused by a man in a shelter with sixty men and two toilets because the side effect was diarrhea. Dennis Raphael's satirical prescriptions ("Don't be poor"; "Don't live in damp, low-quality housing") make the point by absurdity.
- **Treating anger as the pathology rather than its suppression**: Maté cites Baldwin — to be Black and relatively conscious in this country is to be in rage almost all the time — and Baldwin's own next sentence about the problem of controlling that rage so it won't destroy you. Maté's position is that such anger, and its *obligatory suppression* in a society that fears and punishes Black rage, contributes to elevated cancer mortality. Presented as his conviction, not established consensus.
- **Assuming everyone walks the same streets**: Maté names his own blind spots here as a white-presenting middle-class male, despite his own history of being othered as a Jewish child in postwar Hungary. The satellite view suggests common ground; ground level does not.

## Worked Example
Maté sets two 1957 stories side by side. At thirteen, he and nearly thirty-eight thousand other Hungarian refugees were welcomed to Canada — the North seemed "true and strong and free," as the anthem says. In the same year, not far from where he settled, a four-year-old First Nations child named Carlene had a pin stuck through her tongue on her first day at a federally mandated, church-run residential school, for speaking her Native language. For an hour she could not put her tongue back in her mouth for fear of cutting her lips. Years of sexual abuse followed. By nine she was an alcoholic; later she became dependent on opiates. Now a grandmother and years sober, she told Maté her story at a healing ceremony, sobbing — and grieves to watch her grandchildren in the throes of addiction.

Maté places this against the epidemiology: Indigenous lifespan fifteen years shorter than other Canadians', infant mortality two to three times higher, type 2 diabetes four times more widespread in a population that knew no diabetes a century ago, and youth suicide rates in some communities among the highest of any identifiable cohort in the world. Nearly 30% of Canada's jail population is Indigenous, from roughly 5% of the general population.

**What it demonstrates:** the same country, the same year, the same province — and two entirely different exposures, whose divergence is visible three generations later in bodies, in addiction rates, and in life expectancy. The national self-image is not the determinant; the policy is.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask about discrimination as an exposure history, the way you would ask about smoking or occupational hazards. It has dose, duration, and measurable sequelae.
2. When a disparity survives adjustment for income and education, stop looking for a behavioral explanation.
3. Attend to internalized bias, not only overt events — the assaulted sense of self accelerates biological aging jointly with external racism.
4. Treat poverty as a clinical risk factor and, where possible, intervene on it directly, as Gary Bloch does with supplements and referrals to financial aid workers.
5. Remember the gradient runs the whole length of the ladder. Relative position predicts health even among the comfortable — control is the operative variable.
6. Hold intersections rather than isolating variables. The compounding is where the largest effects sit, and it is where Maté's data are most striking.
7. Where suppression of anger is imposed by threat, name the imposition. Maté's argument is about what a society does to people, never about what sufferers did to themselves.
