# Chapter 16: Show of Hands: A New View of Addiction
*Part III: Rethinking Abnormal: Afflictions as Adaptations*

## Core Idea
Maté offers a working definition of addiction built on three hallmarks rather than on drugs or disease — and it is deliberately ecumenical enough that nearly everyone falls under it. What determines addiction is not the substance's properties but the degree of pain needing to be soothed.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The three-hallmark definition of addiction**: A complex psychological, emotional, physiological, neurobiological, social, and spiritual process manifesting through any behavior in which a person finds temporary relief or pleasure and therefore craves, but that in the long term causes negative consequences to self or others, and which they refuse or are unable to give up.
  - The three hallmarks: (1) short-term relief or pleasure, therefore craving; (2) long-term suffering for oneself or others; (3) an inability to stop.
  - When to use: to assess any repeated behavior — substance or not — without importing disease language or moral judgment.
  - How: run the behavior through all three tests. If all three hold, it is the addiction process, whatever the target. Note what the definition deliberately omits: the word *disease*, and any restriction to drugs.
  - Why it works: it locates addiction in the person's *internal relationship* to the target, not in the target itself, which is why the same activity (exercise, work, meditation, sex, shopping) can be healthy for one person and addictive for another. Failure mode: using the definition's breadth to flatten real differences in severity and consequence.

- **The show of hands**: Maté asks any audience who, by that definition, is or has ever been addicted. Virtually no hand stays down.
  - When to use: as a self-audit and as a de-othering device before any discussion of addiction.
  - How: apply the three hallmarks to yourself first, honestly, before applying them to anyone else.

- **"Yes or no, depending on the pain"**: Is alcohol addictive? Food? Work? Sex? The answer embedded in the question is *yes or no* — determined by how much pain the person needs to soothe.
  - When to use: whenever a conversation is about the dangerousness of a substance rather than the state of the person.

- **Inquiry into the "happy childhood" narrative**: Most people's autobiographies are riddled with blind spots that the right questions dissolve.
  - How: (1) accept that genuine happy memories are real and do not rule out concurrent suffering; (2) note the standard bias — recall the good, suppress awareness of the hurt; (3) ask about emotional availability, not events; (4) look for what could not be said or felt in that household. Do this with compassion, not as prosecution.

## Key Concepts
- **The severity spectrum**: Degrees of suffering, dependency, consequence, and social punishment differ enormously and should not be flattened — but the intrinsic addiction process is the same across them.
- **The made-up line**: The boundary between upstanding "us" and downtrodden "them" is not even a fine line; it is invented.
- **Addiction as refugee story**: Flight from intolerable, unprocessed feelings into temporary, often illusory, freedom.
- **ACE Study (Felitti)**: Over 17,000 mostly middle-class subjects; more childhood adversity meant far higher adult risk of addiction, mental health, and medical problems. Adversity categories: abuse, neglect, household dysfunction. Effects multiplied rather than added — an ACE score of 6 carried a 46-fold greater risk of intravenous drug use.
- **Predisposition ≠ predetermination**: No single addiction gene exists; gene collections may confer susceptibility, and environment switches genes on and off. Nurturing rearing can offset genetic risk.
- **Dopamine and the incentive-motivation system**: The neurotransmitter without which mammals are inert; every addiction, substance or behavioral, involves it. In behavioral addictions like pornography, dopamine itself is effectively the substance, spiked repeatedly.
- **Endogenous opiates / endorphins**: The brain's own opioid system, evolved for social bonding — which is why opiate highs are described as maternal, warm, and safe, and why the socially disenfranchised are most vulnerable (Panksepp).
- **Attunement builds the reward apparatus**: Face-to-face interaction with an emotionally available parent drives production of oxytocin, endorphins, and dopamine — the very systems implicated in addiction.

## Mental Models
- **Think of addiction as a big tent you are probably already standing in** — the useful question is not whether you qualify but what you are soothing.
- **Use the three hallmarks when you catch yourself defending a habit by its target** — "but it's just exercise / work / meditation" is answered by the internal-relationship test, not by the activity's respectability.
- **Think of the addict as outsourcing a homegrown chemical** — the drive is toward feelings we were biologically built to generate ourselves, and would have, had development not been disrupted.
- **Use "any genetic risk can be offset by nurturing rearing" whenever someone fears passing on an addiction gene** — the fear is itself often more damaging than the (nonexistent) gene.

## Worked Example
A writer who lost her brother to an overdose invites Maté onto her podcast. She is adamant — insistent — that they grew up in a normal, happy home: a mother who chaired the PTA and chaperoned field trips, a stable traditional household, both parents loving their children as best they could and meeting their physical and social needs.

Maté does not dispute any of it. He simply asks questions about emotional experience rather than about household arrangements. Within the conversation, hurts she had entirely discounted surface from the depths. Afterward she tells her listeners the exchange caught her off guard, and concedes that her talking points on her happy childhood were incomplete.

A parallel case: a father whose bestselling memoir chronicled his son's near-fatal stimulant addiction, and who could find no big-T trauma anywhere in the family history. Pressed to look further, he located the pain in the crucible of a badly failing marriage he had been busy justifying to himself at the time — and came to understand, in conversation with his now-recovered son, that this was a major driver.

**What it demonstrates:** The absence of dramatic trauma is not the absence of pain. Provision of physical and social needs and provision of emotional attunement are different things, and it is the second that the addiction inquiry is after.

## Key Takeaways
1. Assess any repeated behavior by the three hallmarks — craved short-term relief, long-term harm, inability to stop — not by whether a drug is involved.
2. Put yourself in the tent first; the inquiry loses its integrity the moment it becomes about other people.
3. Hold both truths: the severity differences are real and must not be erased, *and* the underlying process is shared.
4. When someone reports a happy childhood alongside an addiction, ask better questions rather than accepting or attacking the narrative.
5. Stop looking for the addiction gene. Look at what happened, and at what didn't happen when it should have.
6. Read the reported "high" as a name for a missing endogenous state — warmth, peace, being adored — and ask what would supply it now, relationally.
7. Remember Maté's closing correction: it is not love we get hooked on, but our desperate attempts to cope with its lack.
