# Chapter 23: Society's Shock Absorbers: Why Women Have It Worse
*Part IV: The Toxicities of Our Culture*

## Core Idea
The gender gap in chronic illness and mental health diagnoses is not a medical mystery but a predictable consequence of what patriarchy asks of women: chronic self-suppression, the abdication of healthy anger, and the role of designated emotional caregiver. The personality traits Maté identifies across autoimmune conditions — compulsive self-sacrificing care for others, suppressed anger, excessive concern with social acceptability — are precisely the traits a patriarchal culture inculcates in women.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Self-silencing**: "the tendency to silence one's thoughts and feelings to maintain safe relationships, particularly intimate relationships."
  - When to use: when assessing chronic illness risk in anyone, but especially where relational or occupational safety is contingent on not speaking.
  - How: ask specifically about conflict behavior — in disagreement with a partner or a boss, does this person usually or always keep their feelings to themselves? Then ask whether that silence is chosen or is a rational adaptation to an environment where self-expression risks the relationship or the job.
  - Evidence Maté cites: in a study following nearly two thousand women over ten years, those who usually or always kept their feelings to themselves in conflict with spouses had over four times the risk of dying during follow-up compared with women who always showed their feelings. A separate study found that for women with non-supportive bosses, squashing anger increased heart disease risk.
  - Failure mode: hearing this as an instruction to "just express yourself." Maté is explicit that self-silencing is a *natural adaptation* to environments where expression carries real cost. The intervention has to address the environment, not only the habit.

- **Repression vs. suppression, applied to gendered anger**: suppression is the conscious holding-in of a feeling; repression occurs with no conscious awareness at all, as healthy feelings are banished below consciousness — out of mind, out of sight.
  - Why the distinction matters: as Julie Holland puts it, when we don't even know we're angry, we cannot converse with the person responsible or tackle the problem. We cry, we eat, we soothe ourselves a thousand different ways. Repressed anger is undiagnosable by self-report.
  - Maté's grounding: anger is a function of the brain's evolutionary RAGE system, whose purpose is to defend our boundaries, physical and emotional. The ingrained abdication of the natural, spontaneous "no" is not restricted to women, but it is imposed on them more widely and with greater force.

- **Emotional labor and the caregiving burden**: the culturally assigned role of designated emotional caregiver, borne at the caregiver's own expense.
  - How to apply: count caregiving as an exposure with immunological consequences. Maté cites that caretakers of Alzheimer's patients — the vast majority women — have significantly diminished immune function, poorer wound healing, more respiratory illness, and much higher depression rates than matched non-caregiving peers; mothers of emotionally challenged children show abnormal cortisol indicators, poorer metabolic function, less healthy fat distribution, and shorter telomeres.
  - The elegant instance, from Caroline Criado Perez's *Invisible Women*: women under fifty-five have worse outcomes than men after heart surgery, and a 2016 Canadian study isolated the care burden as a factor — women who have bypass surgery tend to go right back into caregiving roles, while men were more likely to have someone look after them.

- **Feminine-coded goods and services vs. masculine-coded perks and privileges (Kate Manne, *Down Girl*)**: what is "hers to give" — attention, affection, admiration, sympathy, sex, children; social, domestic, reproductive, and emotional labor; safe haven, nurture, security, soothing, comfort — counterposed against what is "his for the taking" — power, prestige, rank, reputation, honor, hierarchical status, upward mobility.
  - When to use: to make an implicit relational economy explicit and countable.
  - Maté's caveat, which he insists on: Manne is describing women of relative privilege. Others carry assigned gender roles *plus* poverty, single parenthood, and racial discrimination.

## Key Concepts
- **Patriarchy**: for Maté, not the conscious will or even awareness of individual men, but a system of power — ancient, and comfortably adapted by capitalism to its needs.
- **Self-silencing**: chronic negation of authentic experience to preserve relational safety.
- **Emotional labor**: the joblike, externally imposed, stress-inducing work of managing others' feelings.
- **The assaulted sense of self**, imported from Ch 22: Maté asks how women in a sexualized and threatening climate can avoid developing what Kenneth Hardy identified as an imprint of racism.
- **Toxic masculinity as trauma**: Terry Real — "In our culture we 'turn boys into men' through disconnection." Learning to disconnect from feelings, vulnerabilities, and others is what we call autonomy and independence; Real calls it a traumatic wound, hidden because it is culturally normative.
- **Male fragility (Real)**: the essence of toxic masculinity is invulnerability; men are trying to live up to an inhuman standard and are dogged by a sense of falling short of it repeatedly.
- **Connective tissue, literal and social**: Maté's observation that women serve as the emotional glue holding families and communities together — and suffer far more from diseases of actual connective tissue (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, scleroderma, fibromyalgia).
- **"Shock absorbers"**: the *New York Times* headline Maté adopts — mothers absorbing the system's shocks, and, per a survey he cites, blaming themselves for the resulting conflicts and responding by leaving the workforce, starting antidepressants, or ignoring their own COVID concerns.

## Mental Models
- **Use "assaulted sense of self" as a shared mechanism across race and gender.** Maté's linking question: what burdens could women of any color and class share with Black people as a group? His answer — both are targeted by a culture that demeans, distorts, and impels people to suppress who they are. Where these pressures intersect, incidence rises.
- **Think of women's higher psychiatric medication rates as absorbed male distress.** Julie Holland's position, which Maté endorses: the disproportionate rate of anxiety and depression in women stems in large part from their absorption of male angst and their culturally assigned responsibility for soothing it. In that sense, women are ingesting the anxiolytics and antidepressants for both sexes. This is Maté and Holland's interpretation, not established epidemiological consensus.
- **When a man treats his partner as the manager of his emotional state, ask what role he is casting himself in.** Maté's answer about himself: an impotent, dependent child whose welfare hinges on Mommy's compliance — who struts, remonstrates, sulks, and is never sated. Both partners, in their own ways, are powerless. And the children lose maternal attention when the husband demands mothering energy; the father's attunement with them is compromised too.
- **Read "resilience" claims skeptically when the content is suppression.** The chapter's throughline: what gets praised as toughness in women is frequently the disabling of the protective "no."

## Anti-patterns
- **Mistaking compulsive niceness for virtue**: the self-abnegating cluster — doing for others compulsively, suppressing anger, over-concern with social acceptability — is found across autoimmune conditions and is culturally celebrated in women. Maté treats it as a health risk, not a character strength.
- **Prescribing self-expression without addressing the environment**: telling a woman with a hostile boss or an entitled partner to voice her anger, while the cost of doing so remains real, adds risk rather than removing it.
- **Sexualization framed as empowerment**: Alanis Morissette described to Maté the "headiness of the power" of being objectified as a young star, while her intellect and "being-ness" were diminished almost everywhere she turned — "Hey, I'll take the power wherever I can get it." Maté treats being valued for the use another can make of you as an assault on the self, however it is marketed.
- **Ignoring what patriarchy costs men**: the taboo against vulnerability is deeply harmful to men too. Anger may be permitted; sadness, grief, and acknowledging one's limits are not — and Maté names alcoholism, other addictions, workaholism, violence, and suicidality as the resulting escapes from vulnerability, grief, and fear.
- **Treating the gender health gap as biologically given**: the whole chapter is an argument that the answers are "staring us in the face and are entirely predictable" once bodymind unity and biopsychosocial causation are taken seriously.

## Worked Example
Liz, a thirty-eight-year-old Manitoba firefighter (pseudonymized), had been off work nearly a year with Crohn's disease. When the gut condition resolved, post-traumatic symptoms arrived: daily shaking, terror without proportionate cause, insomnia, crying without explicable reason, suicidal thoughts, and daily drinking to manage them. Her history included sexual abuse from age seven, recurring through childhood and adolescence.

Her workplace supplied the second layer. "It was a very macho scene. If you have any issues, you're a liability. Particularly if you are a woman, if you talk about it, you're considered a 'pussy.' They'll physically do stuff to you, sabotage you in some way. They threw tampons into my bed." Maté cites a 2017 study of female firefighters linking on-the-job harassment and threats to suicidal ideation and more severe psychiatric symptoms.

Liz's own summary of the adaptation: "I was denying myself as a person, denying my own desires, my wants. I was not paying attention to what I needed. Everyone else was far more important. My job was way more important than any concern that I had. I wasn't listening to myself in any regard."

Maté closes the loop when Terry Real speaks of male fragility: he was reminded of the men who threw the tampons — and of the vulnerability they were trying to shame in Liz, because they were ashamed of their own.

**What it demonstrates:** early sexual trauma, a self-abnegating coping style, and an environment that punishes vulnerability compound into autoimmune disease and post-traumatic symptoms — and the men enforcing that environment are themselves acting out an injury.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask about conflict behavior directly: in disagreement with a partner or supervisor, does this person say what they feel? Self-silencing carries mortality risk.
2. Distinguish repression from suppression before concluding someone "isn't angry." Repressed anger does not show up on self-report.
3. Count caregiving as a clinical exposure with documented immune, metabolic, and cellular-aging consequences.
4. Look for the self-abnegating triad — compulsive care for others, suppressed anger, over-concern with acceptability — and name it as risk rather than admiring it.
5. When treating a woman's chronic illness, examine the relational economy she is embedded in, not only her physiology or her history.
6. Include men in the analysis. The invulnerability standard damages them, and their damage is transmitted to the women assigned to absorb it.
7. Where Maté makes causal claims linking gendered self-suppression to autoimmune disease and cancer, hold them as his clinical position — he presents them as convictions grounded in pattern and mechanism, not as settled consensus, and he consistently rejects any reading that blames the ill for their illness.
