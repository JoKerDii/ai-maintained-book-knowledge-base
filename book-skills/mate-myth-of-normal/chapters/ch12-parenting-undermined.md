# Chapter 12: Horticulture on the Moon: Parenting, Undermined
*Part II: The Distortion of Human Development*

*Part II: The Distortion of Human Development*

## Core Idea

Parenting instinct is innate to our species — "so inherent to our species that it cannot be taught, only activated or disabled" — but instincts require the right environment to be evoked, and modern culture systematically fails to evoke them. The problem is not incompetent parents; it is a sociocultural job site that isolates, stresses, and misinforms them.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Evolved Nest (Darcia Narvaez)**: The set of early child-rearing conditions shared across small-band hunter-gatherer groups, constituting the developmental baseline our species evolved to expect.
  - When to use: as a diagnostic checklist when assessing a family's conditions, or when a parent asks "am I doing enough?" — it reframes the question from parental adequacy to environmental provision.
  - How: assess against the seven components — (1) soothing perinatal experience; (2) prompt responsiveness to the infant's needs and prevention of distress; (3) extensive touch and constant physical presence, including carrying and holding; (4) frequent, infant-initiated breastfeeding for two to five years; (5) a community of multiple warm, responsive adult caregivers; (6) a climate of positive social support for mother *and* infant; (7) creative free play in Nature with multi-aged mates. Note which are absent — Narvaez's term for the deficit is **unnestedness**.
  - Why it works: it moves the conversation off individual blame and onto conditions, which are the actual causal variables and the actual repairable ones. Failure mode: read as a purity standard, it becomes one more source of parental shame — the exact injury it was meant to relieve. The list is a description of a niche, not a scorecard.

- **Allomothering**: The provision of a select circle of supplementary caregivers beyond the parents (Greek *allo-*, "other than the usual"), who carry, rock, and play with the child and absorb household tasks.
  - When to use: whenever parenting stress, maternal isolation, or "I have no break" is the presenting problem.
  - How: identify who can genuinely take the baby — not advise, take. Restore some functional version of the traditional "lying in" period, in which the community waits on the new mother so her full attention can go to bonding and feeding. The research direction is consistent: the more support parents receive, the more responsive they can be to their children.
  - Why it works: allomothers are a buffer for the parent-child relationship, not a substitute for it. Failure mode: importing paid or intermittent care without warmth or continuity supplies logistics while leaving the attachment deficit untouched.

- **The Nursing Couple as the Unit of Analysis**: Following Winnicott — "There is no such thing as a baby"; the unit is the individual-environment set-up, not the individual. Montagu adds that when a baby is born, a mother is born, and for months her need for contact may exceed the infant's.
  - When to use: any time an infant's need is being assessed in isolation from the caregiver's state.
  - How: treat the dyad. Ask what the mother needs before asking what the baby needs; they are the same physiological system.

## Key Concepts

- **Interpersonal biology / co-regulation**: Infant and parent physiology are regulated *by each other's* interactions; the imprint of those interactions, or their absence, can last a lifetime.
- **The socializing mode (deMause)**: The mid-nineteenth-century shift toward producing a socially functional personality that conforms to society's expectations — the source of most twentieth-century psychological parenting models.
- **Unnestedness**: Narvaez's term for a childhood missing most components of the evolved nest.
- **Proximate separation (Allan Schore)**: The caregiver is physically present but psychologically absent — visually available, emotionally elsewhere. Primate infants show severe separation reactions to it.
- **Species-atypical**: Maté's recurring charge that humans are the only beings on Earth who routinely thwart their own species' inbuilt needs for healthy growth.
- **The parenting-industrial complex**: The proliferating expert-advice economy, which Maté reads as a *symptom* of instinctual disconnect rather than its cure.
- **The love cocktail**: Oxytocin, vasopressin, and endorphins, released by natural labor, skin-to-skin contact, and suckling — the neurochemistry that makes caregiving rewarding enough to be sustained.

## Mental Models

- **Think of parenting instinct as a gene, not a reflex.** Like genes, instincts are not automatic or autonomous; they must be evoked by the proper environment or we lose touch with them. This is as true for humans as for any animal kept in unnatural conditions.
- **Use the "horticulture on the moon" frame when someone concludes that parenting is inherently miserable.** Gardening on the moon would be maddening — but that tells you nothing about gardening, only that certain conditions must be in place. Loneliness and isolation are attributes of motherhood *in an alienating culture*, not of motherhood.
- **Read a stated parental preference as partly a cultural artifact.** Any culture shapes the preferences of its members in its own image; what adults "prefer" under unnatural circumstances may clash with what their nature would have them choose. Preference is real and deserves respect — it is just not self-evidently a guide to the child's needs.
- **Use "the more stressed the adult, the more stressed the child" as the working equation.** It is the shortest statement of interpersonal-biological math and predicts more than any technique-level variable.

## Anti-patterns

- **Centering the parent's preference over the child's need**: The governing question becomes "what do *you* want?" rather than what the child requires. Maté's objection is not to the parent having wants — it is that a culture out of touch with children's developmental needs *and* with what parents require to meet them is a poor source of preferences.
- **Treating data as an antidote to guilt**: Even carefully selected data is a poor remedy, because the angst usually speaks to a culturally induced alienation from instinct, not to an information gap.
- **Socialization as the organizing goal**: Kinder than treating children as putty to be beaten into shape, but it still centers something other than the child — the dictates of a society for which parents act as well-meaning, unwitting agents.
- **Intimidation-based discipline**: Backing threats with the fact that adults are larger and stronger. The American Academy of Pediatrics, reviewing nearly a hundred studies, called in 2018 for an end to spanking and harsh verbal punishment: it increases long-term aggression, undermines self-control, elevates stress hormones, and may harm brain development.
- **Shutting down your own response to a baby's distress**: Beyond stressing the infant's brain, it may weaken the parent's instincts, with consequences that outlast infancy. Maté cites his mother-in-law, who followed 1950s medical counsel to ignore her infants' cries: it was torture, and it went against all her motherly emotions.

## Worked Example

Emily Oster, an economist and author of a bestselling data-driven parenting manual, recounts attending her brother's wedding and trying to nurse her screaming daughter in a hundred-degree closet. Elsewhere she writes that motherhood can be lonely and isolating.

Maté does not dispute either report. He relocates the cause. That closet is the metaphor: shamed or shunned into isolation, hidden away, cramped, sweltering. Given how interpersonal neurobiology works — a distressed mother co-regulating a distressed infant, with none of the touch, support, or communal presence the nest presumes — the screaming is the expected output of the conditions, not evidence that nursing is burdensome or that the mother is inadequate. In a stressed environment, Maté notes from family practice, breastfeeding itself becomes an onerous chore and a source of maternal misery.

He extends the same reading to her preference for work over a fourth consecutive hour with her children, and refuses the moralizing conclusion: she is right to value the quality of hours over their number, has every right to her choice, and for far too long women's fulfillment through meaningful work apart from homemaking was squelched. He also insists class is the decisive variable — one in four American women returns to work within two weeks of giving birth, and fewer than 20 percent of new mothers have access to paid leave.

**What it demonstrates:** The felt difficulty of parenting is data about the environment, not a verdict on the parent — and the correct intervention is at the level of conditions, not technique or willpower.

## Key Takeaways

1. When a parent presents as failing, audit the conditions before the conduct: support, isolation, economic pressure, contact. Isolated parenting is stressed parenting.
2. Assess the dyad, never the infant alone. The mother's need for contact may exceed the baby's, and their physiology is one system.
3. Treat proliferating expert advice as a signal of instinctual disconnection, not a remedy for it. The counter-instinctual chase is itself a stressor.
4. Watch for proximate separation — the loving but preoccupied parent. Its imprint on development is as real as rage or coldness, and it delivers the message: *you must earn my attention.*
5. Name the class and policy dimension explicitly. Nine months minimum of maternal presence is what the infant's neurobiology asks for; most American mothers are structurally denied it. That is a policy failure, not a parenting failure.
6. Restore contact wherever you can — touch, carrying, holding, responsive presence — and restore adult support around the parent. These are the two levers with the most leverage.
