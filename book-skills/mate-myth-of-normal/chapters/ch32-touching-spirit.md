# Chapter 32: My Life as a Genuine Thing: Touching Spirit
*Part V: Pathways to Wholeness*

## Core Idea
Healing is not in the thinking mind's wheelhouse. Maté's argument here is that at some point the intellect's armored certainties have to recuse themselves and let something more vulnerable enter — and that the resulting contact with spirit (however named) is not exotic but a universal capacity blocked by a culture that teaches us to fill our emptiness from outside.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Surrendering to a God you don't believe in** (Ashley Judd's phrase): the leap of *nonliteral* faith. What shifts is not the acquisition of beliefs but the relaxation of the personality's militant *un*belief — which Maté notes can be every bit as fundamentalist as any religious certainty.
  - When to use: with people (often the intellectually capable ones) whose skepticism has hardened into a defense that forecloses any transformative experience.
  - How: the target is not conversion. It is loosening the grip of the certainty — theistic or atheistic — that already knows what is possible.

- **The three conditions Maté extracts from his own opening**: he lays out what had to happen, explicitly framing them as universal principles available without shamans or plants.
  1. **The thinking mind must step aside.** The mind is a house divided — one part hoping for the big aha, another stoking cynicism. Spirit, by contrast, is one with itself. Knowledge can lead you toward the door; it cannot walk through it.
  2. **It cannot be planned.** The events that brought him there laid waste to every plan he had made. Giving up the illusion of control was "the ante required to sit at the table of mystery."
  3. **Identity has to be surrendered.** He had to relinquish the persona of leader and healer, the habit of helping others while sparing nothing for himself, and accept the diminishment he feared would follow. The ego's protest — *they came all this way to work with me* — could only negotiate terms of surrender.
  - Why it works: agency springs paradoxically from the willingness to give up rigid control. Maté is careful about causation: letting go did not *cause* the healing — "that's not how grace works" — but was a prerequisite.

- **Healing is not necessarily catharsis**: Maté had long held the fixed idea that healing would require a monumental cathartic release or a return to relive and redeem the past. It can take that form; it need not.
  - The correction: **it is not the past that has to change, only our present relationship to ourselves.** Lying on the mat he was acutely aware that his infancy had happened exactly as it had, that his grandparents will never not have gone to their deaths — and that none of it could touch the peace that was his birthright. He describes this as "beyond acceptance": nothing to accept, except in the sense of gladly receive.

- **Listening for your "special truth"** (Wilhelm Reich): the one common rule is to listen patiently to yourself and give yourself the chance to find your own way, which is yours and nobody else's.
  - How: mindfulness practices create the space by separating us from the mind's traffic so we can observe it without being seduced, overwhelmed, or intimidated. Maté is emphatic that there is no single path — he tried ten days of silent contemplation and concluded, never again. Yoga, brief sittings, reading across traditions, contemplative silence, walking, gardening, drawing all count.
  - The point is not the big aha but the arising — sudden or gradual — of the **consciousness that holds the mind but doesn't mistake itself for its contents.**

## Key Concepts
- **"Holes" (A. H. Almaas)**: the broken connections where we lost contact with our deepest selves; the emptiness we then try to fill from outside. Society, Almaas says, is not an enemy but asleep — everyone around you is filling their holes and feels threatened if you don't fill yours the same way.
- **The compartmentalized psyche (Jack Kornfield)**: we live in a split world, so our psyche splits — money at work, body at the gym, psyche in therapy, art at a concert, the sacred at church — as if the sacred were separable from the work we do.
- **Higher power**: whether we know it or not, we all seek one; the longing shows up as the desire to belong, the drive to know our purpose, the urge to escape the conditioned personality, the taste for transcendence.
- **"More than meets the I" (Eckhart Tolle)**: there is more to being human than the egoic mind, with its baked-in sense of separateness, can comprehend or prove.
- **Cultural myopia (Wade Davis)**: the assumption that other peoples are failed or vestigial versions of ourselves rather than living, dynamic people with something to say.
- **Myth in its original sense**: not "that's just a myth" but a fount of knowledge and a portal to spirit — a collective expression of imagination in which nothing is isolated happenstance, and a potent antidote to the fantasy that mind and body are separable.
- **"A thought in the heart" (Michael Meade)**: collective knowing as old as we are.
- **Touching spirit**: Rick's phrase from San Quentin — "If I touch spirit, I'm not separated."

## Mental Models
- Think of spiritual opening as **subtraction, not acquisition**: Will Cooke's image of a spark in everyone, "that shimmering self," cluttered and stacked with what life has stuck to them so they can't shine — pull it away a little at a time and what's revealed is always something beautiful.
- Use **Nature as an available doorway** when ceremony, therapy, or practice is out of reach. The chapter's cases are deliberately ordinary in their means: Ashley Judd sitting in a creek in the Smokies; Clara Hughes quitting everything and walking six months a year; V, through metastatic cancer, falling in love with a single potted tree outside her hospital window until, on her last day there, it blossomed.
- Think of **illness as a signal the whole community is implicated in**. Lewis Mehl-Madrona's Lakota framing: the ill are the canaries in the mine, showing us that society is out of balance, and all of us bear responsibility for contributing to their healing — a genuinely biopsychosocial premise taken as a given rather than argued for.
- **"I'll be okay" as portable evidence**: Judd's epiphanic moment stays alive as something she can return to — "I kicked its ass before. I'll be okay." Hughes's version: I can breathe through anything; I can sit, write, draw, garden, do the dishes, come back to my breath.

## Anti-patterns
- **Filling yourself with evanescent externals**: it cannot be done. What is missing within cannot be replaced from without.
- **Militant unbelief mistaken for rigor**: a fundamentalism of its own, and a barricade against exactly the experience it demands proof of.
- **Waiting for the big cathartic aha**: a fixed idea Maté held for years and names as a mistake. Insisting healing must look monumental is one more way of controlling it.
- **Prescribing a spiritual path**: spirituality defies both description and prescription. Ten days of silence worked for someone; it did not work for Maté.
- **Romanticizing or aping Indigenous practice**: Maté is explicit — Native wisdom is not something to be consumed. It is a trove of traditions about living and dying that demands humble curiosity and respect, and can complement Western medicine's scientific wizardry rather than be harvested by it. He also notes these traditions are themselves fighting for survival, and names his own position as an outsider from the settler side.
- **Training doctors to disbelieve mind-body unity**: Mehl-Madrona's observation that most people intuitively grasp that what happens to the body reflects mind and spirit — "except maybe doctors, who are trained to disbelieve the idea."

## Worked Example
Rick, a lifer at San Quentin — a small, thin Black man with gray stubble, thirty years past a childhood carrying every category of adverse childhood experience, a violent adolescence, drug-addled young adulthood, and a killing — had come through a volunteer-led program that took him deep into himself. He told Maté the group made him stop running and face the inner demons; that he had learned to love himself.

Asked what he'd want the parole board to know: at the time of the crime, he said, he was separated from himself. He didn't know who he was. He didn't respect himself, so he couldn't respect anyone else; didn't love himself, so had no love for anybody else. After doing the time and "really stopping and looking at my life as a genuine thing," love opened him to everything outside himself — learning about himself was learning about everyone else, because he isn't different from everybody else. "If I touch spirit, I'm not separated." And: if they let him out, this is the work he wants to do; but even if they don't, he already knows who he is.

**What it demonstrates:** the spiritual opening Maté found in a Peruvian maloca is not conditional on jungles, plants, or shamans. It occurred in a prison meeting room, through sustained honest self-confrontation, and produced the same core recognition — non-separateness, and an identity that no longer depends on the outcome.

## Key Takeaways
1. Don't try to think your way through the last stretch. The intellect brings you to the threshold; it cannot cross it, and insisting it lead is itself the obstruction.
2. Loosen certainty in both directions. Militant unbelief closes the same door that dogma does.
3. Stop asking what healing is supposed to look like. It is not the past that changes — only your present relationship to yourself.
4. Find the practice that is actually yours, and accept that this may take discarding several that are respected and useful to other people.
5. Nature is the most available doorway most people have: walking, gardening, a single tree. The requirement is stillness and undistracted attention, not scale.
6. Approach Indigenous traditions as living cultures with something to say, not as a resource to extract technique from.
7. Mindfulness has documented physiological correlates in the research Maté cites — reduced inflammation, epigenetic and telomere effects, lowered stress hormones — which he reads as mind-body unity in action. Note these are his interpretation of an evolving literature.
