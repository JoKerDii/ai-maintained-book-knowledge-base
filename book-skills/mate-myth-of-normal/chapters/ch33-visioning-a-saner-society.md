# Chapter 33: Unmaking a Myth: Visioning a Saner Society
*Part V: Pathways to Wholeness*

> This chapter is Maté's social and political argument, and he says so plainly — he opens by admitting he is more comfortable describing the problem than charting a course out of it, and states that his particular political examples are not offered to enroll the reader in his views. Read the positions below as **Maté's**, attributed, not as findings.

## Core Idea
Maté's claim is that the same principles that heal a person scale to a society: biopsychosocial medicine, disease as teacher, the primacy of both attachment and authenticity, and above all fearless self-inquiry conducted collectively. He names the necessary conditions for a saner culture while declining to supply a blueprint, and identifies **disillusionment** — willingly shedding what we have believed — as the entry point.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Disillusionment as a healing step (individual and collective)**: Maté's reframe of a word we normally use ruefully. His diagnostic question: *"Would you prefer to be illusioned or disillusioned?"* Would you rather engage the world as it is, or only as you wish it were — and which brings more suffering in the end?
  - When to use: whenever letting go of a belief feels like loss. Applies to a personal narrative ("my happy childhood") and to a national or ideological one identically.
  - How: (1) Name the illusion and what it has been doing for you — comfort, belonging, identity. (2) Expect the cost: something cherished is relinquished, and there is a period of feeling unmoored. (3) Notice what happens after: something relaxes, "no longer tasked with squaring circles and holding together impossible contradictions." (4) Weigh that against the cost of *refusing*, which we see far less easily.
  - Why it works: illusion requires continuous unconscious maintenance. Ignorance can bring a blissed-out tranquility, but Maté distinguishes that from true bliss, and notes that collectively it produces wide suffering.
  - Anchor quote he uses (James Baldwin): not everything faced can be changed, but nothing can be changed until it is faced.

- **Creative vs. reactive seeing (the pre-check before any reform)**: Before engaging major social tasks, examine whether you are approaching them from possibility or from your own habitual coping patterns.
  - How: automatic reaction is the specialty of the traumatized personality — "the ultimate hammer that only sees nails." Creativity starts with seeing that we *can* create, then developing a feel for what **wants** to be created. Maté calls it a facet of authenticity, a close cousin of authorship.
  - The stance it requires: "Something is possible here, no matter how things may look" — plus patience, perspective, and tolerance for both the real and the ideal. Chomsky's formulation, borrowed from Gramsci: pessimism of the intellect, optimism of the will.

- **The two additional A's: Activism and Advocacy**: Extending the four A's of healing (Authenticity, Agency, healthy Anger, Acceptance) to the social scale.
  - What they add: solidarity, collective thinking, and connection — Maté's explicit counter to capitalism's atomizing effects. They are, he says, socially meaningful ways of synthesizing the first four, and cannot be individual pursuits.
  - **Advocacy**: using whatever privilege you have to amplify the voices society denies. **Activism**: organizing people to demand change. Both express a healthy, necessary "no," often paired with a resounding "yes" to a concrete goal.
  - Why it heals: the health reward is having something bigger than yourself. Maté's point is not that activism is therapy but that authentic engagement resolves the isolation that drives so much suffering.

- **A trauma-informed institution (the audit)**: Applied by Maté across three domains, with the same test each time — does this system recognize what happened to people, and does it care for the emotional health of the people who staff it?
  - **Medicine**: Maté reports that in dozens of interviews with medical colleagues, including recent graduates, virtually none recalled being taught the mind-body unity or the documented links between adversity and physical disease. He describes the resistance as more subliminal than deliberate, more passive than active — a profession priding itself on evidence-based practice while ignoring evidence that questions its dogma. He also indicts medical training itself as traumatizing ("teaching by terror"), noting residents' suicides and time-constrained, fee-for-service visits that make history-taking impossible. Positive signs he cites: empathy training in some schools, Indigenous history curricula in Canada, ACEs screening in California public health under Nadine Burke Harris.
  - **Law**: he argues the current model would be more accurately named a trauma-punishing-and-inducing system, and that legal training leaves lawyers and judges more trauma-ignorant than physicians. A trauma-informed system would **not justify or excuse harmful behavior** — it would replace nakedly punitive measures with genuine rehabilitation.
  - **Education**: teachers trained in developmental science; emotional intelligence valued as highly as intellectual achievement; performance metrics that mostly reproduce social and racial advantage replaced with settings where all can thrive; and crucially, **"acting out" read as a plea for help or a marker of emotional pain rather than bad behavior to suppress, punish, or exclude.**

## Key Concepts
- **Malignant normality (Robert J. Lifton)**: great crimes committed by people held up as the epitome of normal in their societies. Maté's opening case: Eichmann certified normal by court psychiatrists before trial — one reportedly calling him more normal than himself, another finding his family relations "not only normal but most desirable."
- **"The barbarians of civilization" (Victor Hugo)**: Maté's label for respected figures whose policies cause mass suffering at a distance.
- **Self-actualization and non-adjustment (Maslow)**: healthy people were *not* "well-adjusted" in the naive sense of approving of and identifying with the culture. Neither conformists nor reflexive rebels, they held an inner detachment — able to stand off from the culture as if not quite belonging — expressing unconventionality without hostility but not without fight.
- **"The lie of the separate solo self" (Daniel Siegel)**: for Maté, the ultimate abnormality, and the falsehood authenticity exists to see through.
- **Trauma-literate society**: Bessel van der Kolk's claim that we are "on the verge" of one; Maté explicitly does *not* share the short-term optimism, because that consciousness has not penetrated the decisive institutions.
- **Scrupulous passivity (Joan Didion)**: what "fairness" has come to mean in journalism — covering the story not as it occurs but as it is presented, which is to say as manufactured.
- **The new citizenship**: seeking out alternative sources of knowledge, exposing yourself to uncertainty, entering others' points of view whether or not you agree, listening to people doing activist work on the ground, staying alert to how the myth of normal keeps itself normalized.

## Mental Models
- Think of **crisis as danger plus opportunity**: what an individual does with a life-threatening illness — learn from it and transform — is what Maté proposes a society could do with climate change and COVID-19. His reading of the pandemic: it unmasked our interconnection, and also exposed "we are all in this together" as a sad fiction given who bore the economic ravages and who took the windfalls.
- Use **"is this system correcting or compounding?"** as the single question when evaluating a medical, legal, or educational institution. Both answers are usually present; the ratio is the finding.
- Think of **normality as a hypnotic influence** and authenticity as its antidote — finding meaning in one's inner experience, unobscured by societally promulgated fictions.

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting for the system to reform itself**: Maté states that anyone expecting global corporate capitalism to face the truth of its own nature and transform is in for a long wait, and that its academic institutions and media will not readily surrender their role as ideological enablers.
- **Denial as a buttress of the status quo**: were we to see the state of things and what it costs us, we would no longer consent so easily. Baldwin's line, which Maté extends to almost anywhere on earth: words used mostly to cover the sleeper, not wake him.
- **Reducing people to their worst behavior**: Nneka Jones Tapia — "we are not our worst behavior"; it is not just people who need healing, it is the system that must be indicted and transformed.
- **Reactive rather than creative engagement**: bringing the traumatized personality's automaticity to social problems adds your own stress to problems already hard enough.
- **Mistaking a trauma-informed stance for excusing harm**: Maté forecloses this reading explicitly.

## Worked Example
Nan Goldin, whose opioid addiction appears earlier in the book, learned that the Sacklers — the family controlling Purdue Pharma, whom she had known only as benevolent art philanthropists with great taste from museum walls — had profited from marketing OxyContin as less addictive while suppressing evidence otherwise. Maté marks the moment as another salutary disillusionment.

Her friends in AA advised against going public, warning it would destroy her sobriety. She did it anyway, using her standing as an artist against the family's cultivated reputation as art benefactors, and induced institutions including the Met and NYU's medical school to stop taking Sackler money and remove the name.

Asked why she called it the best choice she ever made: "You need something bigger than yourself. For me what's bigger than myself is other people suffering. And that's a situation I can help rectify... that's my power, that's what I fight about. It helps keep me sober."

**What it demonstrates:** Maté's summary — standing up to a toxic system can help us find a place to stand within ourselves. Healthy anger directed outward at a genuine harm, in solidarity rather than isolation, did what her advisors feared would undo her.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask yourself the disillusionment question directly, about your own beliefs first: illusioned, or disillusioned? Then notice what refusing has been costing.
2. Check your stance before you engage. Reactive is the traumatized personality's default; creative begins with "something is possible here."
3. When assessing an institution, ask whether it cares for the emotional health of the people who staff it. Maté treats a system that traumatizes its own practitioners as structurally incapable of recognizing trauma in those it serves.
4. Trauma-informed does not mean consequence-free. It means replacing purely punitive measures with what actually rehabilitates.
5. Read a child's acting out as a signal, not a misdeed. This is the single most consequential shift Maté asks of education.
6. Activism and advocacy are not add-ons to personal healing; in his framing they complete it by supplying the connection and solidarity that individual work cannot.
7. Be suspicious of anyone certified as impeccably normal by a culture that is itself unwell — Maslow's healthy people were, by that culture's standards, not well-adjusted at all.
