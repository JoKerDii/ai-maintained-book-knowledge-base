# Chapter 28: Before the Body Says No: First Steps on the Return to Self
*Part V: Pathways to Wholeness*

## Core Idea
Major illness is only the latest and loudest summons from parts of ourselves we've lost touch with. **Compassionate Inquiry** — Maté's own method — retrains mind and body to hear the subtler calls first, chiefly by locating the chronic, unwilled "no" we keep swallowing and the authentic "yes" we keep withholding.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Compassionate Inquiry (CI)** — both a professional training taught to thousands of therapists in 80+ countries and a practice of individual self-reflection. Its trainees spend the first three months on their own material, not clients'. *Therapist, heal thyself.*
  - **Inquiry** means an open-ended exploration, and demands humility: allowing, with Socrates, that we don't already know the answer — or better, that we haven't yet found the right questions. Suspend what you believe you know about yourself. Pop psychology mostly delivers **the personality as expert on the subject of itself**; CI is after the deeper knowing. "We are out to know ourselves, not merely to know *about* ourselves."
  - **Compassion** means openness, patience, generosity — the leeway you'd extend a struggling friend to be confused, perplexed, frustrated. Crucially: **compassion is an attitude, not a feeling.** Feelings come and go on their own; attitudes can be invited and generated in the face of any emotional state. The attitude is inexhaustible non-judgment toward whatever is noticed; when self-judgments arise, stay curious about their origin without believing their content.
  - **Never instruct someone to be self-compassionate.** "Compassion brooks no 'should.'" Walled-off parts don't respond to demands — why would they? Instead, bring attention to the *lack* of self-compassion, notice it, get curious about how it shows up. Once seen, it softens, and its origins and present-day impacts become investigable.
  - Everything is a candidate for inquiry, including self-loathing: rather than admonishing yourself for hating yourself, ask why self-hatred arrived in the first place.

- **The four working premises** to hold while practicing:
  a. **Your personality is not you.** Not that it's "false" — clothing isn't true or false — but it doesn't define you. The problem is that it *seems like who we are*.
  b. **The personality is an adaptation**: a jumble of genuine traits and conditioned coping styles, formed by temperament interacting with family, community, culture. It may express not our deepest longings but our compensation for estrangement from them. The aim is not to shed it but to free ourselves from its automatic programming.
  c. **The body keeps the score**: the authentic self can be layered over but never obliterated; it keeps speaking through the body, and we can learn its language.
  d. **This is not personal.** The disconnect from self is endemic in a materialist culture that encourages and then exploits it. You are grappling with a universal dynamic.

- **The "Before the Body Says No" six-question self-inquiry** — done in writing, by hand, in a quiet room, daily or weekly, minimum weekly. Handwriting engages the mind more actively and keeps digital distraction out; it also leaves a record.
  1. **In my life's important areas, what am I not saying no to?** Where did I sense a "no" that wanted expression and convey a "yes" or a silence instead? Get current and stay specific. This is about **chronic patterns, not occasional lapses** — CI in no way stigmatizes genuine altruism; the parent's sleepless night and the friend met in crisis are authentic choices. What's under the lens is *habitual, unwilled* selflessness. Look in the two usual realms, work and personal relationships. Then: With whom and in what situations is it hardest to say no? When I do say it, is it reluctant, apologetic, guilty? Do I beat myself up afterward? Note the world of difference between a considered, conscious "yes" and a compulsive suppression of a "no" — and note that some people's economic survival genuinely depends on saying yes, which is a social problem of vast proportions, not a personal failing. Even so, knowing you are consciously accepting a stressful situation beats doing it automatically.
  2. **How does my inability to say no impact my life?** Three spheres. *Physical*: insomnia, back pain, muscle spasms, dry mouth, frequent colds, digestive trouble, fatigue, headaches, rashes, appetite changes. *Emotional*: sadness, alienation, anxiety, boredom — including deficits, like losing pleasure in what used to bring joy, or a dulling of one's humor. *Interpersonal*: above all **resentment**, which is ironic, since the "no" was suppressed to preserve closeness and the resentment drives you further away — it contaminates the love, and the other person senses the withdrawal in your face, tone, and body. "Resent" comes from *ressentir*, "to feel again" — and again, until we get the memo. A fourth place to look: what do I miss out on materially — fun, spontaneity, self-respect, libido, adventure, growth?
  3. **What bodily signals have I been overlooking?** This reverses direction: *start* from the physical and let it reveal where authenticity went missing. It requires a regular, deliberate body scan. This is the essential backup for people whose self-denial is so normalized that the word "no" doesn't even form in the mind. Take inventory of ongoing symptoms and ask what unsaid "no" they might signal. Note the addiction-like reward loop: dopamine and endorphins flow when others appreciate or benefit from our self-denial. Note too that in people fully identified with a role — doctor, teacher, CEO, supermom — the "no" can't break through the soundproof armor of identity.
  4. **What is the hidden story behind my inability to say no?** **The story** is the narrative, justification, or rationalization that makes the habit seem normal and necessary; we mostly don't know these are stories, we think and act as though they're true. Get past the surface ("you know how my mom is") to the subtextual layer, which is **always about the self, not the circumstances**. If it won't surface, ask: *"What must I believe about myself to deny my own needs this way?"* Even a speculative answer lands close, because our stories, while neither objective nor accurate, are always **internally consistent** with our behavior. Typical stories: saying no means I can't handle it / I have to be good to deserve love / I'm responsible for how others feel / I'm not worthy unless I'm useful / if people knew how I felt they wouldn't like me / it's selfish to say no / it's not loving to have anger.
     - The tell in all of them is a **double standard run in reverse** — "reverse hypocrisy." Ask: would you call a friend "weak" for saying no when that's what felt true? Would you burden anyone else with never disappointing others? Would you tell your child she's worthless unless useful? The answer is always no.
  5. **Where did I learn these stories?** No one is born with a sense of worthlessness; it forms through interactions with caregivers. If their own trauma made them treat us badly, we took it personally; if they were merely stressed or unhappy, we took *that* personally too, even when verbally assured of love. The point of looking back is **not to dwell on the past but to let go of it** — "The moment you know how your suffering came to be, you are already on the path of release from it."
  6. **Where have I ignored or denied the "yes" that wanted to be said?** If stifling a "no" can make us ill, so can withholding an authentic "yes." What have you wanted to make, do, create, or say that you forsook out of perceived duty or fear? What play or exploration did you ignore? What joy did you deny yourself believing you don't deserve it, or fearing it would be snatched away? Then ask, as with the "no": what belief is keeping me from affirming my creative impulses?

## Key Concepts
- **Self-compassion vs. self-improvement**: inquiry into what does not serve us, with no exhortation that we be other than we are — as opposed to a program of correction.
- **The story**: the internally consistent narrative that makes chronic self-denial feel normal and necessary.
- **Reverse hypocrisy**: applying a punishing standard to yourself that you'd never apply to anyone else.
- **Resentment as residue**: things unsaid and feelings not honored, felt corrosively in belly, chest, jaw, neck.
- **Unwilled strength vs. conscious power**: being "the strong one" whom everyone relies on costs the real power of having a say in which burdens you pick up.
- **Vigilance without judgment**: personalities throw up rationalizations when their hold is questioned; "I don't have the time" is the standard and lamest excuse. Most of us lack not time but **intention** for its use.

## Mental Models
- Use **"what must I believe about myself to do this?"** whenever behavior looks irrational from the outside.
- Think of the body as **a language to learn**, not an alarm to silence.
- Treat **resistance to the practice itself** as material: if a few minutes of self-inquiry feels undoable, notice that without judgment and ask where the reluctance comes from — often the vulnerability of possibly being disappointed or pushed past comfort.
- Use the **friend test** on any story you find: would you hold anyone you love to this rule?

## Anti-patterns
- **Coercing, cajoling, or shaming yourself into practice** — "not even ones meant to help you." Shame is the mechanism being dismantled; it cannot be the tool.
- **"You should be more self-compassionate"**: an instruction that defended parts will refuse on principle.
- **Debating the story on its merits**: the work is inquiry into its origins and costs, not argument.
- **Confusing genuine altruism with compulsive selflessness**: CI targets the habitual and unwilled kind only.
- **Treating this as a self-improvement regimen**: the aim is self-retrieval — freeing the personality from automatic programming so genuine strengths have room to stretch out (Almaas: "the personality loses its grip, lets itself just relax").

## Worked Example
Maté's own suppressed "yes": for many years after becoming a doctor he was too caught up in workaholism to attend to his deepest urges. In the rare moments he permitted stillness, he noticed a small fluttering at the pit of his belly, a barely perceptible disturbance, and the faint whisper of one word: *writing*. At first he could not say whether it was heartburn or inspiration. The more he listened, the louder it became — he needed to write, not only so others might hear him but so he could hear himself. Every book since, including this one, came from heeding what in him needed out.

**What it demonstrates:** the withheld "yes" registers first as a bodily signal too faint to name, and the practice is simply listening long enough for it to say its word.

## Key Takeaways
1. Inquire, don't instruct. Every practice here is a question, and the register is that of a friend, not a coach.
2. Bring attention to the *absence* of self-compassion rather than demanding its presence.
3. Distinguish the conscious yes from the compulsive suppression of a no — and don't moralize about people whose economic survival depends on the latter.
4. Work both directions: from unsaid "no" to symptom, and from symptom back to unsaid "no."
5. Find the story by asking what you must believe about yourself; check it against the friend test.
6. Look back to release, not to dwell.
7. Ask what "yes" you're withholding — creativity suppressed is as costly as boundaries unheld.
