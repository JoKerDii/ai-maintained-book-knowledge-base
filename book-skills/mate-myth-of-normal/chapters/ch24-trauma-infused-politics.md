# Chapter 24: We Feel Their Pain: Our Trauma-Infused Politics
*Part IV: The Toxicities of Our Culture*

## Core Idea
At the outermost layer of the biopsychosocial onion, Maté argues that political life runs on unexamined trauma: the wounded elect the wounded, and the traits that carry a person to power are frequently the same early adaptations that blunt empathy and fear vulnerability — with the result that leaders enact policies entrenching the very conditions that traumatize. He is explicit that this is his observation, not a provable finding: social psychology does not yield the firm conclusions of the physical sciences, so he offers examples and research and asks readers to look for themselves.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The political character**: the counterpart to *social character* (the traits that dispose people to function smoothly within a given system). Maté's claim: a society like ours demands of its leaders a set of dispositions without which their careers would never begin. The traits most suited to stewarding a system that traumatizes populations as a matter of course will be ones that inure their bearer to vital aspects of emotional life, or disable the compassion circuitry outright. This always starts with the self, early in life.
  - When to use: when analyzing why leaders across ostensibly opposed parties converge on similar policy dispositions.
  - How: read biography for early emotional conditions; read present behavior for the adaptations those conditions would predict; then ask what policies such a character would find easy to enact and what suffering it would find easy not to see.
  - Failure mode Maté flags on himself: this is speculative. He notes plainly of Trudeau, "I am speculating, I'll admit — neither Trudeau nor any politician has ever sought me out for counseling."

- **Reading energetic fuel, not only content**: Maté's clinical method transposed to the political sphere. In trauma work he observes that it is not merely the ideas people hold but the emotional resonance of how they speak and act — of *who and how they are being* — that reveals their inner life. Address the content without attending to the fuel and you miss the mark.
  - When to use: in polarized conversations where argument has stopped working.
  - How: ask what the intensity is made of before adjudicating the claim. Maté applies this symmetrically: phantasmagorical vaccine paranoia is not the same as healthy skepticism, *and* contemptuous, self-righteous scorn toward vaccine or lockdown dissenters is not responsible citizenship either.
  - Why it is hard: it requires suspending one's own certainty of rightness — which, Maté says, is yet more reason to take it on.

- **The Nation as Family (George Lakoff), extended to unmet needs**: "We all think with a largely unconscious metaphor: the Nation as Family." Maté's extension is that millions of people unconsciously look to leaders to fulfill their own unmet childhood needs.
  - How it presents on the authoritarian side: Daniel Siegel's account to Maté — people may feel excitement that someone in the public eye is expressing aggression or assertion, the opposite of impotence. "It's like a child wanting to be with a parent that will protect them. There is a sense of 'I'm going to be safe and everything is going to be okay.'" Maté names this a **sense memory**: an indelible, usually unexplored imprint from childhood, stored in the bodymind and activated by present-day insecurities projected into politics.
  - How it presents on the liberal side: idealizing leaders as kind, supportive, caring, and inclusive is another form of displaced longing for attuned parenting. Maté's illustration is the tweet captioning a Biden–Harris photo, "G'night, Mom and Dad. See you in the morning." People under such childlike idealizations, even half in jest, are liable to ignore discomfiting counterevidence.

- **Childhood harshness predicts political orientation (Michael Milburn)**: Milburn's finding, related to Maté, that the harsher the parenting people experienced as young children, the more prone they are to support authoritarian or aggressive policies — foreign wars, punitive laws, capital punishment. The effect was strongest among males who had experienced high levels of physical punishment *and had never had psychotherapy*. Milburn's gloss on that qualifier: psychotherapy speaks to a potential for self-examination and self-reflection.

## Key Concepts
- **Political character**: the dispositional profile a traumatizing system selects for in its leadership.
- **Headline stress disorder**: a term coined by a D.C.-area psychologist for the malaise of the governed. Maté pairs it with a Nebraska–Lincoln study finding many Americans believe politics has harmed their physical health and cost them friendships, and with Elissa Epel's suggestion that political stress may shorten telomeres.
- **Sense memory**: an unexplored bodily imprint from childhood, reactivated by present insecurity and projected onto public figures.
- **Alice Miller's thesis**: that harsh German child-rearing practices helped prepare the template for Nazi authoritarianism, and that the leading figures of the Third Reich had, without exception she could find, strict and rigid upbringings. Maté substitutes "traumatizing" for "strict and rigid," specifying that Miller meant environments imprinting a fear-tinged worldview or requiring the child to go numb before suffering, starting with his own.
- **Coldheartedness**: the psychopathy-adjacent trait on which, per a 2016 *Scientific American* analysis Maté cites, both Trump and Clinton scored in the upper quintile.
- **"Second nature is nobody's real nature"**: Maté's response to Tony Schwartz's account of Trump's relationship to truth. There are plenty of congenial liars but no congenital ones; lying, in his reading, first insulated Trump from devastating childhood rejection and later served him in politics.
- **Pop culture as grooming for spectatorship**: Maté's claim that hero worship and emotional projection in showbiz run on fuel distilled largely from trauma, training a passive, spectator-like engagement with politics.

## Mental Models
- **Use the symmetry test.** Maté deliberately pairs nemeses — Harper with Trudeau, Trump with Clinton — because the framework only holds if it applies to the side you favor. His summary of Trump and Clinton, borrowing David Brooks's observation that both hew to a distrustful, stark, combative, zero-sum view in which vulnerability is dangerous: "a match made in childhood suffering."
- **Think of "resilience" narratives as candidates for repression.** Maté's sharpest reading is of the campaign video in which Clinton recounts her mother sending her, terrified at four, back outside to face bullies with "There is no room for cowards in this house." That, he argues, is not a call to resilience but to repression — the message being that vulnerability is shameful, there is no room for your fear, suck up your feelings, expect no empathy. His point is less about Clinton than about the audience: no commentator registered that this handpicked example of inspiring parenting was a public celebration of trauma. A little girl seeking a parent's embrace is not a coward; she is a normal four-year-old.
- **Read the biographies of admired leaders for what "overcoming" cost.** Maté's generalization from reading leaders across countries and periods: each had an emotionally starved childhood and each "overcame" it by means of the very personality qualities that landed them in history books — traits still widely regarded as laudable and worthy of emulation. "That's as normal as normal can get."
- **Treat social maladies as symptoms of the body politic.** If disease is the individual body's way of signalling something out of joint, then addiction, climate catastrophe, and the cynicism surrounding politics are signs of something amiss at the collective level.

## Anti-patterns
- **Debating content while ignoring the emotional fuel**: the political version of treating symptoms while ignoring context. If we want to understand why individuals and groups believe and behave as they do — and Maté says we should want to, if we care about consequences — we have to be willing to see the traumatic scars beneath extreme reactions.
- **Individual coping as a sufficient response**: Maté grants that filtering news consumption, listening better, practising more empathy across disagreement, and a mindfulness regimen would all be salutary. They would also be not enough. Something is going on beneath the lamented "hyperpartisanship" and "polarization."
- **Contempt as politics**: Maté reads Clinton's "basket of deplorables" as revealing to a wide swath of America what they already knew — that many urban elites view them with smug contempt as people whose economic, political, and moral grievances can be ignored. The retort came that November.
- **Idealizing leaders as parents**: on either side, it produces a supporter who cannot process disconfirming evidence.
- **Consuming wounded people as entertainment**: the pop-culture machine treats the flame-outs, the revealed predations, the child stars turned objectified symbols as sobering interruptions — brief solemnity before the gawking resumes. Maté: we venerate the personalities that cover up pathological suffering, then express surprise when things go awry.

## Worked Example
Maté's illustration of a culture that would rather mythologize than empathize is Aretha Franklin. Her sister Erma said, "Aretha is a woman who suffers mightily but doesn't like to show it" — though Maté notes she *did* display it to anyone with eyes to notice. In the 1972 concert documentary *Amazing Grace*, a thirty-year-old Franklin commands the church with thrilling force and depth of feeling. The mask slips only when her preacher father takes the pulpit to praise her gifts: in the presence of this emotionally cruel patriarch she stiffens, her face a mix of practiced deference and involuntary dissociation, as if not quite in the body that had been channelling transcendence minutes earlier. The singer of "Respect" had been more than disrespected in childhood and continued to suffer abuse in adult relationships.

**What it demonstrates:** the wound is visible on camera, in real time, in a beloved public figure — and the culture watched without seeing, because averting our gaze from the pain is what keeps the magic intact. The same blindness operates when we watch leaders.

## Key Takeaways
1. Apply the trauma lens symmetrically or not at all — starting with the figures you admire.
2. Before arguing content, read the emotional fuel. What is the intensity made of, and what does it protect?
3. Treat "resilience" stories that describe a child being denied comfort as accounts of repression, and notice who applauds them.
4. Watch for parental projection in your own political attachments — protection-longing on one side, attuned-caregiver longing on the other. Both suppress counterevidence.
5. Recognize that self-examination is the variable Milburn's data pointed to. The capacity to look inward is what interrupts the transmission of early harshness into political aggression.
6. Hold the whole of this chapter as Maté's interpretation. He says outright he cannot prove it, offers it as observation supported by examples and available research, and asks readers to look for themselves.
7. The cycle he describes runs: mistaken beliefs about human nature → conditions that frustrate basic needs → a populace in pain and disconnected → the elevation of those primed to deny reality and block empathy → policies that keep conditions as they were, or worse. Interrupting it, in his closing formulation, requires doing what many leaders constitutionally cannot — looking within bravely, the better to look out and around honestly.
