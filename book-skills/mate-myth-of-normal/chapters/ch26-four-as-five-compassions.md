# Chapter 26: Four A's and Five Compassions: Some Healing Principles
*Part V: Pathways to Wholeness*

## Core Idea
Healing cannot be plotted for another person, but its terrain can be described. Four qualities — **Authenticity, Agency, healthy Anger, Acceptance** — each correspond to a human need that was stunted or driven underground early in life; healing means welcoming each back and letting it teach us its ways. Five levels of compassion orient the journey.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Four A's** (devised in *When the Body Says No*, since condensed from seven to four): *not* how-to steps or injunctions — guideposts describing natural laws of healing.
  - When to use: as a diagnostic lens on where a person's wholeness is unlived, and as a slow reintegration project — never as a checklist to complete.
  - How: identify which A is most absent in a given domain of life; inquire into what conditions banished it; make room for it to re-emerge on its own timing. "You cannot force nature / only nurture it" (Jewel).

  1. **Authenticity** — cannot be pursued, only embodied; striving toward an idealized self-image is by definition incompatible with being who one is. The most direct approach is **noticing when it isn't there**. Its absence announces itself as tension, anxiety, irritability, regret, depression, or fatigue. Then inquire: Is there an inner guidance I am defying or avoiding? What truth am I withholding out of fear of losing security or belonging? Where did I abandon myself in a recent encounter? Do I even know my own values? The growing ability to say "Ouch, that hurts" or "I didn't really mean what I just said" is the impulse strengthening. Eventually the noticing moves *before* the self-betrayal — and the appearance of a genuine choice where an automatic program used to run is the sign the authentic self is back online.

  2. **Agency** — "the capacity to freely take responsibility for our existence," exercising **"response ability"** in decisions that affect our lives. Deprivation of agency is a stressor, whether social (poverty, injustice, marginalization) or, in illness, internal. Agency is *not* omnipotence or control over circumstance, and it is *not* reflexive opposition — knee-jerk reactivity leaves no room for response flexibility. It is "a self-bestowal of the right to evaluate things freely and fully," choosing from authentic gut feeling, deferring neither to the world's expectations nor to ingrained conditioning. Often it starts by renegotiating our relationship with the personality traits that once kept us safe and now keep us boxed in.

  3. **Healthy Anger** — what it is *not*: blind rage, bluster, resentment, spite, venom, bile. Those come from unexpressed or unintegrated emotion needing to be *experienced and understood* rather than acted out; anger suppressed and anger amplified are both toxic. Healthy anger is a **boundary defense** — activated when physical or emotional integrity is threatened (Panksepp's RAGE system), essential for survival, like an animal protecting its young. It is **a response of the moment, not a beast kept in the basement** fed on shame and self-justifying narrative: situational, limited in duration, flashing up, doing its job, subsiding. Its core message is "a concise and potent *no*, said as forcefully as the moment demands." It carries no moral content; its only desire is to maintain integrity and equilibrium. Where you find yourself explaining away persistent stress — "it's not so bad," "I can handle it," "I don't want to make a fuss" — there is room for anger. Don't idealize or perform it; even "I don't like this" is a step.

  4. **Acceptance** — "allowing things to be as they are, however they are"; the ever-accurate recognition that **in this moment** things cannot be other than how they are. It has nothing to do with complacency or resignation, which sometimes pose as it ("it is what it is"). We abstain from both rejecting and condoning. Acceptance also means accepting how hard acceptance is: it excludes no aspect of experience — **not even the impulse to reject how it is**. Anger, sadness, resistance, hatred all get to say their piece; underneath them there is always pain. Healthy grief is often the jewel hidden inside ossified grievance.
  - **Critical distinction — accepting vs. tolerating**: being with something and putting up with something have almost nothing in common. Acceptance is vitalizing and makes room for the other three A's. Tolerating the intolerable — resigning oneself to abuse or neglect — is deadening, because it *rejects* the parts of self, needs, and values that deserve safeguarding. Socially: accepting that racism or poverty *is* happening is not conceding that it *should*; recognition is the precondition for healthy anger and agency in action.

- **The Five Compassions** — five levels distinguished in Maté's clinical and training work, building on and reinforcing one another **nonhierarchically**.
  1. **Ordinary human compassion** — from the Latin "to suffer with": the ability to *be with* suffering and to be *moved* by it, not to register it as neutral fact. Depletable ("compassion fatigue") and recoverable. Its absence is always a marker of a wound — "the suppression of hurt" (A. H. Almaas). When you notice an empathy gap in yourself, ask what pain of your own you haven't yet metabolized. Distinguish from **pity**, which requires casting the other as beneath you and empowers no one; and self-compassion from **self-pity**, which takes solace in being a beleaguered character. Self-compassion "doesn't resist how things are, nor swaddle the pain in layers of narrative gauze; it just says, 'I am hurting.'"
  2. **The compassion of curiosity and understanding** (the compassion of context) — everything exists for a reason and the reason matters. Ask, without judgment, why any person or group ended up this way, *especially* when they vex you. **Seek the why before leaping to the how.** Without context you are left with inert good wishes and well-meant but ineffective interventions — Maté's charge against current addiction treatment.
  3. **The compassion of recognition** — perceiving that we are all in the same boat. Withholding it costs both parties: you enter the tense physiology of judgment; they get triggered into shame and further isolation. Next time you feel intense judgment, check your chest, belly, throat — it is not pleasant, and not healthy for you. The move is not to stop judging (the automatic mind does that) nor to judge yourself for judging, but to inquire into the judging mind with compassionate curiosity.
  4. **The compassion of truth** — pain is not the enemy; pain is inherently compassionate, alerting us to what is amiss. Shielding people from the inevitable hurts of life is futile, counterproductive, and often inauthentic — arising from our own discomfort with our woundedness. Healing is in part "unlearning the notion that we need to protect ourselves from our own pain." But truth without safety does no healing work: "Only when compassion is present do people allow themselves to see the truth" (Almaas). Being blunt and calling it honesty is not this.
  5. **The compassion of possibility** — there is more to each of us than the conditioned personality, the acted-out emotions, the visible behaviors. Not "maybe someday," but a present, ever-available quality, kin to wonder, awe, mystery, imagination. It keeps the door open on the potential for wholeness that can never be lost, in ourselves and in the people we have written off.

## Key Concepts
- **Response ability**: the capacity to respond rather than react; impaired by trauma, restored as response flexibility.
- **Ersatz authenticity**: performed "realness" — the marketed version, with too much bluster in the delivery.
- **Bogus agency**: personal choice sold as a brand ("Be all you can be"), with no attention to the context in which choices are made.
- **Boundary defense**: anger's natural function — protection of physical or emotional integrity.
- **Grief inside grievance**: mourning is the energy that wants to move; forestalling it makes it build.
- **Compassion fatigue vs. empathy gap**: the first is depletion and recovers with rest; the second is a signal about your own unfelt pain.

## Mental Models
- Use **"is this acceptance or is this tolerating?"** whenever someone reports being "at peace" with a harmful situation. If crucial needs and values are being rejected to sustain it, it is tolerating.
- Think of the four A's as **capacities returning**, not tasks completing — you are noticing their absence, not scoring their presence.
- Use **anger's one-word test**: if it isn't reducible to a clear "no" about something happening now, it's stored grievance, not healthy anger.
- Think of the compassions as **nonhierarchical and mutually reinforcing** — you can enter the ring at any of the five.

## Anti-patterns
- **Making the A's a program**: rigid injunctions and how-to steps are precisely what Maté disclaims; healing "cannot be mandated or hastened."
- **Venting as "getting the anger out"**: stoking resentment and rehearsing grievance is the opposite of healthy anger.
- **Resignation dressed as acceptance**: "it is what it is" applied to abuse; stubborn egotism dressed as authenticity.
- **The compliant patient**: Kelly Turner's finding that the "good" patient who follows orders without question fares worse than the "annoying" one who asks questions and challenges — her radical-remission survivors wished they had become active agents of their destinies far earlier.
- **Truth-dumping**: "I'm just being honest!" without safety.
- **Pity as social policy**: it requires an imagined perch and empowers nobody.

## Worked Example
Darlene, a 38-year-old family therapist raised in a fundamentalist Christian home, had truly believed her God-given duty was to "accept" — meaning endure — the miseries her husband's own traumatic imprints visited on her. Only when she developed an autoimmune disease did the connection between her stress and her illness dawn on her: "Holy shit — I've been in this kind of martyr-honoring-God position of staying in this abusive marriage, and there's just no way: this is going to kill me!" What she had called acceptance was tolerating the intolerable, and it had cost her needs, values, and integrity. Real acceptance of the actual state of her marriage opened the door to anger, agency, and departure.

**What it demonstrates:** counterfeit acceptance is not a milder version of the real thing — it runs in the opposite direction, and the body may be the one to say so.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose by absence: tension, irritability, fatigue, and regret are authenticity's absence announcing itself. Inquire there.
2. Ask "where did I abandon myself?" after difficult encounters, and expect the noticing to migrate earlier over time.
3. Treat healthy anger as an in-the-moment boundary "no," never as venting or grievance; look for it wherever you are saying "it's not so bad."
4. Test any claimed acceptance against the accepting/tolerating line before endorsing it.
5. Seek the why before the how — compassion without context yields good wishes and bad interventions.
6. Never pair truth with pressure: without safety the truth cannot do its work.
7. Maté explicitly rejects reading any of this as "you caused your illness." The A's describe qualities a toxic culture suppresses; naming them is not assigning blame.
