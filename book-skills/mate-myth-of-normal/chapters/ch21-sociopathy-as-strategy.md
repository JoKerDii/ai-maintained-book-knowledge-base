# Chapter 21: They Just Don't Care If It Kills You: Sociopathy as Strategy
*Part IV: The Toxicities of Our Culture*

## Core Idea
Traits we classify as pathological in individuals — absence of conscience, indifference to others' suffering, no compulsion to comply with social norms, no guilt or remorse — are, in Maté's reading, functional and rewarded *strategies* at the top of our economic order. Industries built on engineering addiction into the brain are not aberrations from the system's logic; they are faithful expressions of it.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Pleasure vs. happiness (Rob Lustig)**: Pleasure is "This feels good, I want more." Happiness is "This feels good, I am contented, I am complete."
  - When to use: to distinguish what a product or behavior actually delivers from what it advertises.
  - How: run the neurochemistry. Pleasure runs on dopamine (incentive/motivation) and opiates (reward), both short-burst systems. Contentment runs on the slower, steadier serotonin apparatus. All addictions commandeer the dopamine and/or opiate systems; it is very hard to get addicted to serotonergic substances or behaviors.
  - Why it works commercially: pleasure in the absence of contentment, especially sought as instant gratification, is addictive and therefore profitable. **Contentment sells no products.** True happiness, being a non-commodity, does not make itself obsolete. Don Draper's line — happiness is the moment before you need more happiness — describes the manufactured kind.
  - Failure mode for the practitioner: using this to shame people for seeking pleasure. Maté's aim is to locate the design intent, not to indict the consumer.

- **Neuromarketing as engineered undermining of free will**: corporations hiring neuroscientists and using fMRI to target the brain's pleasure and reward circuits.
  - How it operates, in Maté's account: the "bliss spot" work Michael Moss documented in the food industry (the exact blend of salt, sugar, and fat that most excites the pleasure centers) dampens the prefrontal cortex's capacity to override craving while ratcheting up the lower emotional circuits' capacity to subvert rational thought. Maté means this neurochemically, not rhetorically: the brain is deregulated the way financial markets are deregulated.
  - When to use: whenever "personal responsibility" is invoked to explain a population-level consumption pattern that was deliberately engineered.

- **The corporation assessed as a person (Joel Bakan)**: since US law has treated corporations as "persons" since the late 1800s, Bakan applied standard mental-health criteria to them and found many meet the criteria for sociopathy — acting without conscience, not caring what happens to others as a consequence of their actions, no compulsion to comply with social or legal norms, no guilt or remorse.
  - How to apply: assess behavior, not stated values. Look at the record of concealment, the differential treatment of harms, and what happens when profit and health conflict.
  - Bakan's revision, which Maté highlights: he once imagined corporations as unhealthy life-forms plaguing a basically healthy democratic society. He no longer believes that — "the pathology has metastasized, the pathogen has infected the host."

- **Sociopathy as strategy, not illness (Steven Reisner)**: "In today's America, narcissism and sociopathy are *strategies*. And they're very successful strategies, especially in business and politics and entertainment." Maté names the inverse assumption — that these traits go against the grain — **the myth of abnormal**. Truer to say they *are* the grain.

## Key Concepts
- **Conspiracy realism**: Maté's distinction, via Edward Snowden — the greatest conspiracies are open and notorious, expressed through law, policy, government, technology, and finance. Not theories but practices. This is offered against, not alongside, credulous conspiracy theorizing.
- **The bliss spot**: the engineered salt/sugar/fat ratio calibrated to maximize compulsive consumption.
- **Neuromarketing**: the applied science of targeting consumer reward circuitry.
- **Biological embedding of profit**: sugar-laden foods with high glycemic index activate the same brain regions as cocaine or heroin on fMRI, per a Harvard Medical School study Maté cites.
- **Coca-colonization**: the export of addictive food and drink products to poorer countries where regulation is laxer and governments more pliant.
- **Differential consequence**: Kristof's formulation — hook fifteen people on opioids and you are a thug; hook a hundred and fifty thousand and you are a marketing genius deserving a bonus. Maté sets this against his Downtown Eastside patients imprisoned for selling ounces.
- **Obesity as socially engendered**: Schrecker and Bambra's finding that the timing and international spread of the obesity epidemic mirror the rise and diffusion of neoliberalism.
- **The cancer analogy**: a cell multiplying at the expense of the organism, destroying nearby tissue, spreading, robbing the host of energy, disabling its defenses. Maté applies this to a system "rigged against life."

## Mental Models
- **Use "it's not what you're eating, it's what's eating you" when addressing compulsive consumption.** Maté treats compulsive overeating like all addictions — a response to stress and a way of soothing trauma. Stress itself depletes the serotonin/contentment circuits and shifts the brain toward short-term dopamine-driven pleasure, which is precisely the state the industry monetizes.
- **Think of manipulation and dislocation as multiplicative, not alternative.** Maté is careful: not all food- or tobacco-related illness is directly attributable to corporate mind-hacking. Truer to say the manipulation *is made possible* by the stresses, disconnections, and dislocations that globalized capitalism entrenches.
- **When outrage fails to produce structural change, treat the passivity itself as data.** Maté reads the absence of long-term pushback as evidence of the effectiveness of elite control and of the social character this culture inculcates.
- **Assess institutions by behavior over stated ethics — including philanthropy.** The Sackler name embossed on hospitals, medical schools, and museums is, in Maté's account, part of the pattern rather than a counterweight to it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating obesity, addiction, or diet-related disease as failures of willpower**: this is the individualizing move Maté attacks throughout Part IV. It ignores both engineered craving and the stress that made the person susceptible.
- **A public-health campaign that names the behavior but not the conditions**: Maté's example is Boris Johnson's post-COVID weight-loss push — spot-on as far as it went, but silent on poverty and ethnicity as morbidity risk factors, and silent on the austerity and laissez-faire policies under which obesity rose.
- **Confusing conspiracy realism with conspiracy theory**: Maté grants that nonsensical theories take hold among the credulous and enraged, while insisting the underlying fear of being manipulated is downright sensible. Collapsing the two discredits legitimate inquiry.
- **Expecting the villains to know they are villains**: Milton Friedman, Maté notes, was not a mustache-twirling movie villain but a theorist who believed to his bones that minimally regulated corporate capitalism is what's best for everyone — and whose standing in "normal" circles is the point.

## Worked Example
The endocrinologist Rob Lustig tells Maté why his specialty burns out: "Increasingly, we look after people who don't get better. It's like we're ladling water out of the proverbial leaky boat with a teaspoon while it keeps pouring in through a gaping hole at the bottom." Lustig's subspecialty is children, among whom obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular markers previously seen only in adults have escalated for decades.

The gaping hole, in Lustig's account, is an unregulated industry that hires neuroscientists and uses fMRI machines to target reward circuitry — "their aim is to market happiness in a bottle." Maté extends the arithmetic: eleven million deaths worldwide in 2017 attributable to diets low in vegetables, seeds, and nuts and laden with salt, fat, and sugar; Mexico's overweight rate rising from a fifth of the population in 1996 to about 73%, with diabetes deaths exceeding those of the drug wars.

Lustig's closing line, delivered in mock reassurance: "It's not that they want you to die. They only want your money. They just don't care if it kills you."

**What it demonstrates:** the clinician's futility is structural. Treating the individual downstream of an industry engineered to produce the illness is a teaspoon against a breach — which is why Maté insists the analysis must run to the level where the hole was drilled.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask what a product is engineered to do to the reward system before asking why a person can't stop using it.
2. Distinguish pleasure from contentment in any account of "what makes people happy" — the two run on different chemistry and only one is sellable.
3. Read stress as a precondition for exploitability: stress depletes serotonin and shifts the brain toward dopamine-seeking, which is exactly what the market is positioned to supply.
4. Judge institutions by their record of concealment and their behavior when profit and health conflict, not by their philanthropy or their mission statements.
5. Keep conspiracy realism and conspiracy theory sharply separate — the credibility of the former depends on it.
6. Notice differential consequence. Who is imprisoned for a harm and who is fined for a vastly larger version of the same harm tells you what the system actually values.
