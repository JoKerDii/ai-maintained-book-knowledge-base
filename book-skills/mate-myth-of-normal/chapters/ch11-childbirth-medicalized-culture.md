# Chapter 11: What Choice Do I Have? Childbirth in a Medicalized Culture
*Part II: The Distortion of Human Development*

## Core Idea
Birth is not a mechanical extraction but a major developmental threshold that sets the template for the mother-infant relationship — and a medical culture built on distrust of natural processes routinely overrides it, producing what midwives now have to call obstetrical trauma. The determining variables are not which type of birth occurred but whether the woman had autonomy, and whether she felt safe and supported.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Autonomy and safety as the two determinants of birth experience**: Maté's central claim here is deliberately not about method. He declines to advocate for "natural" birth or to judge any woman's individual choices. What distinguishes a birth a woman can cherish from one that traumatizes her is (1) whether she was in charge of decisions about her own body, and (2) whether she felt safe, heard, and supported by the people in the room.
  - When to use: assessing or preparing for any birth, and understanding a postpartum presentation that doesn't match the medical record's "uncomplicated" note.
  - How: (1) Ask who made the decisions, and whether the woman understood them. (2) Ask whether she felt she could have said no. (3) Ask whether anyone looked her in the eyes and was fully present. (4) Do *not* infer from the presence of intervention that harm occurred, or from its absence that it didn't.
  - Why it works: it explains the two contrasting cases in the chapter — one woman coerced into surgery she never needed and left with PTSD-like symptoms, another who ended in surgery after thirty-six hours and remains elated — with the same variable. Stanger-Ross's research summary agrees: the women reporting the most positive experiences are those who understood all the decisions and had a say in making them, and that holds even for complicated births with multiple interventions and births that end in surgery.

- **Labor as preparation for life**: Mammalian labor is more than expelling an infant from a womb. As Nature designed it, it promotes release of estrogen, oxytocin, and prolactin, which activate neural systems governing the emotions and behaviors that ensure the baby's well-being — warmth, nurturing, bonding, protection. Birth prepares the template for the mother-infant relationship, which is itself the central locus of the child's early development.
  - Practical corollaries, now research-validated but once heretical: routine episiotomy is unnecessary in most labors; supine delivery with feet in stirrups is not required; barring complications, the newborn is best handed to the mother skin-to-skin rather than poked under bright lights with suction tubes; and the cord can be left to complete its pulsations, delivering more oxygen-carrying red cells to the infant.

- **Medicine as Nature's attendant, not its ruler** (Odent): the proposal that surgical teams be at the service of midwives and women rather than controlling them — Maté calls it a radical reinterpretation of "attending physician."

## Key Concepts
- **Obstetrical trauma**: a term now in use (Stanger-Ross) for birth experienced as traumatizing — with consequences for the parent-child relationship that follows. If the birth was traumatizing, how does that translate when you now have a newborn in your arms?
- **Intervention as default**: procedures that in a sane system would be deployed only to reduce risk and ensure survival have become standard. C-sections are the clearest case: roughly 10–15% of deliveries optimally end that way; British Columbia approaches 40%, as do many other places, and worldwide the number doubled between 2000 and 2015. The *Lancet*'s 2018 global survey found the excess came "without significant maternal or perinatal benefits," with markedly high use among low-obstetric-risk births and more educated women. Even ACOG raised significant concern about overuse in 2014.
- **The defective-body script** (Sarah Buckley): doctors are the agents of society's expectations, imprinted on mothers when they are open and vulnerable — that technology is superior to the body and that women's bodies are intrinsically bound to fail. And it carries forward into how she brings up the child to accord with the demands of the culture.
- **Trained distrust of natural processes** (Michael Klein): the paradigm dominating medical training "sees birth as nothing more than an accident waiting to happen"; the women are "unexploded bombs that need defusing."
- **The self-perpetuating cycle**: a body seizes up in the absence of safety and emotional connection, especially under sensitizing hormones. Bright lights, machine noise, and hectoring personnel therefore instigate many of the labor complications hospitals then intervene to resolve.
- **VBAC**: vaginal birth after cesarean, its safety long documented and the feared uterine rupture shown to be negligible — yet "once a cesarean, always a cesarean" persists as practice, a measure of how deep indoctrination runs.
- **Inherent expectation at birth** (Liedloff): a birth without trauma consists of those experiences, and only those, corresponding to the infant's and mother's ancient expectations. We invite birth trauma with steel instruments, bright lights, rubber gloves, antiseptic and anesthetic smells, loud voices, and machinery — while every other mammal seeks dark, quiet, solitary places.

## Mental Models
- **Use "was she in charge?" rather than "what kind of birth was it?"** The method is not the variable; the agency is.
- **Think of birth practices as a readout of a culture's power arrangements.** Who wields power, and how much genuine control people exercise over their own bodies, shows up here more nakedly than almost anywhere. Rich's framing: real choices require understanding the power and powerlessness embodied in motherhood in patriarchal culture.
- **Treat the maternal protective instinct as data, not sentiment.** Odent's image: try picking up a mother gorilla's newborn and you'll understand maternal protective aggression. In our civilization we have suppressed that instinct for a long time.
- **Assume the vulnerability gradient runs steeply downward.** A 2019 WHO report found 42% of women in a global survey experienced physical or verbal abuse or discrimination during childbirth in health centers — punched, slapped, shouted at, mocked, forcibly held down. Nor is this confined to poorer countries; Maté cites a cell-phone video of Quebec hospital staff taunting an Indigenous woman in labor, calling her stupid and saying she'd be better off dead. She died minutes later.

## Anti-patterns
- **Pathologizing a normal process**: by treating birth as inherently pathological, present-day practice contradicts both the wisdom of the body and, frequently, its own commitments to evidence and to "first, do no harm."
- **Reducing women to passive recipients of care**: dehumanizing in the literal sense — it disrupts physiological, hormonal, and psychological processes evolved over millions of years to ensure mother-infant bonding.
- **Suppressing innate knowledge in favor of protocol**: Maté performed routine episiotomies on nearly a thousand deliveries because it was what he'd been taught, and had to be told by illicitly practicing midwives that there was an organic process trying to happen without him.
- **Protocol by category rather than by person**: induction decreed at thirty-nine weeks because the patient is over thirty-five, growth scans asserted as authoritative against the woman's own accurate knowledge that they're notoriously poor at predicting weight.
- **Throwing out the achievements with the critique**: modern obstetrics has spared many women and infants from avoidable suffering and death. We need not abandon it to honor traditional wisdom; we can embrace both.

## Worked Example
Sherri Dolman, thirty-four, had a C-section as a frightened teenager during a slow labor. Three years of trying later, pregnant with a daughter, she resolved on a vaginal birth, interviewed as many doctors as she could, and heard from all of them: "Once a cesarean, always a cesarean" — several would take her as a patient only on the condition of a scheduled surgery. They were medically off base; a high-risk perinatal specialist who scanned her uterus confirmed her rupture risk was no greater than if she had never been pregnant. The one doctor who agreed to support her got cold feet at the last moment. After a routine fetal-monitoring session showing no abnormality, she was physically barred from leaving the hospital, threatened with arrest, and browbeaten into surgery. Afterward: "a version of PTSD," unable to function, feeling like a failure as a mother, disconnected from her daughter, crying herself to sleep every night for the first year.

Her third pregnancy ended in a vaginal birth under midwife care. She called it very, very painful and among the most amazing and exhilarating experiences of her life — "no matter how painful it got, I had support and I was in control of my own body." A fourth followed, a nine-pound boy, also vaginal, also in hospital with a midwife, five years after ten board-certified obstetricians had told her not to attempt it.

Set against Danielle, an anesthesiology resident who planned a water birth in a rented forest cottage and instead got hospitalization, an epidural, oxytocin, thirty-six hours of labor and finally a cesarean — and remains elated. "I was listened to. Everyone made time to hear what I was concerned about... she looked me in the eyes, and she was fully present. I just felt safe with everyone in there."

**What it demonstrates:** the birth that traumatized and the birth that healed were both surgical. What differed was whether the woman was a decision-maker or an obstacle to one.

## Key Takeaways
1. Assess birth experience by autonomy and felt safety, not by intervention count. A cesarean chosen and understood is a different event from a cesarean imposed.
2. Ask about birth when a postpartum bond seems hard to form. Obstetrical trauma has downstream effects on the parent-child relationship that nobody may have named.
3. Notice that fear of what can go wrong is trained, not neutral — and that it manufactures some of the complications it then treats.
4. Support the woman's own knowledge against categorical protocol. Courtney knew growth scans predict weight poorly, said so, was overruled, and was right — the baby came in under eight pounds against a predicted nine-plus.
5. Restore midwifery as a structural correction, not a lifestyle preference. Courtney's own reason for not considering one — "I'm not that left-wing... I completely bought into the system" — is itself the myth of normal talking.
6. Protect the sensory environment of labor. Silence, dim light, and an attuned presence are physiological requirements, not amenities.
7. Hold Stanger-Ross's frame: approach someone in labor as a full person experiencing a sacred life passage. They are not a sick patient; they are a person in labor, which is a very normal thing to be.
