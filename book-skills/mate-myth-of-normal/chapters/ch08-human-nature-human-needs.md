# Chapter 8: Who Are We Really? Human Nature, Human Needs
*Part II: The Distortion of Human Development*

## Core Idea
There is no fixed human nature to appeal to — our nature is a range of possible outcomes, and which version of us appears depends on whether the environment meets a set of wired-in needs laid down over evolutionary time. The question worth asking is not "what are people really like?" but "what circumstances evoke which sorts of outcomes?"

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Inherent expectation** (Jean Liedloff): all life develops as an expectation for its environment. Lungs are an expectation for oxygen; cells for water and nutrients; ears for the vibration of sound waves. An inherent expectation is a wired-in need that, when denied, disturbs physical and psychological equilibrium and produces worse outcomes — physically, mentally, socially. Liedloff's rule: to know what is correct for any species, you must know the inherent expectations of that species.
  - When to use: whenever you're evaluating whether an arrangement — a workplace, a school, a birth practice, a childcare setup — is *healthy* rather than merely normal or legal.
  - How: (1) Identify what the organism arrived expecting, based on the conditions under which it evolved. (2) Compare against what the current environment actually supplies. (3) Read the gap as a predicted health cost, not as a failure of the individual to cope. (4) Ask what change to the environment closes the gap — not what change to the person.
  - Why it works: it gives an evolutionary standard against which to measure culture, which otherwise has no external referent and simply grades itself. Failure mode: romanticizing the ancestral past. Maté is explicit that there was violence and bad behavior and that we were never "perfect" — the claim is about what conditions our physiology was tuned by, not about a lost Eden.

- **Neural expectancy for reciprocity** (Stephen Porges): being "well met" is not a social nicety but a nervous-system requirement. When it's absent, the brain may process the non-response as an assault — a threat to safety.
  - How to see it: notice the involuntary physiology, not the opinion. You greet a sullen cashier; before you form any judgment, your body tightens, heart rate climbs, breathing shallows, irritation arrives.

## Key Concepts
- **Human nature as a range of outcomes**: Sapolsky's formulation — "the nature of our nature is not to be particularly constrained by our nature."
- **Genetically determined not to be genetically determined**: the biological restatement of the same point; genes are inert, and their expression depends on environment and experience.
- **Wétiko**: Cree term (with Ojibwa and Powhatan variants) for a spirit or mindset of greed and domination that cannibalizes people — a *loss* of humanity rather than an expression of it. The Quechua *pishtako* is a parallel.
- **Species-atypical** (Darcia Narvaez): the condition of a modern capitalist society in which humans forsake their own needs and convince themselves this is how things ought to be — something no other species is able to do.
- **Authentic self-interest**: enhancing one's connection and membership in the community, to everyone's benefit — the traditional reading, distinct from the suspicious, competitive stance now called self-interest.
- **Conflating how we're being with who we are**: the reification error — treating behavior evoked by particular conditions as fixed identity, which then licenses reproducing those conditions.
- **The acorn**: it is in an acorn's nature to become an oak — given the right soil, climate, water, light, spacing, and no enterprising squirrel. Potential is conditional.

## Mental Models
- **Use "what circumstances evoke which outcomes?" whenever you catch yourself or someone else saying "it's human nature."** Alfie Kohn's observation is the tell: we only ever invoke human nature to excuse the unsavory. Nobody dismisses an act of generosity as *just* human nature.
- **Think of civilization as a poor gauge of what we need.** It covers roughly 5% of our existence as a species and under 1% of the human genus. The needs were set in a very different crucible — small-band hunter-gatherer life, which is where the nervous system was tuned.
- **Use the acorn frame when someone asks about potential**: the seed carries the pattern; the environment decides whether and how far it manifests. This is true of a child's development and of a culture's.
- **Read a culture's account of human nature as self-justification.** If what's normal is assumed to be natural, the norm endures. Materialistic cultures generate myths of selfish, dominant striving as baseline behavior, which then legitimizes the arrangements that produced them.

## Anti-patterns
- **Genetic determinism**: attributing human behavior to genetic programming. We have a biological makeup; we are not programmed to feel, believe, or act in any particular manner, and we are freer from genetics than any other species on earth.
- **Reifying the present as the permanent**: mistaking the behavior a given place and time evokes for what humans irreducibly are. It forecloses alternatives even when the current arrangement is demonstrably bad for us.
- **Debating fixed human nature at all**: Maté finds the question unhelpful and misleading. Jesus was human and so was Hitler; we are noble and narcissistic, generous and genocidal, ingenious and idiotic. All of the above.
- **Treating adaptability as an unmixed good**: our talent for adjusting to almost any environment is also the liability that lets us survive in settings where wellness becomes a luxury for the lucky rather than a widely available norm.

## Worked Example
You walk into a corner store, pick a candy bar, smile at the cashier and say hello. He's having a bad day — a toothache, a family crisis, a playoff loss — and he looks at you sullenly if at all, grunts, takes your money and hands back change brusquely. Nothing has been done to you. No resource has been withheld. Yet your body tightens, your heart rate rises, your breathing shallows, and you are irritated — perhaps, depending on your state, imagining bad things happening to him. The transaction was completed correctly and efficiently. Only the reciprocity was missing.

**What it demonstrates:** connection is not a preference layered on top of our needs — it is one of them, and its absence registers in the body as threat before any thought about it occurs.

## Key Takeaways
1. When assessing whether an arrangement is healthy, ask what it was designed for and what humans arrived expecting. The gap between the two is the predicted cost.
2. Refuse the framing that people are fundamentally aggressive and acquisitive — and notice who benefits from that framing being treated as settled.
3. Treat "normal" and "natural" as separate categories. Conflating them is the mechanism by which unhealthy conditions become permanent.
4. Locate the intervention in the environment, not the individual. If our nature is influenceable, changing conditions is the leverage point.
5. Assume the baseline our nature *wants* is caring, relative harmony, and equilibrium — not that our nature *is* those things. When present, we thrive; when denied, we suffer.
6. Expect the following chapters to argue that today's culture distorts development from conception onward, producing a "normal" that is, measured against the needs and evolutionary history of the species, utterly aberrant.
