# Patterns & Practices — The Myth of Normal

Maté's actual working methods. These are inquiries, not instructions. None of them is a substitute for medical, psychiatric, or therapeutic care, and none is a self-improvement program — the aim is **self-retrieval**, not self-betterment.

---

## Compassionate Inquiry (CI)

**When to use**: whenever a symptom, behavior, or self-judgment resists being argued away — chronic illness, addiction, self-loathing, compulsive people-pleasing. Also as an ongoing self-reflection practice.

**How**:
1. **Suspend what you already know about yourself.** The personality is an expert on itself and that is precisely the problem. Aim to *know* yourself, not to know *about* yourself.
2. **Inquire, don't interrogate.** An inquiry is open-ended; it starts from the possibility that you haven't yet found the right question.
3. **Bring compassion as an attitude, not a feeling.** Feelings come and go; an attitude can be invited in any emotional state. The attitude is inexhaustible non-judgment toward whatever surfaces.
4. **Never issue a "should."** Compassion brooks no should, and walled-off parts don't respond to demands. If self-compassion is absent, don't manufacture it — get curious about its *absence*. Once seen, it softens.
5. **Track the body.** Ask where the belief lives as sensation (throat, chest, belly), then ask: *is this sensation new, or deeply familiar?* The answer is almost always "familiar" — which relocates the material from the present grievance to its origin.
6. **When self-judgment arises, stay curious about its origin without believing its content.**

**Trade-offs**: slow, and it surfaces pain before relief. Done without safety or without genuine non-judgment it becomes another form of self-attack. Maté insists CI participants spend months on their own material before using it with anyone else.

## The Self-Inquiry Exercise ("Before the Body Says No")

**When to use**: as a weekly written practice, especially where chronic self-suppression is suspected. Handwritten, undistracted.

**How** — six questions, in order:
1. In my life's important areas, what am I **not saying no to**? (Where did a "no" want out and a "yes" or a silence came instead?)
2. How does my inability to say no **impact my life**?
3. What **bodily signals** have I been overlooking? What symptoms have I ignored that could be warning signs?
4. What is the **hidden story** behind my inability to say no? (The belief that makes the "no" feel impossible.)
5. **Where did I learn** these stories?
6. Where have I ignored or denied the **"yes"** that wanted to be said?

**Trade-offs**: Maté is explicit that this is not about stigmatizing genuine altruism. Deliberate, chosen self-sacrifice — a parent up at night with a sick child — is not the target. The target is *habitual, unwilled* selflessness that operates without consent. Get current, stay specific, look for chronic patterns rather than lapses. Notice reluctance to do the exercise at all, without judgment — that reluctance is itself data.

## The Authenticity Audit

**When to use**: when tension, anxiety, irritability, regret, depression, or fatigue surfaces. Maté treats these as the signal that authenticity is missing, not as the problem itself.

**How** — ask:
- Is there an inner guidance I am defying, resisting, ignoring, or avoiding?
- Are there truths I'm withholding from expression or even contemplation, out of fear of losing security or belonging?
- In a recent encounter, is there some way I abandoned myself, my needs, my values?
- What fears, rationalizations, or familiar narratives kept me from being myself?
- Do I even know what my own values are?

**Trade-offs**: authenticity can't be pursued, only embodied — striving toward an idealized authentic self is itself inauthentic. The most direct route is noticing where authenticity *isn't*, then applying curiosity and gentle skepticism to the limiting beliefs standing in its place. And the cost is real: choosing authenticity may not preserve every attachment. That grief is the adult reversal of the childhood trade, and Maté does not minimize it.

## Foes to Friends — Working with the Inner Critic

**When to use**: self-accusation, self-condemnation, guilt, self-loathing — anything you have been trying to get rid of.

**How**:
1. Start from the first principle of compassionate self-inquiry: **everything within us exists for a purpose; nothing is there that shouldn't be.** However debilitating, it was once functional.
2. **Shift the question** from "How do I get rid of this?" to **"What is this for? Why is this here?"**
3. Externalize the accusation to make it examinable. Maté's courtroom move: *If this were a trial and you were the defendant, what would the prosecution say about you?* Then: *What's the verdict — because you did those things, that makes you what?* This exposes that the person is simultaneously plaintiff, prosecutor, accused, and hanging judge.
4. Locate the verdict in the body, then ask how **familiar** the sensation and the self-concept are. Familiarity dates the belief to childhood, where it was protective.
5. Do not judge yourself for judging — that keeps the wheel of shame turning.

**Trade-offs**: turning a foe into a friend is not the same as agreeing with it. The aim is that the critic relax and stop hounding, not that it be defeated.

## The Five Compassions

**When to use**: as a diagnostic on the quality of your own regard — for a patient, a person you judge, or yourself. Non-hierarchical; they reinforce one another.

1. **Ordinary human compassion** — the ability to *be with* suffering and to be *moved* by it. Distinct from pity, which requires unequal roles. Self-compassion says "I am hurting"; self-pity wraps the pain in narrative.
2. **The compassion of curiosity and understanding** (the compassion of *context*) — everything exists for a reason and the reason matters. Seek the *why* before leaping to the *how*.
3. **The compassion of recognition** — we are all in the same boat. When intense judgment arises, check your own body state; inquire into the judging mind rather than obeying or condemning it.
4. **The compassion of truth** — pain is not the enemy; it is information. Shielding people from inevitable hurt is neither kind nor effective. But truth without safety does no healing work, and "I'm just being honest" is not compassion.
5. **The compassion of possibility** — there is more to a person than their conditioned personality and their behavior. A present quality, not a someday hope.

## The A's of Healing

**When to use**: as guideposts for the territory, explicitly *not* as how-to steps. Maté: no one can plot another's course of healing.

| A | What it is | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| **Authenticity** | Fidelity to one's own felt sense and values; lived, not conceptual | Performed "realness"; an idealized self to strive toward |
| **Agency** | Free, responsible authorship of one's own life and responses | Control over outcomes; blame for what happened to you |
| **Healthy Anger** | Boundary-defending energy of the moment — flares, does its job of fending off threat, subsides | Rage, bluster, resentment, spite, venting, or a grievance kept and fed. Both suppressed and amplified anger are toxic |
| **Acceptance** | Allowing that reality is as it is right now — the precondition for change | Resignation, approval, or ceasing to work for change |

**Plus two, for collective healing** (Ch 33): **Activism** and **Advocacy** — socially meaningful syntheses of the first four, adding solidarity and collective thinking. These cannot be individual pursuits.

**Trade-offs**: each A corresponds to a human need that was likely driven underground early. Welcoming it back is slow and often frightening. Treating the four as a checklist to complete inverts their purpose.

## Disillusionment as a Healing Step

**When to use**: whenever an illusion is protecting you from a truth about your history, your relationships, or your society.

**How**: treat losing an illusion as a **gain, not a loss**. Ask which you would rather be — deluded or disillusioned. Expect the ache of loss and the sense of being unmoored first; what follows is the relief of no longer having to hold impossible contradictions together. Disillusionment also confronts denial, which is the main buttress of an unhealthy status quo.

**Trade-offs**: it does carry a real cost — you may have to relinquish something you valued or a perspective you took refuge in. Maté's point is that the cost of *refusing* is higher and less visible.
