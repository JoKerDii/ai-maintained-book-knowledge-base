# Glossary — The Myth of Normal

**Acceptance** — Allowing that reality is as it is right now; the precondition for change, not resignation or approval. One of the four A's. (Ch 26)

**ACEs (Adverse Childhood Experiences)** — The ten categories of childhood adversity from the Fellitti/Anda study; dose-dependently associated with adult physical and mental illness. Maté treats them as a floor, not a ceiling — they capture overt harm and miss the absence of attunement. (Ch 1, 9)

**Activism / Advocacy** — The two additional A's for collective healing: organizing for change, and using privilege to amplify silenced voices. Cannot be individual pursuits. (Ch 33)

**Agency** — Free, responsible authorship of one's own responses and life. Not control over outcomes, and not blame for what was done to you. (Ch 26)

**Anger, healthy** — Boundary-defending energy of the moment: it flares, fends off the threat, and subsides. Distinct from rage, resentment, spite, or venting. Both suppressed and amplified anger are toxic. (Ch 26)

**Attachment** — The drive for proximity and connection with a caregiver; in a child, a survival need that outranks everything else. (Ch 7, 9)

**Attachment vs. authenticity** — The central developmental trade-off: when a child cannot have both, attachment wins, authenticity is suppressed, and that adaptation becomes the adult pathology. (Ch 7)

**Attunement** — The caregiver's accurate reading of and resonance with a child's inner state. Its chronic absence is itself traumatic, even where nothing overtly bad happens. (Ch 9)

**Authenticity** — Fidelity to one's own felt sense, needs, and values; lived rather than conceptual. Cannot be pursued, only embodied. (Ch 7, 26)

**Big-T Trauma** — Trauma from overt, identifiable catastrophic events: abuse, violence, war, disaster. (Ch 1)

**Biopsychosocial** — Medicine that treats biological, psychological, and social factors as one inseparable system rather than separate domains. (Ch 2, 3)

**Cartesian split** — The mind/body separation Maté rejects; the philosophical error underwriting modern medicine's blindness to context. (Ch 2)

**Compassionate Inquiry (CI)** — Maté's own methodology: open-ended, non-judgmental exploration of the beliefs and behaviors that don't serve us, tracking bodily sensation and familiarity to reach origins. Both a therapist training and a self-reflection practice. (Ch 28, 30)

**Compassions, the five** — Ordinary human compassion; the compassion of curiosity and understanding (context); of recognition; of truth; of possibility. Non-hierarchical and mutually reinforcing. (Ch 26)

**Coping style** — Habitual emotional patterns — self-suppression, compulsive niceness, repressed anger, chronic role-taking — that Maté's position links to autoimmune illness and cancer risk. A contested empirical claim, not settled consensus. (Ch 2, 5, 6)

**Cure** — The absence of disease. Distinct from healing; either can occur without the other. (Ch 25)

**Developmental trauma** — Trauma arising from the conditions of early development itself — unmet needs, absent attunement, stressed caregivers — rather than from discrete events. (Ch 1, 9)

**Disillusionment** — Shedding an illusion. Maté treats it as a healing gain rather than a loss, and as the confrontation with denial that makes social change thinkable. (Ch 33)

**Epigenetics** — The mechanism by which environment and experience switch gene expression on or off; Maté's counter to genetic determinism. (Ch 4, 10)

**Foes to friends** — The move of turning a distressing inner pattern from enemy into ally by asking what purpose it originally served. (Ch 30)

**Healing** — A natural movement toward wholeness. A direction, not a destination; closer to **self-retrieval** than to self-improvement. (Ch 25)

**Idiopathic** — Medicine's term for a condition of unknown cause; in Maté's reading, often a confession that no one asked about the life. (Ch 6)

**Inner critic** — The internalized self-accusing voice. Approached not by defeat but by inquiry into what it was once protecting. (Ch 30)

**Interpersonal biology** — The finding that human physiology is regulated by relationship: nervous systems, immune systems, and hormones are shaped by the people around us. (Ch 3)

**Interpersonal neurobiology** — Siegel's field describing how relationships physically shape the developing brain. (Ch 3, 9)

**Mind-body unity** — The premise that emotional, psychological, and social life are inseparable from physiology; therefore the social is always also biological. (Ch 2)

**Myth of normal, the** — The book's title claim: a toxic culture normalizes conditions that are in fact pathogenic, so widespread does not mean healthy and "normal" is not a standard of health. (Introduction, Ch 19–24)

**"Not why the addiction, but why the pain"** — Maté's reframe of addiction: the substance or behavior is an adaptation that solved something, so the question is what pain it relieved. (Ch 15, 16)

**Peer orientation** — Children turning to peers rather than adults for attachment cues, undermining the parenting relationship. (Ch 12, after Gordon Neufeld)

**Responsibility vs. blame** — Maté's key distinction: responsibility is the capacity to respond now; blame is fault for what happened. He explicitly rejects the "you caused your cancer" reading of his work. (Ch 6, 25, 27)

**Rupture and repair** — Standard attachment-theory terminology for the cycle of relational break and reconnection: repair, not the absence of rupture, builds security, and unrepaired rupture is where damage accumulates. Maté does not use this exact phrase in this book; Ch 9 makes the same argument as unconditional acceptance holding through correction and momentary anger. He uses **rupture** separately, in his definition of trauma as a lasting rupture or split within the self. (Ch 9; cf. Ch 1)

**Self-compassion** — The attitude of non-judgment toward one's own suffering; says simply "I am hurting." Distinct from self-pity, which wraps pain in narrative, and from self-improvement. (Ch 26, 28)

**Self-suppression** — The habitual, unwilled subordination of one's own needs and feelings to others'. Maté's central pathogenic coping style. (Ch 2, 5, 28)

**small-t trauma** — Trauma from chronic unmet need and absent attunement rather than overt catastrophe. Pervasive, easily missed, and consequential. (Ch 1)

**Sociopathy as strategy** — Maté's argument that institutional and corporate structures reward and require the traits that would be pathologized in an individual. (Ch 21)

**Toxic culture** — The social, economic, and material conditions that systematically frustrate human needs while presenting themselves as normal. (Ch 19–24)

**Trauma** — Not what happens to you, but what happens *inside* you as a result — the wound and the adaptations built around it, not the event. (Ch 1)

**Wholeness** — The state toward which healing moves: recovering the parts of self that were exiled to preserve attachment. (Ch 25, 32)
