# Patterns & Techniques — Deep Work (Cal Newport)

## Choose a Depth Philosophy
**When to use**: Setting up how deep work fits your life; do this before any scheduling tactic.
**How**: Pick the one matching your constraints — Monastic (eliminate shallow entirely), Bimodal (deep in day/season blocks), Rhythmic (same time daily, keep a streak), Journalistic (any open gap, on demand).
**Trade-offs**: Monastic needs a job that tolerates it; Journalistic needs pre-trained focus. Rhythmic is the safe default for most jobs. A mismatch derails the habit.

## Deep Work Ritual
**When to use**: Every deep session, to spend minimal willpower starting.
**How**: Pre-decide (1) *where* and for *how long*; (2) *how* you'll work (rules — no internet; metrics — words/20min); (3) *how* you'll support it (coffee, food, walk, tidy space).
**Trade-offs**: Feels mechanical, but that's the point — remove in-the-moment decisions that leak willpower.

## 4DX for Individual Depth
**When to use**: To translate "work more deeply" into a driven, tracked habit.
**How**: (1) Set a small number of *wildly important* goals; (2) track the *lead measure* — deep-work hours — not lag outcomes; (3) keep a *compelling scoreboard* (visible tally, circle hours that produced results); (4) run a weekly *cadence of accountability* review.
**Trade-offs**: Requires honest daily tracking; the scoreboard only motivates if you actually confront it.

## Shutdown Ritual
**When to use**: End of every workday.
**How**: Algorithm — check inbox for anything urgent → capture loose tasks into master lists → skim every list + next few days' calendar → make a rough plan for tomorrow → say a closing phrase ("Shutdown complete").
**Trade-offs**: Takes 10–15 min; defeats the Zeigarnik effect so evenings truly restore attention.

## Grand Gesture
**When to use**: A high-stakes project that deserves a jolt of importance.
**How**: Make a conspicuous investment/change of environment (a hotel room, a trip, a paid retreat) that raises the psychological stakes and mobilizes focus.
**Trade-offs**: Costs money/effort; overuse dilutes the effect. Reserve for projects that warrant it.

## Hub-and-Spoke Collaboration
**When to use**: When ideas benefit from exchange but execution needs isolation.
**How**: Spend time in shared "hub" spaces for serendipitous encounters, then retreat to isolated "spoke" spaces to work deeply on what surfaced. The whiteboard effect: working *with* someone on a hard problem can push depth further.
**Trade-offs**: Pure isolation misses cross-pollination; pure openness kills focus. Alternate deliberately.

## Scheduled Internet Blocks (Embrace Boredom)
**When to use**: Default operating mode to rebuild concentration.
**How**: Schedule the blocks when internet use is allowed; stay fully offline between them. If you need it sooner, reschedule the *next* block a few minutes out — never jump online impulsively.
**Trade-offs**: Uncomfortable at first; the discomfort *is* the training. Works at home too, not just at work.

## Roosevelt Dash
**When to use**: To stretch your focus ceiling, or to blast through an important task.
**How**: Estimate the time a task needs, set a deadline well *under* it, work at total intensity (no email, no drift) to make it.
**Trade-offs**: Exhausting; use occasionally as interval training, not constantly.

## Productive Meditation
**When to use**: During walks, commutes, showers — physical-but-not-mental time.
**How**: Hold one well-defined work problem in mind; when you notice *distraction* (drift to easier thoughts) or *looping* (rehashing knowns), gently redirect. Structure it: identify variables → determine next-step question → consolidate the gain.
**Trade-offs**: Frustrating early; builds both focus and problem-solving output over time.

## Craftsman Tool Selection + Law of the Vital Few
**When to use**: Deciding whether to keep any network/social tool.
**How**: (1) Name the few high-level goals defining professional and personal success. (2) List the 2–3 key activities supporting each. (3) Keep a tool only if it *substantially* helps those (not "any benefit"). Concentrate on the ~20% of activities driving ~80% of results.
**Trade-offs**: Requires honesty about what actually matters; you'll drop tools that feel socially expected.

## 30-Day Quit (Packing-Party Test)
**When to use**: To measure a tool's real value.
**How**: Quit it quietly for 30 days. Then ask: (1) Would these 30 days have been *notably* better with it? (2) Did anyone *care* I wasn't using it? Readopt only on a clear yes to both.
**Trade-offs**: Mild FOMO; usually reveals the tool was justified only by the any-benefit standard.

## Schedule Every Minute
**When to use**: Every workday, to prevent shallow drift.
**How**: Block the day in ~30-min chunks with an assigned task each; keep an overflow column; when reality derails a block, *rebuild* the remaining schedule rather than abandoning it.
**Trade-offs**: Not about rigid obedience — about staying thoughtful. Expect frequent revision.

## Quantify Depth (College-Grad Heuristic)
**When to use**: To classify any ambiguous task as deep or shallow.
**How**: Ask "How many months to train a smart recent college grad with no field training to do this?" Few months → shallow; years → deep.
**Trade-offs**: A heuristic, not a law; but surprisingly clarifying for borderline tasks.

## Shallow Work Budget + Fixed-Schedule Productivity
**When to use**: To cap shallow work and force prioritization.
**How**: Ask your boss what % of time should be shallow (often 30–50%); defend the budget. Separately, fix a hard workday endpoint and prune obligations backward to fit deep work into fewer hours.
**Trade-offs**: Requires saying no often; the constraint is the mechanism, not a limitation.

## Become Hard to Reach
**When to use**: To reclaim attention from email.
**How**: (1) *Sender filters* — publish expectations that put the burden on the sender; (2) *do more work per email* — send process-centric replies that close the whole loop; (3) *don't respond* to ambiguous/uninteresting/disproportionately costly messages.
**Trade-offs**: Some senders bristle; net attention reclaimed is large. Not for messages where non-response has real cost.
