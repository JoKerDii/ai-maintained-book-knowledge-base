# Chapter 7: Rule #3 — Quit Social Media

## Core Idea
Network tools (social media, entertainment sites) fragment attention and devour the time deep work needs. Stop justifying them with the "any-benefit" mindset and instead evaluate them like a craftsman evaluates tools — by their real net impact on what matters most to you.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Any-Benefit Approach to Network Tool Selection (the trap)**: "You're justified in using a network tool if you can identify *any* possible benefit to its use, or *anything* you might possibly miss out on if you don't use it."
  - Why it fails: Every tool has *some* benefit, so this standard approves everything — while ignoring the (larger, unmeasured) costs to your time and attention.

- **The Craftsman Approach to Tool Selection (the fix)**: "Identify the core factors that determine success and happiness in your professional and personal life. Adopt a tool only if its positive impacts on these factors substantially outweigh its negative impacts."
  - How: (1) List the small number of high-level goals that define a good professional and personal life. (2) For each, list the 2–3 most important activities that support it. (3) Ask whether a given tool has a *substantially positive* effect on those activities — not just *some* effect. Keep only tools that pass.

- **Apply the Law of the Vital Few**: "In many settings, 80 percent of a given effect is due to just 20 percent of the possible causes." A small number of activities produce most of what you care about; low-value activities (most social media) consume time that could serve the vital few.
  - How: Rank your goal-supporting activities by impact and concentrate on the top ~20%; scattering effort across many trivial activities starves the few that matter.

- **The Packing Party / 30-Day Experiment**: To test a tool's real value, quit it (or all social media) for 30 days without announcing it. Then ask two questions: (1) Would the last 30 days have been *notably* better if I'd had it? (2) Did people *care* that I wasn't using it? Only readopt a tool if the answer to both is a clear yes.

- **Don't Use the Internet to Entertain Yourself**: Give your leisure time structure and quality — plan what to do with an evening or weekend, rather than letting the web fill it with low-value scrolling (Arnold Bennett: put "extra" energy into worthy pursuits).

## Key Concepts
- **The cost is hidden**: Network tools' harms (fragmented attention, lost deep-work hours) are diffuse and hard to see; their benefits are salient — which is exactly why the any-benefit mindset wins by default.
- **FOMO as a weak justification**: "Might miss out" is an any-benefit rationalization, not a reason.

## Mental Models
- **Judge tools like a farmer judges a hay baler** (Newport's example): a skilled tradesman weighs cost against real return on the core work — he doesn't adopt every gadget that offers *some* benefit.
- **Ask "does this substantially serve my vital few?"** — if not, drop it, regardless of minor perks.

## Worked Example
Ryan Nicodemus's "packing party": to decide what he actually needed, he packed *all* his possessions into boxes as if moving, then over a week unpacked only the items he genuinely used — after three weeks, ~80% remained boxed and unmissed. Newport applies the same logic to network tools via the **30-day quit**: stop using them quietly, and at the end judge by whether their absence *notably* hurt you and whether anyone *noticed*. Most people find the honest answer is no — revealing that the tools were justified only by the any-benefit standard, not by real value to their vital few.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reject the any-benefit mindset: "some benefit" is not a reason to spend your finite attention.
2. Use the craftsman approach: adopt a tool only if it *substantially* helps your core goals and outweighs its costs.
3. Apply the Law of the Vital Few — concentrate on the ~20% of activities that drive ~80% of results.
4. Run a quiet 30-day quit to test a tool's real value; readopt only on a clear yes to both questions.
5. Don't let the internet be your default leisure — plan quality downtime.

## Anti-patterns
- **"I might miss something" / "it has some networking value"**: textbook any-benefit reasoning that approves any tool.
- **Filling every idle moment with entertainment sites**: trains distraction addiction (Ch 6) and wastes the mind's recovery time.
