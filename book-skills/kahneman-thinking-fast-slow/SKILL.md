## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The two systems (the book's spine — Ch 1–9).** Think of the mind as two agents:
- **System 1** — fast, automatic, effortless, associative, always on. Generates impressions, intuitions, and emotions.
- **System 2** — slow, effortful, deliberate, capacity-limited, and *lazy*. It monitors and can override System 1, but usually just ratifies it.
Most errors are System 1 intuitions that System 2 fails to catch. Debiasing = adding deliberate friction (slow down, ask for the base rate, reframe).

**WYSIATI — "What You See Is All There Is" (Ch 7).** System 1 builds the most coherent story from *available* evidence and ignores what's missing. Coherence, not completeness, drives confidence. Antidote: ask "what would I need to know that I don't?" and check evidence quality/quantity.

**Substitution (Ch 9).** Faced with a hard question ("How happy are you with life?"), we unconsciously answer an easier one ("What's my mood right now?") and don't notice the swap. Most heuristics are substitutions.

**Heuristics & biases (Ch 10–18).** Fast shortcuts that are usually useful but systematically fail:
- **Anchoring** — an arbitrary number pulls estimates toward it (Ch 11).
- **Availability** — judge frequency by ease of recall; vivid events feel common (Ch 12–13).
- **Representativeness** — judge probability by similarity to a stereotype, ignoring base rates and sample size (Ch 14–15); source of the **conjunction fallacy** (Linda).
- **Law of small numbers** — over-trust small samples; see patterns in noise (Ch 10).
- **Regression to the mean** — extremes are followed by less-extreme outcomes; don't invent a cause (Ch 17).

**Overconfidence (Ch 19–24).** We overestimate how much we understand the world:
- **Narrative fallacy** & **hindsight bias** — tidy stories of the past overweight skill and hide luck (Ch 19).
- **Illusion of validity / illusion of skill** — confidence tracks story coherence, not accuracy (Ch 20).
- **Formulas beat experts** in low-validity, noisy environments — because they're consistent (Ch 21).
- **Expert intuition is trustworthy only if** the environment is regular AND there was prolonged practice with rapid feedback (Ch 22).
- **Outside view / reference-class forecasting** beats the **planning fallacy** (Ch 23). The **optimism bias** is the engine of capitalism (Ch 24).

**Prospect theory (Ch 25–34) — how we actually choose under risk:**
- Value is defined over **changes from a reference point**, not final wealth (fixing **Bernoulli's error**).
- **Loss aversion**: losses loom ~1.5–2.5× larger than equal gains (Ch 26, 28) → **endowment effect** (Ch 27), status quo bias.
- **Diminishing sensitivity** and **nonlinear decision weights**: rare events overweighted (**possibility effect**), near-certain outcomes underweighted (**certainty effect**) → the **fourfold pattern** (Ch 29–30).
- **Narrow framing, mental accounting, sunk costs, the disposition effect** distort choices; **broad framing** and **risk policies** repair them (Ch 31–32).
- **Framing effects & preference reversals**: logically equivalent descriptions flip choices (Ch 33–34).

**The two selves (Ch 35–38).** The **experiencing self** lives each moment; the **remembering self** keeps score and decides — using the **peak-end rule** and **duration neglect**. What memory rewards (a good ending) diverges from lived experience. The **focusing illusion**: "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it."

> Practitioner defaults: take the **outside view**, **regress predictions to the mean**, use **simple formulas** over gut in noisy domains, run a **premortem**, **reframe** consequential choices, and adopt **broad-framing risk policies**. See [patterns.md](patterns.md) and [cheatsheet.md](cheatsheet.md).
