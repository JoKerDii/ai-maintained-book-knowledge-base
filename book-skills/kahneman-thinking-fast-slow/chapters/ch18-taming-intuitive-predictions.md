# Chapter 18: Taming Intuitive Predictions

## Core Idea
Intuitive predictions match the extremeness of the prediction to the extremeness of the evidence — they are nonregressive and therefore biased and overconfident. System 1 substitutes an *evaluation of the current evidence* for a *prediction of an uncertain future*, ignoring how weak the evidence's predictive power really is. The fix is a deliberate System 2 correction: anchor on a baseline, then move toward your intuition only as far as the evidence's correlation with the outcome justifies.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four-step correction for intuitive prediction**: A procedure to regress an intuitive numerical prediction toward the mean.
  - When to use: Predicting any quantitative outcome (GPA, firm revenue, company growth) from imperfect evidence, when stakes are high.
  - How: (1) Estimate the average (baseline) outcome. (2) Determine the intuitive prediction that matches your impression of the evidence. (3) Estimate the correlation between evidence and outcome. (4) Move from the baseline toward the intuitive prediction by that fraction of the distance (e.g., 30% for r = .30).
- **Baseline + intuition blending (categorical parallel)**: The same logic used for discrete predictions (Tom W), where the baseline is the base rate and you aim between it and intuition.
  - When to use: Probability judgments about categories or discrete outcomes.
  - How: Start from the base rate, adjust toward intuition by the quality of information; with no useful evidence, stay at the base rate; with perfect confidence, stay at intuition.
- **Substitution/intensity-matching diagnosis**: Recognize when a prediction is really a disguised evaluation.
  - When to use: Whenever a forecast feels as confident as an assessment of present facts.
  - How: Check whether your predicted percentile equals your evidence percentile (e.g., reading precocity = predicted GPA). If so, you have failed to regress.

## Key Concepts
- **Intuitive prediction**: The number that comes to mind, matching the evidence's apparent strength; nonregressive and overconfident.
- **Nonregressive prediction**: A forecast as extreme as its evidence, ignoring regression to the mean, hence biased.
- **Substitution**: Answering the hard prediction question by substituting an easier evaluation of the evidence.
- **Intensity matching**: Translating the evidence's percentile directly onto the outcome scale, producing equal-percentile answers.
- **Baseline prediction**: What you would predict knowing nothing about the case — the average (numerical) or base rate (categorical).
- **Correlation as regression weight**: The fraction of the distance from baseline to intuition you are entitled to move.
- **Unbiased vs. moderate prediction**: A corrected prediction is equally likely to over- or underestimate and never calls rare extremes from weak evidence.

## Mental Models
- Use the four-step correction when a numerical forecast feels obvious: the confident intuition is almost certainly too extreme.
- Use the "prediction = evaluation" check to detect substitution — identical percentiles for evidence and outcome mean zero regression.
- Use the law of small numbers when comparing candidates: the one with less information (Kim) should be regressed more deeply than the one with a longer track record (Jane).
- Use extreme predictions deliberately only when asymmetric payoffs justify them (venture capital hunting the next Google), and stay aware it is a self-indulgence, not accuracy.

## Anti-patterns
- **Matching prediction to evidence**: Predicting Julie's GPA at 3.7-3.8 straight from "read fluently at four," ignoring the weak correlation.
- **Confusing evaluation with prediction**: Officers translating their assessment grades directly into predicted officer-school grades, reproducing the grade distribution nonregressively.
- **Overconfident extreme calls**: Trusting a vivid, coherent impression to predict a rare outcome from weak evidence.
- **Favoring the sparkling small sample**: Choosing the dazzling candidate with little track record over the solid one with a long record, forgetting that small samples yield extreme, luck-driven outcomes.

## Worked Example
Julie read fluently at age four; what is her college GPA? People answer ~3.7-3.8, matching her reading precocity's high percentile directly onto the GPA scale — pure substitution and intensity matching, with no allowance for the weak link between early reading and college grades. The corrected procedure: (1) baseline = average GPA, say ~3.0; (2) intuitive match = ~3.8; (3) estimate the correlation between reading age and GPA, optimistically ~.30; (4) move 30% of the distance from 3.0 toward 3.8, landing near 3.24. The prediction still leans on the evidence but is far more moderate. Kahneman's officer-selection study shows the untamed version: officers' predicted grade distribution was nearly identical to actual school grades — they predicted as if their weak-validity ratings were perfect.

## Key Takeaways
1. Intuitive predictions are nonregressive: as extreme as the evidence, hence biased and overconfident.
2. The mind substitutes evaluation of present evidence for prediction of an uncertain future without noticing.
3. Correct predictions by anchoring on a baseline and moving toward intuition only by the correlation's fraction.
4. The categorical (base-rate) and numerical (average) corrections share the same structure: aim between baseline and intuition.
5. Unbiased predictions rarely call extreme cases and forfeit the "I thought so!" thrill — but their errors are smaller and unbiased.
6. Extreme predictions can be justified only by asymmetric payoffs; otherwise regress deeply, especially with small samples.
