# Chapter 31: Risk Policies

## Core Idea
Because Humans are risk averse for gains and risk seeking for losses, and because they evaluate risky choices one at a time (narrow framing), they routinely accept combinations of choices that are demonstrably inferior. A rational agent frames broadly—considering all related decisions together—and lets statistical aggregation tame the impact of loss aversion. The practical remedy is a **risk policy**: a standing rule applied to a whole class of similar risky decisions, so that no single choice is evaluated in emotional isolation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Broad vs. Narrow framing**: Narrow framing evaluates each decision separately; broad framing bundles related decisions into one comprehensive choice.
  - When to use: Whenever several risky decisions with small stakes (relative to wealth) will be faced over time or simultaneously.
  - How: Ask "Is this one of many similar bets I'll face?" and evaluate the aggregate, not the instance—"you win a few, you lose a few."
- **Risk policy**: A predetermined rule routinely applied to a category of risky choices ("always take the highest deductible," "never buy extended warranties").
  - When to use: Recurring, independent, small-stakes risks with positive expected value.
  - How: Commit in advance to the rule; accept occasional losses as the known cost of a policy that wins over the long run.
- **Outside view + Risk policy combined**: Two broad-frame remedies against opposing biases—optimism (planning fallacy) and excessive caution (loss aversion).
  - When to use: Organizational decisions where both over-optimism and over-caution are present.
  - How: Use the outside view (reference-class statistics) to curb optimism and a risk policy to curb loss aversion; pursue both, don't rely on them canceling.

## Key Concepts
- **Narrow framing**: Treating each decision "as if it were the only one," which lets loss aversion dominate.
- **Broad framing**: Embedding a choice in a portfolio of similar choices, blunting the emotional reaction to any single loss.
- **Loss aversion (as a curse with narrow framing)**: Weighting losses ~2x gains; combined with narrow framing it produces costly over-caution.
- **Samuelson's problem**: A single favorable coin gamble is rejected, yet 100 of them would be foolish to refuse—aggregation reduces subjective risk.
- **Aggregation / bundling**: Combining independent favorable gambles lowers the probability of any loss, shrinking loss aversion's grip.
- **The trader's mantra**: "You win a few, you lose a few"—a device to control the emotional response to individual losses.
- **Aggregated feedback**: Checking outcomes less often (e.g., quarterly) reduces exposure to painful short-term fluctuations and improves decisions and returns.

## Mental Models
- Use broad framing when facing repeated small independent risks—each is trivial relative to wealth, so treat them as a portfolio.
- Use a risk policy when you notice yourself constructing a fresh preference for a familiar type of decision—decide the rule once.
- Use reduced feedback frequency (quarterly, not daily) when loss aversion sabotages investment decisions—less news means less churn and less over-caution.

## Anti-patterns
- **The loss-aversion-plus-narrow-framing curse**: Rejecting favorable small gambles one at a time, paying a premium to avoid sure losses and to lock in sure gains—both from the same pocket.
- **Over-frequent monitoring**: Watching daily price swings, where frequent small losses hurt more than equal small gains, prompting useless portfolio churning.
- **Executives loss-averse in their own domain**: Each manager rationally declines risk in isolation, so the organization as a whole takes far too little risk.

## Worked Example
Kahneman poses paired decisions. Decision i: (A) sure $240 vs. (B) 25% chance of $1,000. Decision ii: (C) sure -$750 vs. (D) 75% chance of -$1,000. Most people pick A (risk averse for gains) and D (risk seeking for losses)—73% chose A&D, only 3% chose B&C. But combining choices reveals the truth: A+D = "25% win $240, 75% lose $760" while B+C = "25% win $250, 75% lose $750." B+C dominates A+D outright. So the popular pair (A&D) is the objectively inferior combination, chosen only because each decision was framed narrowly. Broad framing—viewing all four options at once—would never produce it. Thaler's CEO example mirrors this: 25 divisional managers each refused a favorable double-or-lose bet, but the CEO wanted all 25 taken, counting on aggregation.

## Key Takeaways
1. Broad framing is superior (or never inferior) whenever multiple decisions can be considered together; Humans are natural narrow framers.
2. Every gain/loss choice can be decomposed into combinations that yield inconsistent, inferior preferences—coherence is a "hopeless mirage."
3. Aggregating favorable independent gambles rapidly cuts the probability of loss, so loss aversion's impact shrinks even though the aversion itself never changes.
4. Adopt risk policies (standing rules) so you apply a broad frame automatically instead of reconstructing a preference each time.
5. The mantra "you win a few, you lose a few" works only for independent, non-catastrophic, non-longshot bets.
6. Combine the outside view (against optimism) with a risk policy (against loss aversion)—don't assume the two biases cancel.
