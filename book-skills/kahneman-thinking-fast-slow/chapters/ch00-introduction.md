# Introduction

## Core Idea
The book aims to give you a richer, more precise vocabulary for diagnosing errors of judgment and choice — in others first, eventually in yourself — by mapping the mind as two agents: an automatic, intuition-generating System 1 and a slow, effortful System 2.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **System 1 / System 2 (preview)**: The organizing metaphor of the whole book. System 1 produces fast, automatic intuitive thought (both expert recognition and heuristic shortcuts); System 2 is slower, deliberate, effortful thinking that we identify as our conscious self.
  - When to use: Any time you want to explain why a judgment felt effortless yet turned out wrong, or why an answer required visible mental strain.
  - How: Attribute impressions, intuitions, and impulses to System 1; attribute rule-following, checking, and self-control to System 2.
- **Heuristics-and-biases program**: Intuitive judgment relies on simplifying shortcuts (heuristics); these shortcuts produce predictable, systematic errors (biases) in specified circumstances.
  - When to use: When a judgment recurs the same wrong way across many people — that regularity signals a heuristic at work.
  - How: Identify the hard question being asked, then find the easier question intuition actually answered.
- **Substitution (the essence of intuitive heuristics)**: When faced with a difficult question, we often answer an easier related one instead, usually without noticing.
- **Two sources of intuition: skill vs. heuristics**: Accurate expert intuition comes from prolonged practice (recognition), not from heuristics; heuristics are the fallback when genuine expertise is absent.

## Key Concepts
- **Bias**: A systematic (not random) error that recurs predictably in particular circumstances.
- **Heuristic**: A rule of thumb; a simplifying shortcut for making a difficult judgment.
- **Halo effect**: Tendency to judge everything about a person (or thing) from one salient positive impression — e.g. rating a confident speaker's content more favorably than deserved.
- **Availability heuristic**: Judging the frequency or size of a category by the ease with which instances come to mind.
- **Affect heuristic**: Letting feelings of liking or disliking drive judgments and decisions with little reasoning (e.g. the executive who bought Ford stock because he liked the cars).
- **Intuition as recognition (Herbert Simon)**: Expert intuition is nothing more than a cue giving access to information stored in memory — "the situation has provided a cue; this cue has given the expert access to information."
- **Prospect theory (preview)**: The model of choice under risk Kahneman and Tversky published in 1979, a foundation of behavioral economics.

## Mental Models
- Think of judgment errors as a physician thinks of diseases: you need a large stock of named labels (each binding symptoms, causes, consequences, interventions) before you can diagnose reliably.
- Use "informed gossip" as a motivator: anticipating intelligent criticism from colleagues is a stronger spur to self-correction than private resolutions.
- When an expert gives a confident intuitive answer, ask whether their environment offered enough regular feedback to build true skill — if not, suspect a heuristic or affect substitution.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating all intuition as either magical or worthless**: Both extremes mislead; intuition is recognition when backed by valid practice, and substitution when not.
- **Attributing irrationality to emotion alone**: The 1970s consensus blamed departures from rationality on fear/affection/hatred; Kahneman traces systematic error instead to the ordinary design of cognition.
- **Assuming errors don't apply to you**: It is far easier to spot others' mistakes than your own, which is precisely why self-diagnosis fails without a shared language.

## Worked Example
**Steve the librarian.** A neighbor describes Steve: "shy and withdrawn, invariably helpful but with little interest in people... a meek and tidy soul... a passion for detail." Is Steve more likely a librarian or a farmer? Nearly everyone answers librarian — the personality sketch resembles the stereotype. But there are more than 20 male farmers for every male librarian in the US, so even if only a small fraction of farmers are "meek and tidy," they vastly outnumber the tidy librarians. Participants ignore this base rate entirely and rely on resemblance as a simplifying heuristic — a demonstration of how easily relevant statistical facts are discarded in favor of similarity.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build a labeled vocabulary of biases; you cannot diagnose what you cannot name.
2. When an intuitive answer arrives instantly, suspect you may have answered an easier substitute question — check what was actually asked.
3. Trust expert intuition only where prolonged practice with reliable feedback was possible.
4. Watch for the affect heuristic: liking something is not evidence that it is a good decision.
5. Media coverage inflates the availability of some issues — your sense of "how common" something is is contaminated by what's reported.
6. Accept that these effects apply to you, not just to undergraduates or "other people."
