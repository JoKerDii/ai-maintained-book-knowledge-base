# Chapter 33: Reversals

## Core Idea
The same options can reverse in rank depending on whether they are evaluated one at a time (single evaluation) or side by side (joint evaluation). In single evaluation, System 1's emotional intensity and anchoring dominate; in joint evaluation, System 2 notices comparative features—safety, humanity, number of entries—that were invisible in isolation. Because life is usually lived in single-evaluation (between-subjects) mode, our preferences and even our moral judgments are locally coherent within categories but globally incoherent. Broadening the frame to joint evaluation generally yields more reasonable, defensible decisions.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Joint vs. single evaluation**: Evaluating options together versus in isolation, which can invert preferences.
  - When to use: Any judgment where you want stability and defensibility—or where you suspect an emotional or anchored reaction.
  - How: Deliberately place the case beside relevant comparators; ask which features become decisive only when seen together.
- **Evaluability hypothesis (Hsee)**: An attribute gets weight only if it is "evaluable" on its own; otherwise it is ignored in single evaluation and decisive in joint evaluation.
  - When to use: Judging options with hard-to-interpret numeric attributes (entries, BTUs, specs).
  - How: Provide a comparison so unevaluable attributes (number of dictionary entries) become meaningful.
- **Broaden the frame for coherence**: Prefer joint evaluation to expose inconsistencies—unless a manipulator controls the comparison set.
  - When to use: Institutional judgments (courts, penalties, compensation) that ought to be consistent.
  - How: Present cases in comparative context; beware salespeople who engineer the comparison.

## Key Concepts
- **Preference reversal**: Choosing B over A yet pricing A above B—the classic Lichtenstein–Slovic bet result.
- **Single evaluation**: Between-subjects judgment of one option, driven by System 1 intensity, anchoring, and emotion.
- **Joint evaluation**: Within-subjects comparison invoking System 2's more careful, stable assessment.
- **Categories and norms**: Judgments are coherent within a category (apples vs. peaches) but unstable across categories (apples vs. steak), because each object recruits its own comparison norm.
- **Substitution and intensity matching**: System 1 answers a hard question (dollar value) by translating an easy emotional response (how much I like dolphins) onto the dollar scale.
- **Poignancy**: A counterfactual feeling (cousin of regret) that inflates single-evaluation judgments (higher compensation for the victim shot in the unfamiliar store).
- **Anchoring in single evaluation**: A salient number (a bank's $10M loss, a bet's prize) anchors the isolated judgment but loses force under comparison.
- **Predictable incoherence in justice**: Awards and penalties coherent within a category or agency but absurd across them.

## Mental Models
- Use joint evaluation when you want a judgment to reflect principles rather than raw feeling—seeing scenarios together surfaces the rule (location shouldn't affect compensation).
- Use the category check when comparing items: same-category comparisons are stable; cross-category ones (dolphins vs. farmworkers) flip once a shared decisive feature (human vs. animal) appears.
- Use "what becomes evaluable here?" to predict which attribute will suddenly dominate when options are placed side by side.
- Distrust joint evaluation only when someone controlling the comparison set has a vested interest (salespeople).

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging in isolation and calling it principled**: Awarding by emotional intensity (burned child vs. defrauded bank) without the comparator that would correct it.
- **Letting anchors set values**: Pricing bet A above B because the large prize anchors the isolated valuation, despite choosing B when compared.
- **Institutional single evaluation**: Legal rules that forbid jurors from considering other cases, guaranteeing globally incoherent punitive awards.
- **Cross-category intensity matching**: Donating more to charming dolphins than to farmworkers because each was scored against its own in-group norm.

## Worked Example
Miller and McFarland's compensation scenarios: a man loses the use of his arm in a store robbery. Version (i) he was shot in his regular store; version (ii) his regular store was closed, so he was shot in a store he rarely visited. In joint evaluation, almost everyone agrees location is irrelevant—compensation should be equal, since the injury is identical. But in single evaluation (each participant sees only one version), the victim shot in the unfamiliar store is awarded much more. The counterfactual "if only he'd shopped at his usual store…" comes readily to mind, and poignancy—via substitution and intensity matching—translates the stronger emotion into more dollars. The principle people endorse when comparing is overridden by feeling when they judge alone, and life is mostly lived alone (between-subjects).

## Key Takeaways
1. Preferences and moral judgments reverse between single and joint evaluation because different features become salient.
2. Single evaluation is System 1's turf—emotion, poignancy, anchoring; joint evaluation engages System 2's comparison.
3. Judgments are coherent within categories but incoherent across them; objects recruit their own comparison norms.
4. Unevaluable attributes (Hsee's dictionary entries) get zero weight alone and decisive weight when compared.
5. Broader frames (joint evaluation) generally yield more reasonable, defensible decisions—except when a manipulator controls the comparison.
6. The justice system perversely favors single evaluation (jurors barred from other cases), producing predictable global incoherence.
