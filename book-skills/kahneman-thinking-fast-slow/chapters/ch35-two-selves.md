# Chapter 35: Two Selves

## Core Idea
There are two distinct meanings of "utility." Bentham's **experienced utility** is the actual pleasure or pain lived moment to moment; economists' **decision utility** is "wantability"—what people choose. The rational-agent model assumes they coincide, but they systematically diverge. Behind this split sits a deeper one: the **experiencing self** (which answers "Does it hurt now?") versus the **remembering self** (which answers "How was it, on the whole?"). Memory is what we keep, so the remembering self keeps score and makes decisions—yet it evaluates episodes by rules that ignore what the experiencing self actually underwent. This is the "tyranny of the remembering self."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Peak-end rule**: The retrospective rating of an episode is well predicted by the average of pain/pleasure at its worst (or best) moment and at its end.
  - When to use: Predicting or shaping how an episode (medical procedure, meeting, trip) will be remembered and chosen again.
  - How: Identify the peak intensity and the final moment; average them. Improve memory by lowering the peak or ending on a mild/upward note.
- **Duration neglect**: The length of an episode has essentially no effect on its remembered value.
  - When to use: Diagnosing why a longer, objectively worse experience is preferred to a shorter one.
  - How: Notice that memory stores a representative moment (a prototype), not a sum; expect duration to drop out of retrospective judgments.
- **Hedonimeter total (duration-weighted measure)**: The "area under the curve" of momentary experience—every moment weighted equally by its intensity and length.
  - When to use: When you want the true experienced cost/benefit rather than the remembered one.
  - How: Integrate reported momentary intensity over time; two minutes at level 9 counts twice one minute at level 9.

## Key Concepts
- **Experienced utility**: Bentham's pain-and-pleasure sense—what is actually felt.
- **Decision utility**: The "wantability" that governs choice; the subject of expected utility theory.
- **Experiencing self**: The part that does the living, answering "Does it hurt now?" It has no voice.
- **Remembering self**: The part that keeps score, tells stories, and makes decisions; often wrong but always in charge.
- **The cold-hand (cold-pressor) experiment**: Immersion of a hand in painfully cold water to compare experienced vs. remembered pain.
- **Less-is-more effect**: Adding a mildly better (but still bad) tail can lower the remembered value, because System 1 represents sets by averages, not sums.
- **Diminishing sensitivity**: The source of error in the injections puzzle—18 vs. 20 feels smaller than 6 vs. 4.

## Mental Models
- Use the peak-end rule when you want to predict which of two experiences a person will *choose to repeat*—choice tracks memory, not total suffering.
- Use the duration-weighted (hedonimeter) view when an *objective observer* must decide on someone's behalf, favoring the experiencing self.
- Use "System 1 stores prototypes, not sums" to explain less-is-more, the Linda conjunction, and cold-hand choices as one phenomenon.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confusing experience with the memory of it**: Believing a scratched ending "ruined the whole experience" when only the memory was harmed—40 minutes of bliss already happened and cannot be undone.
- **Letting the remembering self alone decide painful trade-offs**: The cold-hand subjects chose 30 extra seconds of needless pain because the longer episode ended milder—a plain mistake, not masochism.
- **Optimizing for memory when experience is what matters**: E.g., prolonging a procedure to soften its ending, adding real suffering to buy a better recollection.

## Worked Example
Two colonoscopy patients rate momentary pain (0–10) every 60 seconds. Patient A's procedure lasts 8 minutes; patient B's lasts 24. B spends at least as much time as A at every pain level, so B's hedonimeter total (area under the curve) is clearly worse. But both peaked at 8, and A's final reading was 7 while B's was 1. Peak-end averages: A = (8+7)/2 = 7.5; B = (8+1)/2 = 4.5. So A—who objectively suffered less—retains the far worse memory, purely because his procedure ended at a bad moment. The remembering self ranks B's ordeal as better; the experiencing self ranks A's as better. Memory and experience disagree.

## Key Takeaways
1. Decision utility (what we choose) and experienced utility (what we feel) systematically diverge.
2. Retrospective evaluations obey the peak-end rule and duration neglect, ignoring how long suffering or pleasure actually lasted.
3. System 1 represents episodes by a prototypical moment (an average), not by a sum—hence less-is-more effects.
4. We identify with the remembering self; the experiencing self, which does our living, is treated almost like a stranger.
5. Because memory neglects duration, our choices can leave us with worse actual experience—a built-in inconsistency challenging the rational-agent model.
