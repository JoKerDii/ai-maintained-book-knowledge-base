# Chapter 36: Life as a Story

## Core Idea
The remembering self does not merely rate episodes—it composes stories and keeps them for future reference. Stories are about significant events and memorable moments, not about time passing, so **duration neglect** and the **peak-end rule** govern how we judge entire lives, not just colonoscopies. We care intensely about the *narrative* of a life—our own and others'—and about how it ends, even when the ending cannot affect anyone's actual feelings. Because we identify with the remembering self, the experiencing self that does our living becomes "like a stranger," and choices about vacations, love, and even life itself are made to maximize memories rather than lived experience.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Life-as-a-story evaluation (peak-end + duration neglect applied to whole lives)**: We judge a life by its peaks, its emotional arc, and especially its ending, giving little or no weight to how long the good parts lasted.
  - When to use: Diagnosing intuitions about "a good life," reconciliations, legacies, or "wasted" years.
  - How: Ask what ending or peak the intuition is anchored on; test whether adding duration (more happy years) actually changes the judgment—usually it does not.
- **The amnesic-vacation thought experiment**: Imagine all photos destroyed and a potion erasing every memory of a trip. Ask how much you would still pay for it.
  - When to use: Revealing how little you value the experiencing self relative to the remembering self.
  - How: If the trip loses most of its value—or you would skip it entirely—you are choosing for memory, not for lived enjoyment.

## Key Concepts
- **Narrative of a life**: The story we compose about a person's existence; caring for people often means caring for the quality of their story, not their feelings.
- **Duration neglect (in stories)**: The length of an episode or a life drops out of the evaluation; only significant moments count.
- **Peak-end rule (in stories)**: The ending often defines a story's whole character (La Traviata; the estranged mother reconciled at death).
- **Less-is-more effect**: Adding pleasant-but-lesser years to a very happy life *lowers* its judged total happiness—an average substituting for a sum.
- **Memory tourism**: Vacations chosen and experienced to store memories; the photographer designs a future memory instead of savoring the moment.
- **Remembering self as chooser**: Intentions to repeat an experience are set by the final/global evaluation, even when it misrepresents the diary record.

## Mental Models
- Use "life is a story, not a sum of moments" when someone judges a marriage, career, or life by how it ended rather than by its lived arc.
- Use the amnesic-vacation test when deciding whether an activity serves your experiencing self or only your remembering self—then choose deliberately.
- Use "labor and vacation objections are really about the *end state*" (more depleted after 24h labor; more rested after 6 days) to see that duration seems to matter only via how the episode ends.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging an entire life by its final chapter**: Diener's Jen study—doubling a happy life's length changed nothing, and appending 5 "slightly happy" years made the whole life seem worse. Reasonable adults, not just students, showed this.
- **Devoting an experience to memory-construction**: Photographing a view you never savor; treating the present only as raw material for a future recollection.
- **Assuming duration truly matters in episodes**: It usually matters only because a longer episode changes the quality of the end.

## Worked Example
Ed Diener and his students described "Jen," a happy, childless, never-married woman who dies painlessly in a car crash. Across versions her life lasted 30 or 60 years; another version appended 5 years that were pleasant but less happy. Between subjects, doubling Jen's lifespan had *no effect* on judged desirability or total happiness—her life was represented by a prototypical slice, not a sum of time. Adding the 5 slightly-happy years produced a *less-is-more* drop in total-happiness ratings. When Kahneman pushed for a within-subject test (each participant judging both), he expected sober people to resist—but the intuition that the extra good years made the life *worse* was overwhelming. Peaks and ends matter; duration does not.

## Key Takeaways
1. The remembering self composes and stores stories, so duration neglect and the peak-end rule govern judgments of whole lives.
2. We care about the narrative of a life—including endings that change nothing about anyone's felt experience.
3. The Jen studies show doubling a happy life's length changes nothing, while adding lesser-but-pleasant years can lower its judged worth (less-is-more).
4. Much of tourism and love is memory-construction; the amnesic-vacation test exposes how little we value the experiencing self.
5. "I am my remembering self, and the experiencing self, who does my living, is like a stranger to me."
