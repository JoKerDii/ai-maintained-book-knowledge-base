# Chapter 20: The Illusion of Validity

## Core Idea
Subjective confidence is a feeling produced by the coherence of the story System 1 constructs, not a reasoned estimate of accuracy. Because of WYSIATI and confidence-by-coherence, we hold confident beliefs and make confident predictions even when we know statistically that our forecasts are near-worthless. This is the illusion of validity — and it persists even after we intellectually accept evidence of our own failure, because cognitive illusions are more stubborn than visual ones.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Illusion of validity**: Unjustified confidence in a prediction, sustained by the coherence of the evidence even when track records show the prediction has little or no validity.
  - When to use: Any high-confidence judgment about a person's future performance, a stock, or a political outcome.
  - How: Separate the feeling of confidence from the actual predictive record. Ask whether the environment is predictable, not whether your story feels compelling.
- **Diagnostic of skill = persistence of individual differences**: Real skill shows stable year-to-year rankings; if rankings vary erratically (year-to-year correlation near zero), outcomes are luck.
  - When to use: Judging whether traders, fund managers, or advisers have genuine skill.
  - How: Correlate performance rankings across periods. Near-zero correlation = a game of chance.

## Key Concepts
- **Illusion of skill**: Belief in one's ability to beat the market (or forecast) that is contradicted by both economic theory and one's own record.
- **Confidence by coherence**: Confidence reflects how easily and coherently a story comes to mind, not evidence quality — "poor evidence can make a very good story."
- **WYSIATI**: Only the evidence at hand counts; the mind ignores what it doesn't know.
- **Nonregressive prediction**: Predicting extreme outcomes (failure or stardom) from weak evidence without moderating toward the base rate.
- **Hedgehogs vs. foxes (Tetlock/Berlin)**: Hedgehogs "know one big thing," fit events into a single theory, are confident and resist admitting error; foxes are complex thinkers who recognize luck and multiple forces. Foxes forecast better (but still poorly); hedgehogs get invited on TV.
- **Zero-validity / low-validity environment**: Settings where events are fundamentally unpredictable, so accurate forecasting is impossible.

## Mental Models
- Use "confidence is a feeling, not a judgment" whenever someone (including you) expresses high certainty — take admissions of uncertainty seriously, discount declarations of high confidence.
- Use the persistence-of-differences test to detect skill vs. luck before rewarding performers.
- Use "is their world predictable?" rather than "are they well trained?" to decide whether to trust an expert's forecast.

## Anti-patterns
- **Trusting active stock-picking and pundits**: Individual traders' sold stocks beat their bought stocks by ~3.2 points/year; most active traders and two-thirds of mutual funds underperform; Tetlock's 284 experts beat dart-throwing monkeys at nothing.
- **Letting the professional culture immunize you**: A community of like-minded believers lets any group "maintain an unshakable faith in any proposition, however absurd" and refuse to absorb disconfirming statistics.
- **Extrapolating long-term forecasts from short-term validity**: Short-term trends can be forecast; distant, situation-dependent outcomes cannot.

## Worked Example
Kahneman, invited by a wealth-advisory firm, obtained a spreadsheet of investment outcomes for 25 anonymous advisers over 8 consecutive years — each adviser's yearly score determined his bonus. He computed correlation coefficients between the ranking of advisers in every pair of years (year 1 vs 2, 1 vs 3, … 7 vs 8), yielding 28 correlations. Expecting weak evidence of persistent skill, he instead found the average correlation was .01 — essentially zero. The firm was rewarding luck as if it were skill. Yet when he presented this to executives and advisers, the finding was politely absorbed and swept away: their personal experience of exercising careful judgment was more compelling than an "obscure statistical fact." One executive told him at the airport, "I have done very well for the firm and no one can take that away from me." The illusion of skill was embedded in the culture, not merely an individual quirk.

## Key Takeaways
1. Subjective confidence measures the coherence of your story, not the accuracy of your judgment — treat high confidence as a warning, not a guarantee.
2. The world is often unpredictable; errors of prediction are inevitable, and the line between the predictable near future and the unpredictable distant future matters enormously.
3. Test for skill by persistence: near-zero year-to-year correlation in rankings means outcomes are chance.
4. Cognitive illusions of validity survive intellectual acceptance of disconfirming evidence; knowing the statistic didn't change the army evaluators' feelings or actions.
5. Beware hedgehogs with all-explaining theories; their confidence and media appeal are inversely related to reliability.
6. A test with validity of .20–.30 is still worth using — but expect little more.
