# Chapter 29: The Fourfold Pattern

## Core Idea
People's **decision weights** are not equal to probabilities. Rare outcomes are overweighted (the **possibility effect**) and near-certain outcomes are underweighted relative to true certainty (the **certainty effect**), while intermediate probabilities are compressed. Combining nonlinear decision weights with the gain/loss value function yields **the fourfold pattern**: risk attitude flips across four cells defined by domain (gains vs. losses) and probability (high vs. low). This explains why the same person buys both lotteries and insurance, why plaintiffs settle cheaply while weak-case defendants gamble, and why desperate parties take ruinous risks.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Decision weights (vs. probabilities)**: The psychological weight placed on an outcome; equal to probability only at 0 and 100, but overweighted at the low end and underweighted near certainty.
  - When to use: Predicting choices under risk where probabilities are very small or near-certain.
  - How: Replace each probability with its decision weight (e.g., a 2% chance carries weight ~8.1; a 98% chance carries ~87.1) before evaluating a prospect.
- **The fourfold pattern**: A 2×2 of risk attitudes over {gains, losses} × {high probability, low probability}.
  - When to use: Diagnosing why someone is risk averse or risk seeking; predicting settlement dynamics.
  - How: Locate the choice in one of four cells:
    - High-prob gain → risk averse (accept less to lock in a sure gain).
    - Low-prob gain → risk seeking (buy lottery tickets, chase the jackpot).
    - High-prob loss → risk seeking (gamble to avoid a sure large loss).
    - Low-prob loss → risk averse (buy insurance, pay to eliminate worry).

## Key Concepts
- **Expectation principle**: The rule (von Neumann–Morgenstern) that outcomes should be weighted strictly by probability; normatively required but descriptively false.
- **Possibility effect**: Highly unlikely outcomes are weighted far above their probability (0→5% transforms the situation by creating hope); drives lottery buying and overpayment to eliminate tiny risks.
- **Certainty effect**: Near-sure outcomes are weighted below their probability; a 95%→100% jump has outsized impact, and a 2% chance of not winning cuts a gamble's value by ~13%.
- **Decision weight**: Actual weight on an outcome (a 2% chance is overweighted by a factor of ~4, to 8.1; the 5%–95% range maps to only ~13.2–79.3, about two-thirds of the rational range).
- **Allais's paradox**: A choice pattern (prefer left in problem A, right in problem B) that violates expected utility theory via the certainty effect; the world's top economists committed it.
- **Insensitivity to small probabilities**: Very rare events are either ignored (weight ~0) or, once attended to, sharply overweighted; a 0.001% risk is hard to distinguish from 0.00001%.
- **Certainty premium**: The extra amount people pay to bring a risk all the way to zero rather than merely reducing it (parents paid 3× more to eliminate a poison risk than to cut it two-thirds).

## Mental Models
- Use the possibility effect when a tiny chance drives outsized willingness to pay: a lottery ticket buys the right to dream, so whether the odds are tiny or merely small matters little.
- Use the certainty effect when someone pays to remove the last sliver of risk: structured-settlement firms profit by buying a 95%-likely $1M judgment for $910K — below its $950K expected value — because certainty is worth a premium.
- Use the fourfold pattern to read litigation: a strong-case plaintiff (high-prob gain) is risk averse and settles cheap; a weak-case defendant (high-prob loss) is risk seeking and fights — so the defendant holds the stronger hand.
- Use it to spot desperate gambling in the top-right cell: businesses losing to superior technology, and losing sides in wars, take ruinous risks rather than accept a painful sure loss.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming decision weights equal probabilities**: The expectation principle is "poor psychology"; real weights overweight the rare and underweight the near-certain.
- **Paying certainty premiums on repeated small risks**: For the City of New York facing 200 frivolous suits (each 5% chance of $1M), settling all at $100K costs $20M versus $10M expected from litigating — over-caution on repeated bets is costly.
- **Fighting on to avoid a sure loss**: Top-right-cell risk seeking turns manageable failures into disasters (throwing good money after bad, fighting past certain defeat).
- **Overweighting a small chance of success in frivolous claims**: The plaintiff's boldness and the defendant's insurance-buying risk aversion hand frivolous plaintiffs more than the statistics justify.

## Worked Example
Allais's paradox. Problem A: choose 61% chance to win $520,000 OR 63% chance to win $500,000. Problem B: choose 98% chance to win $520,000 OR 100% chance to win $500,000. Most people pick the higher prize (left) in A but the sure thing (right) in B. Yet problem B's urns are just problem A's urns improved identically — 37 losing marbles replaced by winning ones in each, and the left improvement is worth more per marble ($520K vs. $500K). Preferring left in A but right in B is therefore logically inconsistent. The explanation is the certainty effect: the 2% gap between 98% and 100% (crossing into certainty) feels vastly larger than the same 2% gap between 61% and 63%. When Allais posed this in 1952, the assembled future Nobel laureates committed the violation themselves.

## Key Takeaways
1. Decision weights diverge from probabilities: rare events overweighted (possibility effect), near-certain events underweighted (certainty effect).
2. The fourfold pattern: risk averse for high-prob gains and low-prob losses; risk seeking for low-prob gains and high-prob losses.
3. The same person rationally-seemingly buys both lottery tickets and insurance — both driven by overweighting small probabilities.
4. The top-right cell (high-prob loss) drives desperate, often ruinous, gambling to avoid a sure loss.
5. In litigation, strong-case plaintiffs settle cheap (risk averse) while weak-case defendants gamble (risk seeking); frivolous plaintiffs extract more than the odds warrant.
6. Systematic deviations from expected value are costly over many repeated decisions, for both risk aversion and risk seeking.
