# Chapter 26: Prospect Theory

## Core Idea
Prospect theory replaces Bernoulli's states-of-wealth model with a purely descriptive account in which outcomes are coded as **gains and losses relative to a reference point**. Its **value function** is S-shaped: concave for gains, convex for losses (**diminishing sensitivity**), and steeper for losses than for gains (**loss aversion**). These three features — reference dependence, diminishing sensitivity, and loss aversion — are operating characteristics of System 1. Together they explain risk aversion for gains, risk seeking for losses, and why transparently equivalent problems produce opposite choices.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Prospect theory (value function)**: Value is defined over changes (gains/losses) from a reference point, not over final wealth. The function is S-shaped and asymmetric, with a steeper loss limb.
  - When to use: To predict and explain real human choices under risk, especially where framing, ownership, or reference points matter.
  - How: (1) Locate the reference point; (2) code each outcome as a gain or loss; (3) apply diminishing sensitivity within each domain; (4) weight losses more heavily than gains (~2×).
- **Three cognitive features**: Reference dependence + diminishing sensitivity + loss aversion.
  - When to use: As a checklist to diagnose why a choice violates expected value.
  - How: Ask what the reference point is, whether the change sits on a flat part of the curve, and whether a loss is looming larger than an equal gain.

## Key Concepts
- **Reference point (adaptation level)**: The neutral zero against which outcomes are judged — status quo, expectation, or entitlement. (Three-bowls-of-water demo: the same middle bowl feels hot to one hand, cold to the other.)
- **Value function**: The S-shaped curve mapping gains/losses to psychological value; gains and losses, not wealth states, are the "carriers" of value.
- **Diminishing sensitivity**: The difference between $900 and $1,000 feels smaller than between $100 and $200; the curve flattens away from the reference point in both directions.
- **Loss aversion**: Losses loom larger than equal gains; the value function's slope changes abruptly at the reference point.
- **Loss aversion ratio (~1.5–2.5)**: The smallest gain needed to accept an equal chance of a given loss; typically 1.5–2.5× the loss (often ~2×), rising with stakes and effectively infinite for ruinous risks.
- **Mixed gamble**: A prospect with both an upside and a downside; loss aversion makes people reject many positive-expected-value mixed bets.
- **Rabin's theorem**: A mathematical proof that explaining small-stakes loss aversion via the utility of wealth implies absurd risk aversion on large stakes — "expected utility is an ex-hypothesis."
- **Blind spots**: Prospect theory itself cannot represent disappointment (an unexpected reference point set by high hopes) or regret (dependence on foregone options).

## Mental Models
- Use loss aversion when a positive-EV mixed bet still feels unattractive: rejecting a coin toss that wins $150 or loses $100 is normal because the fear of losing $100 outweighs the hope of $150.
- Use diminishing sensitivity when all options are bad: the pain of losing $900 is more than 90% of the pain of losing $1,000, which drives risk seeking (choosing the gamble over a sure loss).
- Use reference-point relocation to expose framing: the "gift" of $1,000 or $2,000 in Problems 3/4 is absorbed into the reference point and ignored, so the identical final states are chosen differently.
- Use "think like a trader" to dampen loss aversion: instructed to detach emotionally, people become less loss averse and their physiological arousal to losses drops.

## Anti-patterns
- **Evaluating small risks via total wealth**: Extreme aversion to tiny stakes cannot come from wealth utility (Rabin proved it absurd); the reference point and loss aversion are doing the work.
- **Adding realism without new predictions**: Regret/disappointment models are more realistic but made few distinctive predictions, so they lost to the simpler prospect theory — richer assumptions alone don't win.
- **Assuming winning-nothing always has value zero**: When a large prize was 90% likely, failing to win is a crushing loss, not a neutral non-event; the fixed-zero reference is a genuine flaw.

## Worked Example
Problem 3: On top of your wealth you're given $1,000, then choose a 50% chance to win another $1,000 or get $500 for sure. Problem 4: You're given $2,000, then choose a 50% chance to *lose* $1,000 or lose $500 for sure. In final-wealth terms the two problems are identical — both offer a sure +$1,500 versus a gamble between +$1,000 and +$2,000. Yet in Problem 3 most people take the sure thing (risk averse over gains); in Problem 4 most take the gamble (risk seeking over losses). The reason: the reference point moved with the "gift." Being $1,500 richer reads as a gain in Problem 3 and a loss in Problem 4, and the S-shaped, loss-averse value function flips the preferred choice. This is a decisive counterexample to Bernoulli.

## Key Takeaways
1. Gains and losses relative to a reference point — not final wealth — are the carriers of value.
2. The value function is S-shaped (diminishing sensitivity) and steeper for losses (loss aversion).
3. The loss aversion ratio is typically ~1.5–2.5, roughly 2× for many people, and grows with stakes.
4. Loss aversion produces risk aversion in mixed gambles; diminishing sensitivity produces risk seeking when all options are losses — no contradiction.
5. Rabin's theorem shows small-stakes loss aversion cannot be rescued by wealth utility.
6. Prospect theory won on usefulness (reference point + loss aversion yield true new predictions), not on being "true"; it still cannot handle disappointment or regret.
