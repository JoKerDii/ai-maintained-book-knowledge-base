# Chapter 30: Rare Events

## Core Idea
Rare, low-probability events are systematically mishandled: they are either overweighted (given far more influence on decisions than their probability warrants) or entirely neglected. Which way a mind errs depends on whether the event captures focal attention. When a rare outcome is vivid, explicitly described, or emotionally charged, System 1 generates a rich mental representation, and possibility—not probability—drives the response. When the event is never brought to mind or never experienced, it is underweighted or ignored. The mind is simply not built to represent small probabilities accurately.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Overestimation vs. Overweighting**: Two distinct but linked errors. Overestimation = judging a rare event more probable than it is. Overweighting = assigning it a decision weight larger than even its (over)estimated probability. Both are driven by focused attention, confirmation bias, and cognitive ease.
  - When to use: Diagnosing why a rare risk feels bigger than it is—separate the probability judgment from the decision it drives.
  - How: Ask (a) "Am I estimating this too high because a vivid scenario comes easily to mind?" and (b) "Am I acting as if it matters more than even that estimate?"
- **Specify the alternative to 100%**: Because focal events get inflated, force competing possibilities to be equally specific so probabilities sum to 100%.
  - When to use: Scenario planning, forecasting, single-outcome fixation.
  - How: Replace a diffuse "everything else" with explicit named alternatives; check that the set sums to 100%.

## Key Concepts
- **Decision weights**: The psychological impact a probability has on choice, which in prospect theory diverges from the objective probability (e.g., a 10% chance carried a weight of 18.6, a 90% chance only 71.2).
- **Overweighting of rare events**: Giving a minuscule probability an inordinately large influence on feelings and choices (avoiding the bus in Jerusalem).
- **Denominator neglect**: When attention fixes on the numerator (winning marbles, harmed children), the denominator (total pool) is under-processed, so 1-in-10 can be chosen over 8-in-100.
- **Availability cascade**: Self-reinforcing spread of a vivid image through media and conversation that inflates its accessibility and felt probability (how terrorism works).
- **Choice from description vs. choice from experience**: Described prospects ("1% chance") produce overweighting; prospects learned by repeated experience produce underweighting, largely because the rare event is often never sampled.
- **Denominator/frequency format effect**: Risks stated as frequencies ("1 of 1,000") feel larger than the same risk stated as probability ("0.1%") because frequencies evoke an individual image.
- **Global impressions**: When outcomes are learned over time, System 1 pools them into an integrated "personality," and rare events fade unless extreme enough to be recalled separately.

## Mental Models
- Use the "make the alternatives sum to 100%" check when a single scenario dominates your attention—Fox's basketball fans summed to 240% by evaluating each team as focal.
- Use frequency framing when you want a risk to feel real and probability framing when you want it to feel abstract—both describe the same fact.
- Use "did I actually experience or merely hear about this rare event?" to predict whether you'll overweight (described/vivid) or neglect (unsampled) it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating possibility as probability**: Buying lottery tickets or fearing buses because the outcome is imaginable, ignoring that the actual odds are inconsequential.
- **Single-scenario forecasting**: Building probability judgments around one vivid outcome (plan succeeds) while its diffuse alternative (countless failure paths) is under-imagined—fueling the planning fallacy.
- **Being manipulated by format**: Accepting a risk description at face value; advocates and attorneys pick frequency vs. probability framing to steer you.

## Worked Example
Craig Fox asked NBA fans to estimate each of eight playoff teams' probability of winning. Evaluated one at a time, each team is the focal event: memory works in confirmatory mode to construct that team's victory, so each looks plausible. The eight judgments summed to 240%—an impossibility, since they must total 100%. When instead asked whether the winner would come from the Eastern or Western conference (two equally specific alternatives), judgments summed to 100%. Betting revealed overweighting too: the sum of cash equivalents for the eight teams was $287, though the winning payoff was only $160—a guaranteed $127 loss for anyone taking all bets.

## Key Takeaways
1. A rare event is overweighted when it attracts separate attention (explicit description, vivid image, obsessive concern) and neglected when it does not.
2. Overestimation comes from confirmation bias: thinking about an event, you try to make it true in your mind.
3. Specifying only one outcome inflates its probability; force alternatives to be equally concrete and sum to 100%.
4. Frequency formats ("1 of 1,000 disabled") beat probability formats ("0.001%") at raising perceived risk because they conjure an individual—denominator neglect.
5. Emotion and vividness reduce sensitivity to probability: fear of a shock barely tracks its likelihood; the mere possibility triggers the full response.
6. Choices from experience underweight rare events, often because the event was never sampled (no banker had lived a financial crisis in 2007).
