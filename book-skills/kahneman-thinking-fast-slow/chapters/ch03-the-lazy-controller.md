# Chapter 3: The Lazy Controller

## Core Idea
System 2 is a lazy monitor: self-control and cognitive effort draw on one shared, depletable pool of energy, so a busy, tired, or depleted System 2 defers to System 1 — and most people won't spend the few seconds needed to check an appealing but wrong intuitive answer.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Ego depletion (Baumeister)**: All variants of voluntary effort — cognitive, emotional, physical — draw at least partly on a shared pool of mental energy. Exerting self-control on one task leaves you less willing or able to exert it on the next.
  - When to use: To predict lapses in willpower or judgment following prior effortful control.
  - How: After forcing yourself through one demanding task (suppressing emotion, resisting temptation), expect weaker resistance, more impulsive choices, and more intuitive errors on the next.
- **Glucose and mental energy**: Effortful mental work and self-control draw down blood glucose; ingesting glucose can undo ego-depletion effects. Mental energy is "more than a mere metaphor."
- **The lazy System 2 (failure to check)**: A central diagnostic — anyone who gives the intuitive-but-wrong answer endorsed it without checking, though checking would cost only seconds. "People who say 10¢ appear to be ardent followers of the law of least effort."
- **Cognitive Reflection Test (Frederick)**: The bat-and-ball problem plus two others, each inviting a compelling wrong answer; a strong predictor of lazy thinking, impulsivity, and impatience.
- **Intelligence vs. rationality (Stanovich)**: System 2 has two distinguishable "minds" — the *algorithmic* mind (raw brainpower, measured by IQ) and *rationality* (the disposition to check intuitions and resist lazy answers). High intelligence does not make people immune to biases; rationality is separate.

## Key Concepts
- **Flow (Csikszentmihalyi)**: A state of effortless concentration so deep you lose sense of time and self; it separates the two forms of effort by removing the need for self-control, freeing resources for the task.
- **Cognitive busyness / load**: A concurrently loaded System 2 gives System 1 more influence — a load of seven digits makes you likelier to pick the chocolate cake over fruit salad.
- **The engaged mind**: People who resist superficially attractive answers — more alert, more skeptical of their own intuitions, what Stanovich calls more rational.
- **Executive control**: The ability to reallocate attention and override impulses; trainable and correlated with intelligence.
- **Delay of gratification**: Willingness to wait for a larger reward; low CRT scorers strongly prefer smaller-sooner payoffs.

## Mental Models
- Use flow as the target state for hard work: when concentration is intrinsically absorbing, self-control cost drops to zero.
- Think of self-control and hard thinking as withdrawals from one bank account — schedule high-stakes decisions before depletion, not after a long day of meetings.
- Treat an instantly compelling answer to a puzzle as a red flag, not a green light: the more certain and effortless it feels, the more it may be System 1 skipping the check.

## Anti-patterns
- **Endorsing the first plausible answer**: The bat-and-ball trap — over 50% of Harvard/MIT/Princeton students fail; over 80% at less selective schools.
- **Believing an argument because you like its conclusion**: The roses syllogism shows that when a conclusion feels true, people accept even unsound arguments for it — "the conclusion comes first and the arguments follow."
- **Deciding while depleted or hungry**: Parole judges granted ~65% of requests just after a food break, dropping toward ~0% right before the next — falling back on the easy default (denial) when tired and hungry.
- **Failing to retrieve known facts**: Asked about Michigan's murder rate, most don't summon Detroit, and guess lower than a group asked directly about Detroit.

## Worked Example
**The bat-and-ball problem.** "A bat and ball cost $1.10. The bat costs one dollar more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?" The number 10¢ leaps to mind — intuitive, appealing, and wrong. If the ball were 10¢, the bat (a dollar more) would be $1.10, totaling $1.20. The correct answer is 5¢. Frederick used this to measure how closely System 2 monitors System 1: anyone answering 10¢ endorsed an intuition they could have rejected with a few seconds of effort, and also missed the social cue that a questionnaire puzzle with such an "obvious" answer is probably a trap. Even those who get 5¢ report that 10¢ occurred to them first — they simply took the trouble to check.

## Key Takeaways
1. When an answer feels obvious and effortless, deliberately check it — that few-second cost is exactly what lazy System 2 skips.
2. Guard high-stakes judgments against depletion: prior self-control, fatigue, hunger, alcohol, and cognitive load all weaken System 2.
3. Don't trust an argument just because you already believe its conclusion — verify the logic independently.
4. Intelligence ≠ rationality; being smart doesn't protect you from lazy, biased thinking — the disposition to check does.
5. Cultivate flow for demanding work so concentration doesn't compete with self-control.
6. Impulsivity, impatience, and susceptibility to bias cluster together — the CRT predicts them all.
