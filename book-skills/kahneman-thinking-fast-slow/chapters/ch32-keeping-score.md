# Chapter 32: Keeping Score

## Core Idea
Beyond external incentives, people are motivated by emotional bookkeeping: they keep mental score of gains, losses, successes, and failures, and they act to close accounts pleasantly and avoid the pain of regret. This self-administered system of rewards and punishments produces predictable, costly errors—refusing to cut losses, favoring inaction over action to dodge regret, and treating money as non-fungible across mental accounts. Econs care only about future consequences; Humans carry the emotional weight of past decisions and of imagined alternatives.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Mental accounting**: Organizing money and outcomes into separate psychological accounts (per stock, per game, per goal) rather than a single fungible pool.
  - When to use: Diagnosing why economically identical money is treated differently, or why people pay for self-control.
  - How: Ask "What account am I trying to close, and would I decide differently if this were charged to 'general revenue'?"
- **The regret test (default vs. deviation)**: Regret and blame track departures from the default, not commission vs. omission per se.
  - When to use: Anticipating emotional fallout of a choice; guarding against regret-driven conservatism.
  - How: Identify the default option; deviating from it invites more regret if it turns out badly—decide whether that fear is distorting the choice.
- **Regret inoculation**: Be explicit about anticipating regret before deciding, and be either very thorough or completely casual—never "almost right."
  - When to use: Decisions with long-term consequences prone to hindsight.
  - How: Record that you weighed the downside; avoid the "I almost made a better choice" trap that hindsight amplifies.

## Key Concepts
- **Mental accounts**: Separate mental (or physical) ledgers—spending money, earmarked savings, per-hole golf scores—used to keep score and enforce self-control.
- **Disposition effect**: The documented bias to sell winners and hold losers, to close each mental account as a gain rather than a loss.
- **Sunk-cost fallacy**: Investing additional resources in a losing account to justify past commitment, rather than choosing the best future option.
- **Escalation of commitment**: Throwing good money after bad on a failing project—rational for the executive protecting his record, irrational for the firm (an agency problem).
- **Regret**: A counterfactual, self-administered punishment felt most when you can easily imagine having acted otherwise; distinct from blame.
- **Omission/commission and defaults**: Action deviating from a default produces more regret than inaction; George (who switched stocks) feels far more regret than Paul (who stayed put) for the identical outcome.
- **Taboo tradeoff**: Refusal to trade a sacred value (a child's safety, one's health) against money, driven partly by fear of regret—incoherent but powerful.
- **Psychological immune system**: People overestimate future regret because they underrate their own defenses against it.

## Mental Models
- Use "money is money" when a mental account tempts you—would you decide the same if the ticket were free, or the cash charged to general revenue?
- Use "sell the loser, keep the winner" (tax + momentum) when the disposition effect urges the reverse—the answer is not that it makes no difference.
- Use "am I on my deathbed?" / future-only framing when sunk costs pull you to escalate—only future consequences count.
- Use "would I feel regret because I deviated from a default?" to detect regret-driven conservatism masquerading as prudence.

## Anti-patterns
- **Closing accounts to feel good**: Selling winners to score a success and clinging to losers to avoid booking a failure (disposition effect)—costs ~3.4% after-tax annually.
- **Driving into the blizzard**: Persisting with a failing project or job because of prior spending (sunk cost), rather than switching to a better option.
- **Regret-avoidant conformity**: Favoring brand names, conventional treatments, or unchanged portfolios to minimize imaginable regret, at real cost.
- **Taboo tradeoff paralysis**: Refusing any money-for-safety trade "at any price," misallocating a finite safety budget that could reduce greater risks.

## Worked Example
Two sports fans plan to drive 40 miles to a game; a blizzard hits. One paid for his ticket; the other got his free from a friend. Both set up a mental "game" account that closes negative if they skip. But for the buyer, missing the game closes the account out of pocket *and* deprived—doubly negative—so he is more motivated to brave the storm. An Econ would see the ticket cost as sunk and treat both cases identically, asking only "would I drive if I'd gotten the ticket free?" The buyer's extra willingness to risk the drive is the sunk-cost fallacy in miniature—the same error a company makes pouring $60M more into a failing $50M project rather than funding a better new one.

## Key Takeaways
1. We keep emotional score; the ultimate currency of many decisions is regret, pride, and the wish to close accounts favorably.
2. The disposition effect (sell winners, hold losers) sacrifices real wealth to book pleasant account closings—Econs never make it.
3. Sunk costs should be irrelevant, but Humans escalate commitment to failing endeavors—jobs, marriages, projects—to avoid admitting failure.
4. Regret and blame track deviation from the default, not action vs. inaction; anticipated regret pushes choices toward conventional, risk-averse options.
5. Taboo tradeoffs (refusing to price a child's safety) feel moral but are incoherent and can misuse a limited safety budget.
6. Inoculate against regret by being explicit about it beforehand and avoiding half-considered decisions that fuel hindsight.
