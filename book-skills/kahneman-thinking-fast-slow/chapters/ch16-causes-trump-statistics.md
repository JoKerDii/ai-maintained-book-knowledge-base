# Chapter 16: Causes Trump Statistics

## Core Idea
The mind is hungry for causal stories and weak at "mere statistics." When base-rate information can be read as a cause acting on the individual case, people use it; when it is a bald statistical fact about a population, they underweight or ignore it. The same number, reframed from a statistical to a causal base rate, transforms judgment — even though the two are mathematically identical.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two types of base rate (statistical vs. causal)**: Statistical base rates are facts about a population that seem irrelevant to the individual; causal base rates change your view of how the individual case came to be.
  - When to use: When a Bayesian problem gives you a base rate plus specific case evidence, and you must decide how much weight the base rate deserves.
  - How: Ask whether the base rate implies a propensity or situational force acting on the individual. If yes (causal), it will and should be combined with case evidence; if merely descriptive (statistical), guard against neglecting it.
- **Bayesian combination of base rate and evidence**: Combine prior (base rate) with the diagnostic value of case-specific evidence rather than discarding either.
  - When to use: Any diagnosis, prediction, or attribution with a known prevalence and an imperfect signal.
  - How: Start from the base rate as prior; adjust by the reliability of the specific evidence. In the cab problem this yields 41%, not the intuitive 80%.
- **Teach by surprising cases, not statistics**: To change beliefs, present a vivid individual case that forces a causal inference, not a statistical summary.
  - When to use: Training, persuasion, or any attempt to actually shift how someone understands situations.
  - How: Show one or two representative individuals whose surprising behavior must be resolved into a causal story; the generalization follows automatically.

## Key Concepts
- **Bayesian inference**: Rational updating that weighs a prior base rate against imperfectly reliable evidence.
- **Base rate**: The prior probability of an outcome in the population before case-specific evidence.
- **Statistical base rate**: Population fact treated as irrelevant to the individual and typically underweighted.
- **Causal base rate**: Base rate read as a propensity or situational cause, easily merged with case information.
- **Stereotype**: A statement about a group accepted (tentatively) as a fact about every member; in Kahneman's neutral usage, how System 1 represents categories as norms and prototypes.
- **Diffusion of responsibility**: The situational cause in the helping experiment — people fail to help when others could.
- **"Quietly exempt themselves"**: Students accept a surprising statistic yet exclude themselves and acquaintances from its implications.

## Mental Models
- Use the causal test when handing someone a base rate: reframe it as a force acting on the individual if you want it used, but beware — that same framing can inject a false stereotype.
- Use one vivid case, not a table of numbers, when you need to genuinely change how someone reasons about situations.
- Use "deduce the particular from the general is hard; infer the general from the particular is easy" to predict which lessons will stick.
- Use a placebo/control group when interpreting any before-after improvement in an extreme group (bridges to regression, Ch 17).

## Anti-patterns
- **Base-rate neglect**: Going with vivid witness testimony (80%) while ignoring the prevalence of Green cabs (85%).
- **Assuming statistics teach**: Believing a surprising statistical fact will change behavior or beliefs; students recite it yet reason unchanged.
- **Pretending resistance to stereotyping is costless**: Kahneman notes valid stereotypes carry information; neglecting them yields suboptimal judgments, a cost worth paying for a better society but not worth denying.

## Worked Example
The cab problem: 85% of cabs are Green, 15% Blue; a witness who is 80% reliable identifies the culprit as Blue. Most people answer 80%, ignoring the base rate. The correct Bayesian answer is 41% — the base rate (favoring Green) and the witness (favoring Blue) roughly offset, with the base rate slightly more extreme than the witness is reliable. Now change one sentence: the companies are equal in size, but Green cabs cause 85% of accidents. Mathematically identical, but now the base rate reads as a cause — Green drivers are reckless — a stereotype that attaches to the individual driver. People now weight it heavily and land near the Bayesian 41%. Only the causal framing, not the number, changed.

## Key Takeaways
1. Statistical base rates are underweighted; causal base rates are readily used and combined with case evidence.
2. Reframing a base rate as a cause (reckless Green drivers) can move judgment toward the Bayesian answer.
3. System 1 handles causally linked stories well and statistical facts poorly.
4. Surprising individual cases change beliefs; surprising statistics usually do not — people quietly exempt themselves.
5. To teach or persuade, supply a vivid representative case that must be resolved into a causal story.
6. Stereotyping improves accuracy where valid but is rightly constrained by social and legal norms in sensitive contexts.
