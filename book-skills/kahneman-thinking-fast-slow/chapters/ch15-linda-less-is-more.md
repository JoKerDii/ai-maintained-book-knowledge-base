# Chapter 15: Linda: Less Is More

## Core Idea
When you judge probability by how well a case fits a stereotype (representativeness) instead of by the logic of sets, you will rate a detailed, coherent story as *more* probable than the broader event that contains it. This is the conjunction fallacy. Adding plausible detail makes a scenario a better story but a mathematically less likely one. System 2 is too lazy to catch the violation even when all the information sits in front of it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Conjunction rule (subset logic)**: The probability of a conjunction (A and B) can never exceed the probability of either component alone, because the set of "feminist bank tellers" is wholly contained in the set of "bank tellers."
  - When to use: Whenever a forecast or judgment describes a specific, multi-condition scenario and you are tempted to call it "likely."
  - How: Ask whether the detailed event is a subset of a broader one. If it is, its probability must be lower. Think in Venn diagrams; a specified event can only shrink the probability.
- **Frequency (how-many) representation**: Reframe probability questions from percentages to counts of individuals ("How many of the 100 men...?").
  - When to use: When you want to make set-inclusion relations visible and reduce conjunction errors in yourself or others.
  - How: Anchor on a concrete population of N individuals so subsets become spatially obvious. This dropped error rates from 65% to 25% in Kahneman's study.
- **Joint vs. single evaluation**: Compare whether an option is judged alongside alternatives (joint / within-subject) or in isolation (single / between-subject).
  - When to use: When designing decisions, pricing, or presentations, and diagnosing why intuition beats logic.
  - How: Joint evaluation mobilizes System 2 and often restores logic; single evaluation lets System 1's averaging dominate.

## Key Concepts
- **Representativeness**: Judging probability by similarity to a stereotype or prototype rather than by base rates and logic.
- **Conjunction fallacy**: Judging a conjunction (bank teller AND feminist) more probable than one of its constituents (bank teller).
- **Linda problem**: The canonical demonstration; ~85-90% of respondents, including statistically trained ones, violated the conjunction rule.
- **Coherence vs. probability**: The most coherent, plausible story is not the most probable; plausibility and probability are easily confused.
- **Less-is-more**: Removing valued items from a set (broken and intact dishes) can raise its perceived value, because System 1 averages instead of adding.
- **Sum-like variable**: Both economic value and probability are additive; adding a positively valued component can only increase the total, yet intuition treats them as averages.
- **Plausibility trap in forecasting**: Adding detail to a scenario makes it more persuasive but less likely to come true.

## Mental Models
- Use the subset check when a scenario stacks specific conditions: more detail can only lower probability, never raise it.
- Use frequency framing ("how many of 100?") when you need System 2 to see set inclusion that percentages hide.
- Use joint evaluation when you want logic to override a seductive intuition; force an explicit side-by-side comparison.
- Use the coherence-alarm when a forecast feels compelling: ask whether it is merely a good story, not a likely one.

## Anti-patterns
- **Selling probability as plausibility**: Treating a rich, detailed scenario as "highly probable" when it is only a plausible narrative.
- **Padding forecasts with detail**: Adding conditions to make a projection persuasive, which reduces its actual likelihood.
- **Trusting statistical training to protect you**: 85% of Stanford decision-science doctoral students still committed the fallacy; expertise is no guarantee.
- **Bundling a cheap add-on with a premium item**: A low-value freebie can drag down the perceived worth of the whole deal (less-is-more).

## Worked Example
Linda is described as single, outspoken, bright, a philosophy major concerned with discrimination and antinuclear demonstrations. Asked which is more probable — "Linda is a bank teller" or "Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement" — about 85-90% chose the second. But feminist bank tellers are a subset of bank tellers, so the conjunction must be less probable. The feminist detail adds coherence (it fits Linda's profile) while narrowing the set. Kahneman's parallel dinnerware study makes the structure vivid: Set A (40 pieces, some broken) contains all of Set B (24 intact) plus extra intact dishes, so it is objectively worth more. In joint evaluation people paid more for A ($32 vs. $30); in single evaluation they paid more for B ($33 vs. $23), because System 1 averaged in the broken dishes. Same logic, same error.

## Key Takeaways
1. A conjunction can never be more probable than its parts; specificity lowers probability.
2. Representativeness substitutes similarity-to-stereotype for probability, producing the conjunction fallacy.
3. Coherence and plausibility feel like probability but are distinct; the best story is rarely the likeliest event.
4. Adding detail makes scenarios persuasive but less likely — a trap for forecasters and their clients.
5. Frequency framing ("how many of 100") and joint evaluation both mobilize System 2 and cut error rates.
6. Statistical sophistication does not immunize you; System 2 is lazy and endorses plausible answers without checking.
