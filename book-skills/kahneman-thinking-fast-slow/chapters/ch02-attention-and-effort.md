# Chapter 2: Attention and Effort

## Core Idea
System 2's operations cost measurable mental energy — visible in pupil dilation — and because effort is experienced as a cost, the mind obeys a "law of least effort," doing as little deliberate work as it can get away with.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Pupil as index of mental effort**: The pupil dilates in proportion to the momentary demand of a task and contracts the instant the task ends or is abandoned — "a window to the soul" and an "electricity meter" for cognitive energy use.
  - When to use: As a model for thinking about how demand rises and falls second by second during effortful work.
  - How: Effort follows an inverted-V — building with each added load, peaking at the moment of maximum demand, then relaxing as short-term memory unloads.
- **Law of least effort**: If there are several ways to achieve the same goal, people will gravitate to the least demanding course of action. Effort is a cost; skill acquisition trades cost against benefit. "Laziness is built deep into our nature."
  - When to use: To predict that people will offload, simplify, or avoid work whenever possible.
  - How: Divide tasks into easy steps, commit intermediate results to paper or long-term memory, and avoid task-switching under time pressure.
- **Selective allocation of attention**: Unlike a circuit breaker that cuts all power at once, System 2 protects the highest-priority task under overload and gives spare capacity to others second by second.
- **Task sets / executive control**: System 2 can program memory to obey an instruction that overrides habitual responses (e.g. "count every f on this page"). Adopting and terminating task sets is executive control, seated largely in the prefrontal cortex.

## Key Concepts
- **Mental effort**: A physiological cost — dilated pupils, raised heart rate, drawn-down glucose — accompanying demanding cognitive work.
- **Add-1 / Add-3**: Paced digit-transformation tasks that push System 2 to its limit; Add-3 is about the hardest mental task Kahneman ever measured.
- **Working memory load**: Holding several ideas simultaneously, each needing separate action or combination by a rule — the core driver of effort.
- **Task switching**: Moving between task sets is effortful, especially under time pressure.
- **Time pressure**: Forces the pace because material decays in memory; "the most effortful forms of slow thinking are those that require you to think fast."
- **Cognitive pupillometry**: The study of mental effort via pupil measurement.

## Mental Models
- Think of System 2's life as a walk: normally a comfortable stroll (routine chat, ~2–3 digits), occasionally a jog, rarely a frantic sprint (Add-3). Sprints cause temporary blindness to other stimuli.
- Use the "budget of attention" model: you have a limited allocation; try to exceed it and you simply fail. "Pay attention" is literal — the budget can be overspent.
- Think of skill and talent as making tasks cheaper: as you master something, fewer brain regions engage and the pupil dilates less for the same problem.

## Anti-patterns
- **Attempting heavy cognition during demanding physical control**: You cannot compute 17×24 while making a left turn into dense traffic — effortful activities interfere.
- **Overloading working memory instead of externalizing**: Trying to hold long chains in your head rather than committing to paper invites overload and error.
- **Ignoring that busy System 2 misses obvious signals**: At peak effort in Add-1, subjects missed a target letter K their open eyes were pointed straight at.

## Worked Example
**The Add-1 pupil experiment (Kahneman & Beatty).** Subjects rested chin on a stand, stared at a camera, and on each metronome beat repeated four digits with every digit incremented by 1 (5294 → 6305). Pupil size, photographed each second, traced an inverted V: dilation grew with each digit heard, peaked at ~50% area increase (heart rate +7 bpm) as the subject rushed to produce the transformed string, then relaxed as memory unloaded. Add-1 with four digits demanded more than merely holding seven digits for recall. When subjects were asked to also spot a flashed letter K, they detected it near the start and end of the trial but missed it nearly half the time at peak effort — failures following the same inverted-V curve as the pupil. Observers watching the projected pupil from the corridor could tell the instant a subject gave up: "We have a window to your soul."

## Key Takeaways
1. Mental effort is physically real and metered — treat demanding thinking as a scarce, costly resource.
2. Assume people (including yourself) default to the least effortful path; design work to be easy or it won't get done well.
3. Break heavy tasks into small steps and externalize intermediate results to avoid working-memory overload.
4. Avoid task-switching and time pressure when accuracy matters — both multiply effort and error.
5. At peak load you go partially blind; don't expect to catch surprising signals when maxed out.
6. Only System 2 can combine multiple facts by a rule (e.g. resemblance + base rate) — System 1 can't, and few people's System 2 does it either.
