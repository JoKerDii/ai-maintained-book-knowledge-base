# Chapter 11: Anchors

## Core Idea
The anchoring effect occurs when people consider a particular value for an unknown quantity before estimating it — their estimate then stays close to that value, even when the anchor is obviously random or uninformative. It is one of the most reliable and robust findings in experimental psychology. Two distinct mechanisms produce it: a deliberate System 2 adjustment that stops too early, and an automatic System 1 priming effect that makes anchor-compatible evidence easier to retrieve. Anchoring is measurable, large, and exploitable — which makes us more suggestible than we would want to be.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Anchoring effect**: Estimates of an unknown quantity are pulled toward any number considered beforehand, however irrelevant.
  - When to use: Whenever a number is "on the table" before a judgment — prices, forecasts, sentences, damages, contributions.
  - How: Assume any visible number has anchored you; if stakes are high, mobilize System 2 to counter it.
- **Anchoring index**: A measurement of anchoring strength = (difference in mean estimates) / (difference in anchors), as a percentage.
  - When to use: To quantify how much an anchor moved judgments.
  - How: 100% = estimates slavishly track the anchor; 0% = anchor fully ignored. Typical real-world values cluster around 50% (redwoods 55%, wheel of fortune 44%, real-estate agents 41%, students 48%, German judges' dice 50%).
- **Adjust-and-anchor heuristic (System 2 mechanism)**: Start from an anchor, judge it too high/low, and adjust — but stop at the near edge of the region of uncertainty.
  - When to use: When you consciously know an anchor is wrong and know the direction of the true answer.
  - How: Deliberately push farther than feels necessary; adjustment is effortful and fails when System 2 is depleted.
- **Anchoring as priming (System 1 mechanism)**: The anchor acts as a suggestion; System 1 selectively activates memories and evidence compatible with the anchor being true.
  - When to use: To explain anchoring with no felt experience of adjustment (e.g., absurd Gandhi-at-144 anchor).
  - How: Counter by "thinking the opposite" — search memory for arguments and evidence against the anchor.

## Key Concepts
- **Anchor**: The reference number considered before estimating; may be informative or entirely random and still bites.
- **Insufficient adjustment**: Stopping the correction too early, at the boundary of uncertainty; a failure of a weak or lazy System 2.
- **Suggestion**: Being made to see/feel/believe something merely because it was brought to mind — now understood as a priming effect.
- **Associative coherence**: System 1 tries to build a world in which the anchor is the true number, evoking compatible ideas.
- **Random anchors work**: Wheels of fortune, dice, and Social Security digits anchor about as strongly as plausible hints — proof that anchors don't work by being believed informative.

## Mental Models
- Use "assume the number anchored me" when any figure precedes your estimate — the asking price, the plan's projected revenue, the opening offer.
- Use "think the opposite" (Galinsky & Mussweiler) to defend against anchors: focus on the opponent's minimum acceptable offer or their cost of no deal.
- Use "move first" when you hold the informational edge in a single-issue negotiation — the initial anchor has powerful pull.
- Use "refuse the number" when an offer is outrageous: don't counter with an equal extreme; make a scene and refuse to negotiate with that number on the table.

## Anti-patterns
- **Anchoring on plans and best-case scenarios**: Treating a firm's projected revenue or an optimistic plan as the forecast baseline.
- **Trusting your immunity**: Real-estate agents insisted the listing price didn't affect them (index 41%) — professionals deny the effect they cannot see.
- **Capping damages naively**: A $1M cap eliminates larger awards but anchors up many awards that would otherwise be far smaller — helping big offenders more than small ones.
- **Countering an extreme with an extreme**: Creates a gap too wide to bridge.

## Worked Example
The rigged wheel of fortune (Kahneman & Tversky, University of Oregon). The wheel was marked 0–100 but built to stop only at 10 or 65. In front of a small group, an experimenter spun it, and participants wrote down the number shown. They were then asked (a) whether the percentage of African nations in the UN was larger or smaller than that number, and (b) their best guess of that percentage. Those who saw 10 averaged a guess of 25%; those who saw 65 averaged 45%. A spin of a wheel of fortune carries no information about the UN, yet an obviously meaningless number moved estimates by 20 points — the first demonstration of anchoring's sheer absurdity, with an anchoring index of 44%.

## Key Takeaways
1. Any number offered as a possible answer to an estimation problem induces an anchoring effect.
2. Two mechanisms operate: effortful System 2 adjustment (stops short) and automatic System 1 priming (selectively activates compatible evidence).
3. Random and absurd anchors work nearly as strongly as plausible ones — anchors don't need to be believed to bite.
4. Anchoring is measurable via the anchoring index; typical effects run near 50%, in the lab and in the field.
5. Experts (judges, real-estate agents) are as susceptible as novices, and confidently deny the influence.
6. The best defense is to activate System 2 and deliberately think of reasons the anchor is wrong.
