# Chapter 9: Answering an Easier Question

## Core Idea
When faced with a hard question, System 1 quietly swaps in an easier related question, answers that instead, and maps the answer back onto the original — a process called substitution that explains our ready intuitions about matters we barely understand. When emotion is involved, likes and dislikes drive beliefs about facts.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Substitution (Target Question / Heuristic Question)**: Answering an easier heuristic question in place of a difficult target question without noticing the swap.
  - When to use: Whenever a confident intuition arrives for a genuinely hard question (happiness, a candidate's future, appropriate punishment).
  - How: Identify the target question you meant to answer and the heuristic question you actually answered; check whether they match. Answers get fitted back via intensity matching.
- **Heuristic**: A simple procedure that finds adequate but often imperfect answers to hard questions (root shared with "eureka").
  - When to use: Understanding fast, unchosen intuitive judgments.
  - How: Unlike Pólya's deliberate System 2 substitutions ("find an easier problem you can solve"), these heuristics are unchosen consequences of the mental shotgun.
- **Affect Heuristic** (Paul Slovic): Letting likes and dislikes determine beliefs about the world's facts, benefits, and risks.
  - When to use: Any emotionally charged judgment about risk, policy, or people.
  - How: Note that if you like something you'll judge its benefits high and risks low (and vice versa); new information about risk illogically shifts your view of benefits too.

## Key Concepts
- **Target question**: The assessment you actually intend to produce.
- **Heuristic question**: The simpler question you answer instead.
- **Substitution**: Unnoticed replacement of the target question by an easier heuristic question.
- **3-D heuristic**: Substituting perceived three-dimensional size for the requested two-dimensional (on-the-page) size.
- **Mood heuristic for happiness**: Answering "How happy is my life?" with "What is my mood right now?" (or satisfaction in a just-primed domain).
- **Affect heuristic**: Emotional attitude driving beliefs about benefits and risks.
- **Intensity matching (applied)**: Converting a heuristic answer (feeling about dolphins) into the target's units (dollars).
- **System 2 as apologist**: In the realm of attitudes, System 2 endorses and rationalizes System 1's emotions rather than criticizing them.

## Mental Models
- Use "Are we still answering the question we were asked?" as a checkpoint in any hard judgment or meeting.
- Use the target/heuristic pairing to catch substitution: "We meant to ask if she can succeed; we answered whether she interviews well."
- Use the affect-heuristic lens when strong feelings precede your reasoning — your arguments may be conclusions in disguise.
- Use awareness of System 2's apologist role: don't trust that your reasoning examined the emotion; it may just be defending it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Undetected substitution**: Endorsing the easy answer without noticing the target question was hard or different.
- **Mood-driven life judgments**: Rating overall life satisfaction based on a transient mood or a just-mentioned domain (dating, parents, finances).
- **Affect-driven belief**: Deciding a policy's costs are low and benefits high because you already like it.
- **System 2 rubber-stamping**: Letting the lazy monitor follow the path of least effort and approve a heuristic answer unscrutinized.

## Worked Example
The German-students dating survey cleanly demonstrates substitution via the mood heuristic. One group answered, in order: "How happy are you these days?" then "How many dates did you have last month?" The correlation between the two answers was about zero — dating was not what came to mind when assessing overall happiness. A second group saw the same two questions in reverse order: dating first, then happiness. Now the correlation between number of dates and reported happiness was about as high as psychological correlations get. Asking about dating first aroused an emotional reaction — pleasure for those with many dates, loneliness for those with none — and that mood was still active when the general-happiness question arrived. Rather than do the hard work of assessing "happiness these days," students substituted the ready-made answer to "how happy am I with my love life." The same effect appears when questions about parents or finances precede the happiness question. Kahneman notes this is psychologically analogous to the 3-D size illusion: the students weren't confused about the questions' meaning; System 1 simply offered a ready answer to a related question.

## Key Takeaways
1. We are rarely stumped because System 1 substitutes an easier heuristic question for hard target questions.
2. Substitution plus intensity matching lets us produce confident numeric or cross-domain answers to questions we don't fully understand.
3. Unlike Pólya's deliberate problem-substitution, these heuristics are unchosen byproducts of the mental shotgun.
4. Transient mood and just-primed domains dominate global judgments like life happiness (WYSIATI).
5. The affect heuristic makes emotional attitudes drive factual beliefs about risks and benefits, which move together illogically.
6. On attitudes, System 2 acts as an endorser and apologist for System 1's emotions, not a critic — a coherence-seeking System 1 hands solutions to an undemanding System 2.
