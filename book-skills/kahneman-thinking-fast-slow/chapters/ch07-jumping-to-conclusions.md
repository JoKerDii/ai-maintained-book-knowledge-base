# Chapter 7: A Machine for Jumping to Conclusions

## Core Idea
System 1 leaps to a single coherent interpretation, silently resolving ambiguity, suppressing doubt, and defaulting to belief — and because it uses only currently activated information, it treats "what you see is all there is" (WYSIATI), producing confidence that reflects story coherence rather than evidence quality or completeness.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **WYSIATI (What You See Is All There Is)**: System 1 builds the best possible story from activated information and ignores what it doesn't have.
  - When to use: Diagnosing overconfidence, framing effects, and base-rate neglect; auditing decisions based on thin evidence.
  - How: Ask "What critical evidence might be missing?" Recognize that confidence tracks coherence, not amount or quality of data; often knowing less makes a neater story.
- **Positive Test Strategy / Confirmation Bias**: Both System 1 and deliberate System 2 tend to seek confirming rather than disconfirming evidence.
  - When to use: Testing any hypothesis about a person, product, or event.
  - How: Notice that asking "Is Sam friendly?" retrieves different instances than "Is Sam unfriendly?"; deliberately seek refuting evidence instead.
- **Halo Effect**: The tendency to like (or dislike) everything about a person or thing, including unobserved traits, from an initial impression.
  - When to use: Evaluating people/options where first impressions or presentation order may distort weight.
  - How: Recognize first impressions get outsized weight (Alan vs. Ben trait order); counter it.
- **Decorrelate Error**: To reduce aggregate error, keep sources of judgment independent so their errors average out.
  - When to use: Meetings, panels, witness testimony, any multi-source estimate.
  - How: Collect independent judgments before discussion; have committee members write positions first; keep witnesses apart.

## Key Concepts
- **Neglect of ambiguity**: System 1 picks one interpretation of an ambiguous stimulus and never registers the alternatives (the "A 13 C" / bank example).
- **Suppression of doubt**: Holding incompatible interpretations requires effort; doubt is the domain of System 2.
- **Bias to believe** (Gilbert/Spinoza): Understanding a statement begins with provisionally believing it; unbelieving is a separate System 2 act.
- **Confirmation bias**: Preference for evidence compatible with current beliefs.
- **Halo effect**: Emotional coherence that spreads an initial impression across all attributes.
- **Wisdom of crowds** (Surowiecki): Averaged independent judgments are accurate because uncorrelated errors cancel.
- **Overconfidence**: Confidence driven by story coherence even when evidence is scant.
- **Framing effects**: Logically equivalent formulations (90% survival vs. 10% mortality) evoke different responses.
- **Base-rate neglect**: Ignoring statistical base rates in favor of a vivid description (Steve the "librarian").

## Mental Models
- Use "WYSIATI" as a mental flag whenever a conclusion feels solid on little data — ask what's absent.
- Use "decorrelate error": gather independent views before any group discussion.
- Use halo-effect awareness when order or first impressions could be doing the work.
- Use the "bias to believe" model to explain why depleted or distracted people accept almost anything.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confidence from coherence**: Feeling more sure after hearing one side than both — coherence masquerading as evidence.
- **Believing while depleted**: When System 2 is busy, false statements slip through as true (Gilbert's digit-load experiment).
- **Sequential grading / order effects**: Letting the first item set the tone for all that follow.
- **Redundant sources**: Letting witnesses or panelists influence each other, which shrinks effective sample size and preserves shared bias.

## Worked Example
Kahneman describes reforming his own exam grading. Originally he read each student's booklet straight through, scoring all their essays in sequence — and noticed his scores within a booklet were strikingly homogeneous. A strong first essay earned the student the benefit of the doubt on later, vaguer answers: a halo effect in which the first question disproportionately drove the final grade, so a student with one strong and one weak essay got different totals depending on reading order. He switched to grading all students' answers to question one, then all answers to question two, writing scores on the back page to avoid being biased on the next essay. His confidence dropped — he now often found the same student scoring high on one question and low on another, and felt the temptation to "fix" the discrepancy. But the discomfort signaled the new method was better: the earlier consistency had been spurious cognitive ease. This illustrates the general principle "decorrelate error" — the same logic behind averaging independent crowd estimates of pennies in a jar and keeping witnesses separate.

## Key Takeaways
1. System 1 resolves ambiguity to a single interpretation without registering that alternatives existed.
2. Doubt and unbelieving require System 2; a busy or depleted System 2 defaults to belief.
3. The halo effect gives first impressions outsized, often decisive, weight.
4. Confidence reflects the coherence of the constructed story, not the quantity or quality of evidence — knowing less can make a tidier story.
5. Independent, decorrelated judgments beat correlated ones; solicit positions before discussion.
6. WYSIATI is the engine behind overconfidence, framing effects, and base-rate neglect.
