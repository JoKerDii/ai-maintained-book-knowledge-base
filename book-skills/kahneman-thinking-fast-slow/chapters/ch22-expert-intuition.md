# Chapter 22: Expert Intuition: When Can We Trust It?

## Core Idea
Intuition is nothing more than recognition (Herbert Simon): a cue gives the expert access to information stored in memory, and that information is the answer. Genuine expert intuition is real but conditional. Emerging from an adversarial collaboration between Kahneman (skeptic) and Gary Klein (defender of naturalistic expertise), the resolution is that intuition can be trusted only when two conditions hold: the environment is sufficiently regular to be predictable, and the person has had prolonged practice with rapid, clear feedback. Subjective confidence is not a reliable guide to validity — evaluate the intuition by its provenance (environment + learning history), not by how confident it feels.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two conditions for trustworthy intuition**: (1) an environment sufficiently regular to be predictable; (2) an opportunity to learn those regularities through prolonged practice.
  - When to use: Deciding whether to trust any expert's (or your own) intuitive judgment.
  - How: Ask — is this domain statistically regular? Did the expert get enough practice with quick, clear feedback? If either fails, distrust the intuition regardless of confidence.
- **Recognition-primed decision (RPD) model (Klein)**: Experts generate a single plausible option from a repertoire of patterns (System 1), then mentally simulate it to check if it works (System 2); if flawed, modify or move to the next.
  - When to use: Understanding how real experts (firefighters, chess masters, nurses) actually decide under time pressure.
  - How: Recognize that expertise generates good first options, not exhaustive comparison of alternatives.
- **Evaluate provenance, not the piece**: Like judging art authenticity, assess the source (regularity + learning) rather than the surface plausibility of the intuition.

## Key Concepts
- **Intuition as recognition (Simon)**: "The situation has provided a cue; this cue has given the expert access to information stored in memory, and the information provides the answer."
- **Regular (high-validity) environment**: Statistically orderly settings (chess, bridge, poker, firefighting, anesthesiology) where valid cues exist to be learned.
- **Zero-validity environment**: Fundamentally unpredictable settings (long-term political forecasting, stock picking) where intuitive hits are luck or lies.
- **Wicked environment (Hogarth)**: A setting that teaches the wrong lessons because feedback is misleading (the typhoid physician who "diagnosed" by palpating tongues without washing hands).
- **10,000 hours / miniskills**: Expertise is a large collection of miniskills acquired slowly; chess mastery needs ~10,000 hours of dedicated practice.
- **Feedback quality and speed**: Skill develops only where outcomes are quick and unambiguous (braking a car, anesthesiology) — not where feedback is delayed or absent (harbor pilots, radiologists, therapists' long-term prognoses).
- **Confidence is a feeling, not a diagnostic**: Substitution can produce high confidence on the wrong question.

## Mental Models
- Use "regular environment + adequate practice?" to gate trust in any intuition — set confidence aside.
- Use the feedback test when judging a professional's specific skill: was feedback quick and clear enough to learn from? (Anesthesiologist's "something is wrong" > radiologist's unverified read.)
- Use "expertise is a collection of miniskills" — an expert may be masterful at one task in her domain and a novice at another (therapist: short-term rapport yes, long-term prognosis no).

## Anti-patterns
- **Trusting confidence as evidence of accuracy**: Do not trust anyone — including yourself — to tell you how much to trust their judgment.
- **Believing you can succeed at an impossible forecasting task**: Blameless to fail in an unpredictable world; culpable to believe you can succeed. Intuitive "hits" without valid cues are luck or self-delusion.
- **Extending validity beyond its domain**: A therapist's skill at anticipating the next sentence doesn't license predicting next year's outcome; a Middle East expert knows much but not the future.

## Worked Example
Klein's fireground commanders: he initially hypothesized commanders weighed two options; instead they usually generated only a single option. Drawing on a repertoire of patterns built over a decade of real and virtual experience, a commander identifies one plausible action, mentally simulates it to see if it works in the situation, implements it if sound, modifies it if flawed, or moves to the next option — the RPD model. This works because firefighting is a regular environment with rich, fast feedback, so System 1 has learned highly valid cues even when System 2 can't name them. Contrast the "sixth sense" firefighter who flees just before a house collapses: not magic, but recognition of cues honed by practice. Now contrast Meehl's clinicians and long-term political forecasters, who operate in low- or zero-validity environments — there the same confident intuition is illusory, because the regularities needed to support it don't exist.

## Key Takeaways
1. Intuition is recognition — the everyday memory operation, not magic.
2. Trust intuition only when the environment is regular AND the person had prolonged practice with quick, clear feedback; both conditions must hold.
3. Subjective confidence is not a diagnostic of accuracy; judge intuition by provenance, not by how compelling it feels.
4. Expertise is a collection of miniskills; the same professional can be expert at one task and a novice at another, and usually can't see the boundary.
5. Beware wicked environments that teach the wrong lessons through misleading feedback.
6. In the absence of stable regularities, intuitive "hits" are luck or lies — it is fair to blame professionals for believing they can succeed at impossible tasks.
