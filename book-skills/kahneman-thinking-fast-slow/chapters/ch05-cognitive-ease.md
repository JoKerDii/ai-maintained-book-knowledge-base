# Chapter 5: Cognitive Ease

## Core Idea
System 1 continuously registers whether mental processing is easy ("cognitive ease") or effortful ("cognitive strain"), and this single signal — fed by font clarity, repetition, priming, and mood — silently biases what we believe, like, and trust. Ease breeds trust, familiarity, and superficial thinking; strain mobilizes the vigilant, analytic System 2.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Cognitive Ease vs. Cognitive Strain**: A single "dial" System 1 maintains, ranging from Easy to Strained, connected to a network of interchangeable inputs and outputs.
  - When to use: Whenever you notice a judgment feels effortless and obvious, or conversely feels like hard work — pause to ask why.
  - How: Recognize causes of ease (clear font, prior exposure, priming, good mood) produce good mood, belief, trust, and superficial thinking; causes of strain (bad font, novelty, effort) produce vigilance, suspicion, and fewer errors but less creativity.
- **How to Write a Persuasive Message**: A checklist for enlisting cognitive ease to make a (true) message believable.
  - When to use: Composing anything you want an audience to accept.
  - How: Maximize legibility (high-contrast, quality paper, bright blue/red); avoid needlessly complex vocabulary; make it memorable (rhyme/verse); cite sources with easy-to-pronounce names; repeat key phrases.
- **Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT)** (Shane Frederick): Three problems (bat-and-ball, widgets, lily pads) engineered so the first intuitive answer is wrong.
  - When to use: Testing susceptibility to accepting System 1's first answer without checking.
  - How: Score whether the person overrides the tempting intuitive answer. Inducing strain (barely legible font) improved performance — errors dropped from 90% to 35%.

## Key Concepts
- **Cognitive ease**: The pleasant sense that mental processing is running smoothly, signaling safety and no need for effort.
- **Cognitive strain**: The felt effort of engaged, analytic processing, which mobilizes System 2.
- **Illusion of familiarity**: A previously seen item is perceived more fluently, and that ease is misread as a memory of "pastness."
- **Illusion of truth**: Statements that feel easy (familiar, legible, repeated) are judged more likely to be true.
- **Mere exposure effect** (Robert Zajonc): Repeated exposure to an arbitrary stimulus produces mild liking for it — even without conscious awareness of the exposure.
- **Priming**: Prior unconscious exposure that increases processing fluency for a later stimulus.
- **Mood–intuition link**: Good mood enhances intuition and creativity but reduces vigilance and increases logical errors.
- **Remote Association Test (RAT)** (Mednick): A creativity test asking for a word linking three cue words; coherence is felt as ease before the answer is known.

## Mental Models
- Use the "cognitive ease" lens when a claim feels obviously true — the ease may come from the font or repetition, not the facts.
- Use deliberate cognitive strain (slow down, question the source) when stakes are high and the answer came too easily.
- Use the mere-exposure insight when you find yourself liking something only because it is familiar.
- Use mood-awareness before creative or high-stakes judgments: a good mood weakens System 2's guard.

## Anti-patterns
- **Mistaking familiarity for truth**: Frequent repetition makes falsehoods feel true; marketers and authoritarians exploit this.
- **Erudite vernacular**: Using pretentious complex language to seem smart actually reads as less intelligent and less credible (Oppenheimer).
- **Trusting fluent sources over accurate ones**: Weighting an easy-to-pronounce name (Artan) over a hard one (Taahhut) regardless of content.
- **Letting good mood run unchecked**: Being intuitive and gullible when analytic care is needed.

## Worked Example
Larry Jacoby's "Becoming Famous Overnight" demonstrates the illusion of familiarity. Participants first read a list of made-up, non-famous names (e.g., David Stenbill). Days later they saw a longer list mixing real minor celebrities with the previously-seen fake names and were asked to check the celebrities. They frequently marked David Stenbill as famous — not because they recalled the earlier list, but because the name was processed with greater cognitive ease (seen more clearly, read faster). That ease was misinterpreted as the "pastness" of having encountered a celebrity. Kahneman links this to the truth illusion: repeatedly exposing people to a phrase like "the body temperature of a chicken" made them more likely to accept an arbitrary completion ("...is 144°") as true, because familiarity of one fragment lent the whole statement a feeling of truth.

## Key Takeaways
1. A single cognitive-ease signal, driven by many interchangeable causes, secretly shapes belief, liking, and trust — and you cannot trace it to its source.
2. Familiarity is not distinguishable from truth; repetition manufactures both liking and belief.
3. Legibility, rhyme, simple words, and pronounceable names make messages more persuasive independent of their content.
4. Cognitive strain mobilizes System 2 and reduces errors — sometimes a "bad font" makes you think better.
5. The mere exposure effect works even without conscious awareness and has deep evolutionary roots: repeated-and-safe becomes liked.
6. Good mood clusters with intuition, creativity, and gullibility; bad mood clusters with vigilance, suspicion, and accuracy.
