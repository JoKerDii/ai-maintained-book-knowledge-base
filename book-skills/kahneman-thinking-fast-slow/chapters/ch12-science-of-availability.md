# Chapter 12: The Science of Availability

## Core Idea
The availability heuristic judges the frequency or size of a category by the ease with which instances come to mind. This substitutes an easier question (how fluently do examples arrive?) for a harder one (how frequent is it, really?), so any factor that makes instances easy to recall — salience, drama, personal experience, vividness — biases the estimate. A refinement: judgment often follows the *fluency* of retrieval (how easy it felt) rather than the *number* retrieved, and the effect can even reverse when System 2 is engaged or when the difficulty of retrieval is given an alternative explanation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Availability heuristic**: Judging frequency by "the ease with which instances come to mind."
  - When to use: Whenever you estimate how common, likely, or large something is.
  - How: Notice that no instances need actually be retrieved (you sense "more words from this letter set" instantly). Because it substitutes ease for frequency, it produces systematic error.
- **List-the-biases procedure**: Enumerate factors other than frequency that make instances easy to recall; each is a source of bias.
  - When to use: To audit a frequency impression for distortion.
  - How: Ask "Is this belief driven by a few recent, salient, or personal instances rather than by the base rate?" (e.g., teenage theft, skipping a flu shot).
- **Fluency vs. content (Schwarz's refinement)**: Listing instances raises a trait judgment via two competing routes — the number retrieved and the ease of retrieval — and ease usually wins.
  - When to use: When self-assessing traits or evaluating anything after generating examples.
  - How: Beware that being asked for *more* examples can *lower* your rating, because struggling for the last few feels like disconfirming evidence ("unexplained unavailability").

## Key Concepts
- **Availability bias**: Systematic error from letting recall-ease stand in for frequency (media-salient events over-weighted).
- **Substitution**: Answering an easier question in place of the intended harder one.
- **Fluency**: The subjective ease of retrieval; the actual driver of many availability judgments.
- **"Unexplained unavailability" heuristic**: The real inference — surprising difficulty in recalling instances signals the trait is weak; when the surprise is explained away, the effect vanishes.
- **More-than-100% credit**: Spouses' self-estimated contributions to shared tasks sum above 100% because each recalls their own efforts more readily.
- **Going with the flow**: Conditions (dual task, good mood, non-depressed, novice-not-expert, faith in intuition, feeling powerful) under which people rely on ease rather than content.

## Mental Models
- Use "audit the ease" when a risk feels vivid: ask whether recent salient instances, not real frequency, drive the impression.
- Use "the 100%-credit rule" in teams and marriages: expect everyone to feel they did more than their share, because own-contributions are more available.
- Use "more examples can undercut" when someone piles on arguments or advantages — struggling to reach a set number can lower, not raise, conviction.
- Use "engage System 2" when the judgment matters personally: focus on the content retrieved, not on how easily it came.

## Anti-patterns
- **Over-weighting salient events**: Exaggerating Hollywood divorces and political sex scandals because they attract attention.
- **Letting a dramatic event reset your risk feeling**: A burning car or a covered plane crash temporarily makes the world feel more dangerous.
- **Confusing struggle with truth**: Concluding you are unassertive because twelve examples were hard to produce, when six felt easy.

## Worked Example
Schwarz and colleagues' assertiveness study. Participants first listed instances of their own assertive behavior, then rated how assertive they were. One group listed six instances (easy); another listed twelve (a number most find hard). If number retrieved drove judgment, the twelve-group — having produced *more* evidence of assertiveness — should rate themselves higher. Instead they rated themselves *less* assertive, because the last few instances came only with difficulty, and low fluency was read as evidence. Symmetrically, people asked for twelve instances of *non*-assertive behavior concluded they were quite assertive. A follow-up isolated fluency directly: participants held a facial expression while recalling six instances — "frowners" (furrowed brow, inducing cognitive strain) found retrieval harder and rated themselves less assertive than "smilers." And when the difficulty was attributed to a spurious cause (background music said to hinder recall), the effect disappeared — confirming it is *unexpected* low fluency, a System 1 surprise, that drives the judgment.

## Key Takeaways
1. Availability substitutes ease-of-retrieval for actual frequency, so salient, dramatic, vivid, and personal instances inflate frequency estimates.
2. You often need retrieve zero instances — the *sense* of ease is enough.
3. Fluency usually trumps the number retrieved; asking for more examples can lower a judgment.
4. The mechanism is really "unexplained unavailability": a System 1 surprise at difficulty, which dissolves when the difficulty is given another explanation.
5. Engaged, personally-involved, vigilant thinkers go by content; those "going with the flow" go by ease — and are more biased.
6. Availability biases are resistible but only with effortful, tiresome self-monitoring — credit-allocation debiasing (the 100% rule) is a rare easy win.
