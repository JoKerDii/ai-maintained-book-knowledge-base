# Chapter 14: Tom W's Specialty

## Core Idea
When judging the probability that an individual belongs to a category, people substitute a judgment of representativeness — how similar the person is to the stereotype of that category — for the harder question of actual probability. This causes them to neglect two things that logic demands: base rates (how common the category is) and the quality of the evidence (how trustworthy the description is). The disciplined alternative is Bayesian reasoning: anchor on a plausible base rate, then adjust by the diagnosticity of the evidence — moving less when the evidence is weak.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Representativeness heuristic**: Judging probability by similarity to a stereotype rather than by statistical likelihood.
  - When to use: To spot when a "probability" judgment is really a similarity judgment in disguise.
  - How: Notice cues that trigger a stereotype (nerdy puns, tidy systems, tattoos, a "winner's" look) and ask whether you're rating fit rather than likelihood.
- **Base-rate reasoning**: Use the proportion of the category in the population as the starting probability, like drawing a marble of known color-proportions from an urn.
  - When to use: Always as the prior; especially when case evidence is weak or untrustworthy.
  - How: Start from base rates when no specific info exists; keep judgments close to base rates when the specific info is doubtful.
- **Bayesian discipline (two keys)**: Combine prior beliefs (base rates) with the diagnosticity of the evidence.
  - When to use: Any probability judgment about an individual case with both a prior and some evidence.
  - How: (1) Anchor your judgment of the outcome's probability on a plausible base rate; (2) question the diagnosticity of your evidence — intuitive diagnosticity is usually exaggerated.

## Key Concepts
- **Base rate**: The proportion of a category within the relevant population; the correct prior probability absent other information.
- **Representativeness**: Similarity of an individual to the stereotype of a group — unaffected by group size or by whether the group even exists.
- **Substitution**: Answering the easy similarity question in place of the hard probability question ("mental shotgun").
- **Diagnosticity of evidence**: The degree to which evidence favors one hypothesis over its alternatives; the factor by which a base rate should be updated.
- **Insensitivity to evidence quality (WYSIATI)**: Worthless information should be treated like no information, but System 1 processes available information as if true unless it is immediately rejected.
- **Anti-base-rate character**: A description (like Tom W) deliberately built to fit small specialties and poorly fit the largest ones — pitting representativeness against base rates.
- **Ignorance vs. laziness**: Two reasons System 2 fails to correct base-rate neglect — believing base rates irrelevant, or simply not investing effort.

## Mental Models
- Use "which is more populous?" (base-rate check) when betting on an individual's category: many more subway riders lack a college degree than hold a PhD, so bet accordingly even against a "PhD-looking" reader of the Times.
- Use "similarity ≠ probability" when a case seems to fit a rare type: a "shy poetry lover" is more likely a business student than a Chinese-literature student, because the business pool is vastly larger.
- Use "stay near the base rate" when evidence is weak or of uncertain validity — discipline your estimate toward the prior.
- Use "think like a statistician, not a clinician" (Schwarz) to prompt base-rate use; frowning/vigilance (System 2) also restores some base-rate sensitivity.

## Anti-patterns
- **Predicting rare (low-base-rate) events from weak evidence**: The classic sin — betting on the unlikely because it's representative.
- **Treating untrustworthy evidence as informative**: Ranking Tom W into computer science despite being told the personality sketch is of "uncertain validity."
- **Going by representativeness in hiring/investing**: A tidy lawn, competent receptionist, or can't-fail startup pitch don't override the industry base rate of success.
- **Judging by surface cues**: Assessing leadership by chin shape, or an academic's prospects by tattoos.

## Worked Example
The Tom W experiment (Kahneman & Tversky). Subjects first ranked nine graduate fields by base rate (enrollment size) — easy, and correctly dominated by large fields like humanities/education. Then they read a personality sketch describing Tom W as intelligent but uncreative, orderly, fond of "neat and tidy systems," writing dull prose with "corny puns," with little feel for other people — explicitly noted to be based on "tests of uncertain validity." Ranked by *similarity* to field stereotypes, Tom W fit computer science and engineering best, humanities and social work worst. The critical third task asked graduate students in psychology — who knew the base rates and knew the description was untrustworthy — to rank fields by *probability*. Their probability rankings were essentially identical to the similarity rankings: substitution was perfect. Even Robyn Dawes, a sophisticated statistician and skeptic, tentatively guessed "computer scientist?" before catching himself at the words "base rate." The correct answer: stay close to the base rates, only slightly nudging the rare fields up and the populous fields down, because the little evidence available is not trustworthy. Bayes's rule makes this precise — a 3% base rate updated by evidence 4x more likely for computer scientists yields only 11%, not a top ranking.

## Key Takeaways
1. A question about probability triggers a "mental shotgun" that answers the easier question of representativeness (similarity to a stereotype).
2. Representativeness ignores base rates and evidence quality; similarity is legitimately blind to both, but probability is not.
3. Base rates matter even in the presence of case-specific evidence — this is often not intuitively obvious.
4. Intuitive impressions of evidence diagnosticity are usually exaggerated (WYSIATI + associative coherence make us believe our own stories).
5. Base-rate neglect is often laziness, not ignorance — engaging System 2 (statistician mindset, frowning/vigilance) restores some sensitivity.
6. Discipline in two steps: anchor on a plausible base rate, then question the diagnosticity of your evidence — the weaker the evidence, the closer to the base rate you stay.
