# Chapter 6: Norms, Surprises, and Causes

## Core Idea
System 1 maintains a running model of what is "normal" in your world, built from associations, and it uses that model both to detect surprises with astonishing speed and to impose causal and intentional stories on events — even when only statistical reasoning is warranted.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Norm Theory** (Kahneman & Dale Miller): Events are judged normal or abnormal not only by advance expectation but by how readily they recruit similar past episodes from memory.
  - When to use: Explaining why a surprising event stops feeling surprising after one recurrence, or why anomalies pop out instantly.
  - How: An abnormal event that retrieves an earlier matching event gets reinterpreted "in conjunction with it," so both fit a pattern and feel normal.
- **Two Varieties of Surprise**: Active (consciously awaited events) vs. passive (events not awaited but unsurprising when they occur).
  - When to use: Distinguishing what you predicted from what merely fits your model of normality.
  - How: Active expectations, when violated, surprise strongly; passive expectations cover the vast range of things that are "normal but not probable enough to await."
- **Two Modes of Causal Perception** (Michotte; Heider & Simmel): Physical causality (one object "launches" another) and intentional causality (agents with goals) are perceived directly, not inferred.
  - When to use: Recognizing when a causal or intentional story is a perception, not a reasoned conclusion.
  - How: Note that both are automatic products of System 1, present from infancy, and irresistible.

## Key Concepts
- **Assessing normality**: System 1's core job of representing what is typical, which frames interpretation of the present and expectations of the future.
- **Norms of categories**: Stored typical values and ranges (size of a mouse vs. elephant) that make communication and anomaly detection possible.
- **Passive expectation**: An event you don't await but accept as normal when it happens.
- **Associative coherence**: The tendency for juxtaposed ideas to jointly evoke a fitting interpretation (lost wallet + New York + crowds → pickpocket).
- **Moses illusion**: Failing to notice an error ("How many animals did Moses take into the ark?") because the wrong element fits the activated context.
- **Illusion of causality** (Michotte): Seeing one moving shape "launch" another though no physical contact exists.
- **Intentional causality** (Heider & Simmel): Perceiving moving triangles and a circle as bullies, victims, and allies with feelings.
- **Statistical vs. causal thinking**: Statistics derives conclusions about individuals from ensembles; System 1 cannot do this and defaults to causal stories.

## Mental Models
- Use norm theory to explain diminishing surprise: one recurrence rewrites what counts as normal.
- Use the "two causalities" model when you catch yourself attributing intention to random or statistical events.
- Use anomaly-detection awareness: your instant sense that something is "off" reflects vast world knowledge applied automatically.
- Use the caution "this needs statistics, not a story" when a vivid causal narrative forms around an individual case.

## Anti-patterns
- **Inventing causes for randomness**: Demanding a causal story for chance events (the two contradictory Hussein-capture headlines that "explain" opposite market moves).
- **Applying causal thinking to statistical problems**: The book's recurring warning; System 1 substitutes a causal story where base rates are required.
- **Over-attributing intention**: Assuming sabotage or agency where luck or coincidence suffices.

## Worked Example
Kahneman recounts meeting his acquaintance Jon, a psychologist, at a 40-room island resort on the Great Barrier Reef — a striking coincidence they remarked on. Two weeks later, at a London theater, a latecomer sat next to Kahneman; at intermission it was Jon again. He and his wife noticed two things simultaneously: the second meeting was objectively far more improbable than the first, yet they were distinctly less surprised. The first encounter had rewritten their mental model — Jon became "the psychologist who shows up when we travel abroad." By norm theory, the second event recruited the first from memory and the two fit a pattern, so meeting Jon felt more normal than meeting any of their hundreds of other acquaintances would have. A parallel roadside example (a burning car seen two Sundays in a row) shows passive expectation converting into an active one when circumstances repeat.

## Key Takeaways
1. System 1's central function is maintaining a model of normality that governs interpretation and expectation.
2. Surprise is the most sensitive index of how we understand the world; a single recurrence can normalize the improbable.
3. Anomalies are detected extraordinarily fast (within ~0.2 seconds) using vast implicit world knowledge.
4. Norms carry both typical values and ranges, which underwrites shared language and instant anomaly detection.
5. We perceive both physical and intentional causality directly and from infancy — these are System 1 outputs, not reasoned inferences.
6. The pull toward causal stories leads us to misapply causal thinking where statistical reasoning is required.
