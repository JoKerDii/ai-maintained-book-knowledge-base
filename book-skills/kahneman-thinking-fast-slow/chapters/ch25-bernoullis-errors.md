# Chapter 25: Bernoulli's Errors

## Core Idea
Standard economics assumes an agent who is "rational, selfish, and whose tastes do not change" (Econs), while real people (Humans) have a System 1, are bounded by WYSIATI, and are neither fully rational nor consistent. The dominant framework for risky choice, **expected utility theory**, treats the utility of an outcome as a function of the resulting *state of wealth*. This is **Bernoulli's error**: it omits the **reference point**. Happiness and choice depend on *changes* relative to where you started, not on final wealth alone. The same final wealth can leave one person elated and another despondent.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Expected utility theory**: Value a gamble as the average of the *utilities* of outcomes, each weighted by its probability (not the average of dollar values). Diminishing marginal utility of wealth explains ordinary risk aversion.
  - When to use: As a normative logic of consistent choice; a good approximation in many economic contexts.
  - How: Assign a utility to each possible state of wealth; pick the option with the highest probability-weighted utility.
- **Reference-dependence (the correction)**: Evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point (usually the status quo or recent wealth), not as absolute states.
  - When to use: Whenever the same objective outcome is experienced differently depending on the person's history or expectations.
  - How: First identify each decider's reference point, then code outcomes as gains or losses from that point.

## Key Concepts
- **Econs vs. Humans**: Idealized rational, selfish, stable-taste agents versus real people governed partly by System 1.
- **Expected value**: Probability-weighted average of dollar outcomes; pre-Bernoulli standard, which people do not actually follow.
- **Diminishing marginal utility of wealth**: Each added dollar adds less subjective value; utility is roughly logarithmic in wealth.
- **Risk aversion**: Preferring a sure thing to a gamble of equal (or slightly higher) expected value; a decider will even pay a premium to avoid uncertainty.
- **Reference point**: The prior state (e.g., yesterday's wealth) against which current outcomes are judged; the "missing moving part" in Bernoulli's model.
- **Psychophysics**: Fechner's study of how subjective experience relates to physical magnitude; sensation is reference-dependent (a sound is loud or faint relative to what preceded it).
- **Theory-induced blindness**: Once you adopt a theory as a thinking tool, its flaws become nearly invisible; disconfirming cases get the benefit of the doubt.

## Mental Models
- Use reference-point thinking when two people face identical final states: Jack (up from 1M) and Jill (down from 9M) both hold 5M, yet Jack is elated and Jill despondent — utility tracks the change, not the level.
- Use gain/loss framing when predicting risk attitude: Anthony (framing a move to 2M as doubling from 1M) is risk averse; Betty (framing the same 2M as halving from 4M) is risk seeking because all her options feel like losses.
- Use "disbelieving is hard work" when auditing an accepted model: System 2 tires easily, so obvious counterexamples get waved away rather than pursued.

## Anti-patterns
- **Modeling utility on final wealth alone**: Ignores history and reference points, so it cannot explain why identical wealth produces opposite emotions or why Betty gambles.
- **Giving a revered theory the benefit of the doubt**: Assuming a "perfectly good explanation you're missing" lets flawed models survive for centuries (Bernoulli's stood ~250 years).
- **Assuming stable tastes**: People often don't know what they'll want tomorrow, so predictions built on fixed preferences fail.

## Worked Example
Anthony has 1 million; Betty has 4 million. Both are offered the same choice: a sure 2 million, or a 50/50 gamble to end with 1 million or 4 million. In wealth terms the options are identical for both (sure 2M vs. an expected 2.5M gamble), so expected utility theory predicts identical choices. But their reference points differ. Anthony frames the sure thing as *doubling* his wealth — very attractive — so he takes the sure 2M (risk averse). Betty frames the same sure 2M as *losing half* her wealth — awful — and the gamble as a chance to lose nothing versus losing three-quarters, so she gambles (risk seeking). Same final states, opposite choices. Bernoulli's model can explain Anthony but not Betty, because it has no reference point.

## Key Takeaways
1. Choice and happiness are driven by changes relative to a reference point, not by absolute states of wealth.
2. Expected utility theory's diminishing marginal utility explains everyday risk aversion but is silent on losses.
3. The same final outcome is a gain for one person and a loss for another; you must know each decider's reference point.
4. People facing only bad options (Betty, entrepreneurs, cornered generals) often become risk seeking.
5. A theory's deepest errors hide in what it tacitly ignores, not in what it asserts.
6. Theory-induced blindness — plus a tired System 2 — lets obviously flawed models persist for generations.
