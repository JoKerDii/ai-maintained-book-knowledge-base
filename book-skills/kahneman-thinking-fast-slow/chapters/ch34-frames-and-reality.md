# Chapter 34: Frames and Reality

## Core Idea
Logically equivalent descriptions of the same reality routinely evoke different preferences, because meaning for Humans is not the truth-condition of a statement but what happens in the associative machinery when we understand it. "Italy won" and "France lost" designate the same fact yet feel different. System 1 responds to the emotional coloring of words (KEEP vs. LOSE, survival vs. mortality, cash discount vs. credit surcharge), and System 2 is too lazy to reframe. Preferences are therefore frame-bound, not reality-bound—and often there is no underlying "true" preference the frame distorts; the preference exists only relative to the frame. Some frames are nonetheless objectively better, and choosing the frame is a real and consequential decision.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Framing effects**: Unjustified influences of formulation on beliefs and preferences.
  - When to use: Diagnosing why the same options draw different choices; auditing your own or others' presentations.
  - How: Restate the problem in a logically equivalent alternative frame; if your preference moves, it was frame-bound.
- **Reframing to reality**: Actively translate emotionally loaded frames into neutral, equivalent ones before deciding.
  - When to use: High-stakes decisions passively accepted as presented (medical, financial, policy).
  - How: Convert LOSE amounts to KEEP equivalents, mortality to survival, sunk losses to "am I wealthier or poorer now?"
- **Choosing the better frame**: Where frames differ in quality, adopt the one yielding more rational decisions.
  - When to use: Policy and personal decisions with a demonstrably superior representation.
  - How: Prefer broader/inclusive accounts (cash lost, not tickets lost), gallons-per-mile over mpg, and defaults aligned with the desired outcome.

## Key Concepts
- **Framing effects**: Formulation alters preference though the objective outcome is unchanged.
- **Reality-bound vs. frame-bound**: Econs respond to states of the world; Humans respond to descriptions of them.
- **Emotional framing**: Word choice (costs vs. losses, keep vs. lose) triggers approach/avoid tendencies via the amygdala.
- **Asian disease problem**: Identical outcomes framed as "lives saved" evoke risk aversion; framed as "deaths" evoke risk seeking—preferences reverse.
- **Costs are not losses**: A bad outcome is more acceptable framed as a cost (lottery ticket that didn't win) than as a loss; hence "cash discount," not "credit surcharge."
- **Mental accounting and sunk cost (in framing)**: Lost tickets get posted to the show's account (feels doubled); lost cash to general revenue (ignored)—the latter is the better frame.
- **The MPG illusion**: mpg is a misleading frame; gallons-per-mile reveals that 12→14 mpg saves more fuel than 30→40 mpg.
- **Defaults / opt-in vs. opt-out**: The default is the best predictor of organ-donation rates (near 100% opt-out vs. ~4–12% opt-in); this effect is driven by System 2's laziness, not System 1 emotion.
- **Rationality index**: A ranking of individuals by resistance to framing; the most rational engaged a frontal region combining emotion and reasoning, with little conflict.

## Mental Models
- Use "restate it in the opposite frame" when a choice feels obvious—if your preference flips (KEEP £20 vs. LOSE £30), it was never reality-bound.
- Use broad, inclusive accounts when a loss tempts you to abandon a plan—"would I buy tickets if I'd lost equivalent cash?"
- Use the "which formulation should we adopt?" question once you see a frame controls the outcome—neutrality between opt-in and opt-out is a decision, not an abstention.
- Use gallons-per-mile (or the outcome unit that scales correctly) when a familiar metric distorts the math.

## Anti-patterns
- **Passively accepting the given frame**: System 2's laziness means we rarely reframe and never discover our preferences are frame-bound.
- **Assuming a "true" preference under the frame**: In the Asian disease, KEEP/LOSE, and surgery/radiation cases there is no masked underlying preference—moral intuitions attach to descriptions, not substance.
- **Emotional word-leakage in expert decisions**: Physicians chose surgery 84% under "90% survival" but only 50% under "10% mortality"—training is no defense.
- **Ignoring default design**: Leaving opt-in/opt-out formulation to chance when it can swing an important outcome from 4% to 100%.

## Worked Example
The Asian disease problem: 600 people are threatened. Frame 1—Program A "200 will be saved" vs. Program B "1/3 chance 600 saved, 2/3 chance none saved." A substantial majority choose the sure A (risk averse when outcomes are framed as gains). Frame 2—Program A′ "400 will die" vs. Program B′ "1/3 chance nobody dies, 2/3 chance 600 die." Now a large majority choose the gamble B′ (risk seeking when outcomes are framed as losses). A=A′ and B=B′ in every objective respect; only "saved" vs. "die" changed. Confronted with the inconsistency, people fall into embarrassed silence—System 2 has no moral intuition of its own to resolve it, because the original preference came from System 1's reaction to words. Amos found even public-health officials susceptible.

## Key Takeaways
1. Logically equivalent frames evoke different choices because meaning, for Humans, is associative reaction, not truth-condition.
2. Risk attitudes reverse with framing: risk averse for gains, risk seeking for losses—the same options can flip preference (Asian disease).
3. There is often no underlying preference the frame distorts; preferences and moral intuitions are attached to descriptions, not substance.
4. Reframing is effortful and System 2 is lazy, so we passively accept problems as presented—experts included.
5. Some frames are objectively better (gallons-per-mile, cash-lost accounts); choosing the frame is a real, consequential decision.
6. Defaults (opt-in vs. opt-out) can swing important outcomes enormously; neutrality between formulations is itself a choice.
