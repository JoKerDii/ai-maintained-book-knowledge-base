# Chapter 27: The Endowment Effect

## Core Idea
Standard theory (indifference curves) assumes your utility for a state depends only on that state, not on your history — the same Bernoulli's error, minus the reference point. Prospect theory predicts a **status quo bias**: because losses loom larger than gains, the disadvantages of any change outweigh its advantages. Applied to ownership, this yields the **endowment effect**: once you own a good "for use," giving it up is coded as a loss and giving-up hurts more than acquiring pleases, so your selling price far exceeds your buying price. Crucially, the effect vanishes for goods held "for exchange" and for experienced traders.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Endowment effect**: Ownership raises a good's value; the minimum you'll accept to sell (WTA) exceeds the maximum you'll pay to buy (WTP), because loss aversion makes giving up the good hurt more than getting it pleases.
  - When to use: Predicting reluctance to sell, trade, or switch; explaining bid-ask gaps for goods held for use.
  - How: Ask whether the person owns the good and is treating it as a loss to relinquish; if so, expect WTA ≈ 2× WTP.
- **"For use" vs. "for exchange" distinction**: Goods intended to be consumed/enjoyed trigger loss aversion; goods (or money) held as a proxy for exchange do not.
  - When to use: To predict when the endowment effect will or won't appear.
  - How: Classify the good — routine commercial inventory and money show no endowment effect; personally used goods do.

## Key Concepts
- **Status quo bias**: A strong preference to stay put; loss aversion favors minimal changes from the reference situation (holds neighborhoods, marriages, jobs together).
- **Indifference map / reference point omission**: Textbook indifference curves leave out current income/leisure — an unstated, unrealistic assumption that history is irrelevant.
- **Willingness-to-accept (WTA) vs. willingness-to-pay (WTP)**: The selling vs. buying price; their gap measures the endowment effect (Professor R: won't sell wine under $100, won't buy over $35).
- **Held "for use"**: Goods consumed or enjoyed (wine, concert tickets, leisure, standard of living) — subject to loss aversion.
- **Held "for exchange"**: Money and merchant inventory held as a proxy to be traded — no loss aversion, no endowment effect.
- **Sellers / Buyers / Choosers**: Experimental roles; Sellers (~$7.12) far exceed Choosers (~$3.12) and Buyers (~$2.87) though Sellers and Choosers face the identical option.
- **Thinking like a trader**: The Econ's question — "How much do I want this vs. other things I could have?" — which eliminates the endowment effect.

## Mental Models
- Use the endowment effect when someone won't trade something they just acquired: hedonic twins Albert and Ben, indifferent between a raise and extra vacation before receiving one, both refuse to swap afterward because the swap now includes a painful loss.
- Use "for use vs. for exchange" to predict bid-ask gaps: exchanging a $5 bill for five singles or buying shoes triggers no loss aversion; selling a wine bottle or Super Bowl ticket does.
- Use the Chooser test to isolate the effect: Sellers and Choosers walk home with mug-or-money either way, so the ~2× gap is pure reluctance to give up what you hold, not a real difference in value.
- Use trading experience as the antidote: novice card traders cling to their cards; veterans show no endowment effect and trade freely — they've learned to ask the Econ's question.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming a single price for owned goods**: Standard theory says buying and selling prices should match; the persistent WTA–WTP gap shows they don't for goods held for use.
- **Reading indifference curves as history-free**: Omitting the reference point invites the false belief that your current job's terms don't shape how you value a new one.
- **Ignoring who is "in the losses"**: The poor live below their reference point, so every purchase is a loss of another good — they think like traders but under all-loss dynamics, and show no endowment effect.

## Worked Example
The mugs experiment (Kahneman, Knetsch, Thaler): A university mug worth about $6 is handed to half the participants at random (Sellers); the other half (Buyers) may buy one; a third group (Choosers) may take a mug or an equivalent cash sum. Sellers demand about $7.12, Choosers value it at $3.12, and Buyers offer $2.87. Sellers and Choosers face the *identical* end choice — leave with a mug or with money — yet Sellers price it more than twice as high. The only difference is the emotion of giving up something already owned. Consequently the number of trades is less than half of what standard supply-and-demand predicts: the "magic of the market" fails for a good owners expect to use, and the ~2:1 ratio matches the loss aversion coefficient from risky choice.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ownership of goods held "for use" inflates their value; selling prices run about twice buying prices.
2. The endowment effect is loss aversion applied to giving up what you own, built into System 1 (visible even in babies clutching a toy).
3. It disappears for money and goods held "for exchange," and for experienced traders who ask the Econ's question.
4. Loss aversion produces a broad status quo bias that resists trades and changes even when beneficial.
5. Textbook indifference maps err by omitting the reference point — Bernoulli's error again.
6. Reference points are labile; changing them (physical possession, market norms, poverty) turns the effect on or off.
