# Chapter 28: Bad Events

## Core Idea
Loss aversion is one instance of a much broader biological principle: **negativity dominance** — "bad is stronger than good." The brain is built to prioritize threats: it detects danger before conscious awareness (the amygdala responds to masked fearful eyes), processes bad words and disagreeable opinions faster, and lets one cockroach ruin a bowl of cherries while a cherry does nothing for a bowl of cockroaches. Reference points can be the status quo, a goal, or an entitlement; falling short is a loss and defending against losses drives behavior far more forcefully than pursuing equivalent gains.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Negativity dominance**: Bad events, emotions, feedback, and information have more impact and are processed more thoroughly than good ones; threats are privileged above opportunities.
  - When to use: Predicting attention, emotion, relationships, reform, and any asymmetry between avoiding harm and seeking benefit.
  - How: Expect the negative side of any comparison to loom larger; budget accordingly (e.g., good interactions must outnumber bad ~5:1 for a stable relationship).
- **Goals as reference points**: A future goal functions as a reference point — falling short is a loss, exceeding it is a gain — and the aversion to missing dominates the desire to exceed.
  - When to use: Predicting effort, effort collapse after hitting targets, and where people try hardest.
  - How: Identify the salient goal (often a short-term target), then expect maximal effort just below it and slackening once met.
- **Dual entitlements (fairness)**: Existing wage/price/rent is a reference point that customers and workers feel entitled to; firms are entitled to their current profit.
  - When to use: Judging what the public will condemn as unfair.
  - How: A firm may transfer a loss to protect its own entitlement, but exploiting market power to impose losses simply "because it can" is judged unfair.

## Key Concepts
- **Negativity dominance**: Broad asymmetry in which negative stimuli outweigh equivalent positive ones across perception, emotion, and cognition.
- **Amygdala / threat channel**: A superfast neural route that flags danger (even unseen fearful eyes) before the visual cortex produces conscious sight.
- **Goal as reference point**: A target that converts falling short into a felt loss (NYC cabdrivers quit once a daily earnings target is met, violating economic logic).
- **Par as reference point**: In golf, par is the baseline; a bogey is a loss, a birdie a foregone gain — players putt 3.6% better for par than for birdie.
- **Status quo defense**: Loss aversion as a conservative force; territory holders and incumbents almost always win contests because they fight harder to keep what they have.
- **Reference transaction / entitlement**: The existing wage, price, or rent that establishes what customers and workers may not fairly be denied.
- **Dual entitlements**: Firms may pass losses on to protect their own profit entitlement but may not impose losses purely to increase profit.
- **Altruistic punishment**: Third parties will punish unfairness at a cost, with activity in the brain's pleasure centers — possibly the glue of social order.
- **Loss aversion in law**: Legal systems compensate actual out-of-pocket losses more readily than foregone gains ("possession is nine-tenths of the law").

## Mental Models
- Use negativity dominance when weighing threats vs. opportunities: a friendship built over years can be destroyed by a single act; bad impressions form faster and resist disconfirmation.
- Use goals-as-reference-points to predict effort and quitting: cabdrivers work long hours on slow (mild-weather) days chasing a fixed daily target and quit early on lucrative rainy days — the reverse of economic logic.
- Use loss aversion to predict why reforms fail: reforms create many small winners and a few concentrated losers; the losers fight harder, so outcomes skew in their favor, hence grandfather clauses and attrition rather than dismissal.
- Use the reference-transaction rule to anticipate fairness verdicts: raising snow-shovel prices after a storm is condemned (82% unfair) as imposing a loss "because it can," while paying a new hire less is acceptable (entitlement is personal).

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating every stroke, dollar, or concession as symmetric**: Some strokes count more; missing par (a loss) mobilizes more effort than chasing a birdie (a foregone gain).
- **Exploiting market power to impose losses**: Cutting a current worker's wage to $7 because unemployment rose is judged unfair (83%) and is punished by lost productivity and sales.
- **Designing reform as if winners and losers weigh equally**: Concentrated losers out-fight diffuse winners, so plans end up more expensive and less effective than projected.
- **Expecting generosity to be rewarded like meanness is punished**: Brains punish unfairness reliably but do not reward generosity as reliably — another loss/gain asymmetry.

## Worked Example
Pope and Schweitzer analyzed over 2.5 million professional golf putts. Par sets the reference point: a birdie putt is a chance at a gain, a par putt avoids the loss of a bogey. Prospect theory predicts players will concentrate harder to avoid a loss than to secure an equivalent gain — so they should sink par putts more reliably than birdie putts of the same difficulty. They did: at every distance, success was 3.6% higher for par than for birdie. The effect is costly at the top — had Tiger Woods putted for birdies as well as he did for par, his average tournament score would have dropped by a stroke, worth nearly $1 million a season. These elite competitors don't consciously slack on birdies; their aversion to a bogey simply mobilizes extra focus.

## Key Takeaways
1. Loss aversion is a special case of negativity dominance — bad is biologically stronger than good.
2. The brain flags threats faster than opportunities, often before conscious awareness.
3. A reference point can be the status quo, a goal, or an entitlement; missing it registers as a loss.
4. Goals-as-reference-points shape effort — people work hard up to a target and slacken once it's met (cabdrivers, golfers).
5. Loss aversion is a conservative force: incumbents and defenders win, and reforms stall because losers fight harder.
6. Fairness norms are reference-dependent — imposing losses on customers/workers to exploit power is condemned and economically punished, while protecting one's own entitlement is accepted (dual entitlements).
