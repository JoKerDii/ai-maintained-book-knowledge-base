# Chapter 13: Availability, Emotion, and Risk

## Core Idea
The availability heuristic warps public and personal perception of risk because our estimates of danger track the emotional intensity and media prevalence of a threat rather than its true frequency. Ease of retrieval and emotional reaction are inextricably linked: frightening, vivid images come to mind easily and in turn amplify fear. This feeds the affect heuristic (letting feelings answer questions of fact) and, socially, availability cascades — self-sustaining media-and-public feedback loops that inflate minor risks and misallocate public resources.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Affect heuristic**: People judge and decide by consulting emotion — "Do I like it? Do I hate it?" — substituting the easy question "How do I feel about it?" for the hard one "What do I think about it?"
  - When to use: To spot judgments dominated by liking/disliking rather than evidence.
  - How: Watch for implausibly tidy views where a liked option has all benefits and no costs; force yourself to consider real tradeoffs.
- **Availability cascade (Kuran & Sunstein)**: A self-sustaining chain in which a minor event → media coverage → public alarm → more coverage → panic and large-scale government action.
  - When to use: To diagnose why a specific risk suddenly dominates policy attention.
  - How: Trace whether concern is driven by escalating coverage and emotion rather than by new evidence; watch for "availability entrepreneurs" who keep the worrying news flowing.
- **Probability neglect (Sunstein)**: The mind either ignores a small risk entirely or gives it far too much weight — nothing in between.
  - When to use: When reasoning about rare, dramatic hazards (terrorism, contamination scares).
  - How: Deliberately think about the denominator (base rate), not just the vivid numerator (the tragic story).

## Key Concepts
- **Availability and affect linked**: Fluent, vivid images of danger heighten fear; heightened fear makes the images more available — a loop.
- **Media distortion of death estimates**: Coverage biased toward novelty and poignancy; the public over-estimates dramatic causes and under-estimates common ones.
- **Availability entrepreneurs**: Individuals/organizations who deliberately sustain a flow of alarming news to drive a cascade.
- **Risk as constructed, not objective (Slovic)**: "Risk" does not exist "out there" waiting to be measured; the choice of a mortality measure (deaths per million people vs. per dollar of product) is "an exercise in power."
- **Experts vs. the public**: Experts count lives/life-years; the public draws finer, legitimate distinctions (good vs. bad deaths, voluntary vs. involuntary risk) — a genuine conflict of values, not just lay bias.
- **Worst-case anchoring in protection**: Defenses are built to the worst disaster actually experienced (river high-water marks); images of a worse disaster don't come easily to mind.

## Mental Models
- Use "check the numerator vs. denominator" when a rare threat feels overwhelming: you are imagining the vivid case, not the probability.
- Use "the emotional tail wags the rational dog" (Haidt) to catch affect driving belief — when liking a technology more makes you rate it as less risky with no new evidence.
- Use "disaster–concern–complacency cycle" (Kunreuther) to anticipate that vigilance after an earthquake or flood will fade as memory dims.
- Use "is this a cascade?" when a topic suddenly fills every screen: ask whether coverage, not new facts, is escalating the alarm.

## Anti-patterns
- **Switching trains after two plane crashes**: The risk hasn't changed; availability has.
- **Ignoring low-coverage risks**: Underrating indoor pollution because few media stories exist.
- **Letting affect erase tradeoffs**: Believing a favored technology has large benefits and negligible risks because you like it — even British toxicologists did this.
- **Insulating experts entirely from public emotion**: Produces technically "rational" policies the public rejects — unworkable in a democracy.

## Worked Example
The Slovic–Lichtenstein–Fischhoff causes-of-death survey. Participants saw pairs of causes (e.g., stroke vs. accidents, tornado vs. asthma, lightning vs. botulism), picked the more frequent, and estimated the ratio; judgments were checked against health statistics. Results systematically tracked media vividness, not reality: strokes cause nearly twice the deaths of all accidents combined, yet 80% judged accidental death more likely; tornadoes were seen as deadlier than asthma, which actually kills 20x more; death by lightning was judged less likely than botulism though it is 52x more frequent; accidents were judged 300x more likely than diabetes, though diabetes is roughly 4x more deadly. Unusual, dramatic causes (botulism, tornadoes) are over-covered and thus perceived as more common than they are — a direct readout of associative availability. Slovic's team then showed the affect side: rating technologies, people who were told only of a technology's *benefits* also came to see it as *less risky*, and vice versa — belief about risk moved with feeling, absent any new risk evidence.

## Key Takeaways
1. Perceived risk tracks emotional intensity and media coverage, not statistical frequency.
2. The affect heuristic makes judgments follow liking/disliking, producing an unrealistically tidy world with no painful tradeoffs.
3. Availability cascades convert minor events into large-scale panic and policy through media-public feedback, sometimes stoked by availability entrepreneurs.
4. Probability neglect means small risks are either ignored or hugely over-weighted — we picture the numerator, not the denominator.
5. Terrorism is the most potent modern cascade-inducer: casualties are small versus other causes, but availability keeps everyone on edge — it speaks directly to System 1.
6. Good risk policy should neither surrender to irrational fear nor ignore it; fear itself is harmful, so it must be managed alongside real dangers (Kahneman sides with both Sunstein and Slovic).
