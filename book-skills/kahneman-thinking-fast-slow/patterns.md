# Patterns & Techniques — Thinking, Fast and Slow

Concrete, reusable methods Kahneman endorses for making better judgments and decisions.

## Take the Outside View (Reference-Class Forecasting)
**When to use**: Any plan, estimate, or forecast (project timelines, budgets, success odds).
**How**:
1. Identify a reference class of past, similar cases.
2. Get the distribution of actual outcomes for that class (the base rate).
3. Use that base rate as your anchor.
4. Adjust for your specific case only with information that has proven predictive value.
**Trade-offs**: Feels impersonal and can be resisted ("we're different"); requires real reference data. Counters the planning fallacy and optimism bias.

## Run a Premortem
**When to use**: Before committing to a major decision the group is converging on.
**How**: Assemble the people who know about the decision. Say: "Imagine it's a year from now and this plan has failed disastrously. Write the history of that failure." Legitimizing dissent surfaces risks that groupthink and the illusion of validity suppress.
**Trade-offs**: Doesn't stop bad decisions outright; it widens the search for problems and tempers overconfidence.

## Decompose an Intuitive Prediction (Regress Toward the Mean)
**When to use**: Predicting an outcome (GPA, sales, performance) from an impression or single indicator.
**How**:
1. Start from the baseline (the average outcome / base rate).
2. Make your intuitive prediction from the evidence.
3. Estimate the correlation between your evidence and the outcome.
4. Move from baseline toward your intuitive prediction by that fraction (e.g. correlation 0.3 → move 30% of the way).
**Trade-offs**: Deliberately sacrifices vivid, extreme predictions for calibrated moderate ones; feels less satisfying but is more accurate.

## Adopt Broad Framing & Risk Policies
**When to use**: Repeated, small, independent risky decisions (investments, insurance, deadlines).
**How**: Decide a standing rule you'll apply to the whole class ("never buy extended warranties"; "always take the favorable gamble if I can afford the loss") rather than agonizing over each instance in isolation.
**Trade-offs**: Reduces loss-averse, narrow-framing errors and emotional churn; requires discipline to hold the policy when a single loss stings.

## Use Algorithms / Simple Formulas Over Clinical Judgment
**When to use**: Repeated predictions in low-validity, noisy environments (hiring, admissions, loan approval, medical triage).
**How**: Select 2–6 predictive, independent attributes; score each on a standard scale; sum with equal weights. Trust the total over a holistic gut feel. (Apgar score is the archetype.)
**Trade-offs**: Experts resist ceding judgment; formulas beat them mainly because they're consistent, not smarter.

## Structure Interviews to Beat the Halo Effect
**When to use**: Evaluating candidates or options where a first impression contaminates the rest.
**How**: Predefine 4–6 independent traits. Score each trait separately, one at a time, across all candidates before forming an overall view. Sum the scores; give the formula its due weight.
**Trade-offs**: Slower and less "intuitive"; sharply improves validity.

## Reframe to Neutralize Framing Effects
**When to use**: Any consequential choice stated in one frame (losses vs gains, survival vs mortality, monthly vs annual cost).
**How**: Restate the same facts in the opposite frame and in a common unit; decide only if your choice survives both framings. Adopt "Econ" defaults where possible.
**Trade-offs**: Effortful System 2 work; most people won't do it unaided — so design defaults for others (nudges).

## Trust Expert Intuition Only Under Two Conditions
**When to use**: Deciding whether to believe a confident expert (or yourself).
**How**: Ask (1) Is the environment regular and predictable enough to contain valid cues? (2) Did the expert have prolonged practice with rapid, unambiguous feedback? Both yes → trust it. Otherwise treat the confidence as an illusion of validity.
**Trade-offs**: Confidence and subjective certainty are not evidence of skill; long experience in an irregular world (e.g. stock picking) breeds skill-less confidence.

## Guard Against WYSIATI
**When to use**: Whenever a story feels coherent and conclusion feels obvious.
**How**: Ask "What would I need to know that I don't?" and "What's the quality and quantity of the evidence?" Deliberately seek disconfirming data and the base rate.
**Trade-offs**: Coherence is seductive; this friction is the price of calibration.

## Design for the Peak-End & Duration Neglect
**When to use**: Designing experiences (service, care, events) or reasoning about your own well-being.
**How**: Improve the most intense moment and, especially, the ending; don't assume adding pleasant-but-milder time at the end helps memory. For your own choices, ask whether you're optimizing the experiencing self or the remembering self.
**Trade-offs**: What memory rewards (a good ending) and what the experiencing self lives through can diverge.
