# Glossary — Thinking, Fast and Slow

**Affect heuristic** — Letting likes and dislikes determine beliefs about risks and benefits; emotion substitutes for analysis (Ch 9, Ch 13).

**Affective forecasting** — Predicting how future events will make you feel; usually distorted by the focusing illusion (Ch 38).

**Anchoring effect** — A number you consider before estimating pulls your estimate toward it, even when arbitrary (Ch 11).

**Anchoring index** — The ratio measuring how much an anchor moves estimates; ~55% is common (Ch 11).

**Associative activation** — One idea automatically triggering a cascade of linked ideas, emotions, and bodily responses (Ch 4).

**Availability cascade** — A self-sustaining chain where media coverage of a minor risk breeds public fear that breeds more coverage (Ch 13).

**Availability heuristic** — Judging frequency or probability by how easily examples come to mind (Ch 12, Ch 13).

**Base rate** — The prior probability of an outcome in the population, before individuating evidence (Ch 14, Ch 16).

**Base-rate neglect** — Ignoring statistical base rates in favor of a representative description (Ch 14, Ch 16).

**Bernoulli's error** — Modeling utility as a function of wealth states rather than changes relative to a reference point (Ch 25, Ch 26).

**Broad framing** — Evaluating decisions as part of a portfolio of many similar choices rather than one at a time (Ch 31, Ch 34).

**Certainty effect** — Overweighting outcomes that move from very likely to certain; drives the fourfold pattern (Ch 29).

**Cognitive ease** — A pleasant state of low mental strain signaling that things are familiar, true, and safe; opposite of cognitive strain (Ch 5).

**Competition neglect** — Ignoring rivals' plans when forecasting your own success ("this town is big enough only for us") (Ch 24).

**Conjunction fallacy** — Judging a specific conjunction (Linda is a feminist bank teller) as more probable than one of its parts (Ch 15).

**Decision weights** — The psychological weights the mind assigns to outcomes, differing from their objective probabilities (Ch 29, Ch 30).

**Denominator neglect** — Focusing on the numerator (13 winning marbles) and ignoring the denominator, so vivid frequencies beat probabilities (Ch 30).

**Diminishing sensitivity** — Each additional unit of gain or loss feels smaller as you move away from the reference point (Ch 26).

**Disposition effect** — Selling winners and holding losers to avoid closing a mental account at a loss (Ch 32).

**Duration neglect** — When evaluating an experience, ignoring how long it lasted (Ch 35).

**Ego depletion** — Reduced self-control and effort after prior mental exertion (Ch 3).

**Endowment effect** — Valuing something more merely because you own it; selling price exceeds buying price (Ch 27).

**Experiencing self** — The self that lives in the present moment and feels each experience as it happens (Ch 35, Ch 37).

**Expert intuition** — Reliable pattern recognition, valid only in high-validity, regular environments with prolonged practice and rapid feedback (Ch 22).

**Focusing illusion** — "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it"; attention inflates the felt weight of any factor (Ch 38).

**Fourfold pattern** — The signature pattern of risk attitudes across gains/losses × high/low probability (Ch 29).

**Framing effect** — Logically equivalent descriptions (survival vs. mortality, 90% fat-free vs. 10% fat) producing different choices (Ch 34).

**Halo effect** — Letting one salient trait (or first impression) color your evaluation of everything else about a person (Ch 7, Ch 19).

**Hindsight bias** — After an event, believing you knew it would happen; the "I-knew-it-all-along" effect (Ch 19).

**Illusion of validity** — Unjustified confidence in a judgment that fits a coherent story, despite poor predictive accuracy (Ch 20).

**Intensity matching** — Translating a value on one scale (crime severity) onto another (loudness, dollars) automatically (Ch 8, Ch 9).

**Law of small numbers** — Overtrusting small samples and seeing patterns in what is really sampling noise (Ch 10).

**Loss aversion** — Losses loom larger than equivalent gains, by a factor of roughly 1.5–2.5 (Ch 26, Ch 28).

**Mental accounting** — Keeping money and outcomes in separate psychological accounts that shouldn't be treated separately (Ch 32).

**Mere exposure effect** — Repeated exposure to a stimulus increasing liking for it (Ch 5).

**Mood/coherence (WYSIATI)** — "What You See Is All There Is": building confident stories from available evidence while ignoring what's missing (Ch 7, Ch 19–20).

**Narrative fallacy** — Constructing simple, coherent stories of the past that overweight skill and intention and hide the role of luck (Ch 19).

**Norm theory** — System 1 maintains a model of what's normal; deviations trigger surprise and a search for causes (Ch 6).

**Outside view** — Forecasting by using the statistics of a reference class of similar cases, instead of the details of your own case (Ch 23).

**Peak-end rule** — Retrospective evaluation of an episode is dominated by its most intense moment and its ending (Ch 35).

**Planning fallacy** — Plans and forecasts that are unrealistically close to best-case scenarios (Ch 23).

**Possibility effect** — Overweighting a change from impossible to merely possible (0% → 5%) far more than a proportional change in the middle (Ch 29, Ch 30).

**Priming** — Exposure to one idea unconsciously influencing thoughts and actions related to it (Ch 4).

**Prospect theory** — Kahneman & Tversky's descriptive theory of choice: value defined over gains/losses from a reference point, with loss aversion, diminishing sensitivity, and nonlinear decision weights (Ch 26).

**Regression to the mean** — Extreme observations tend to be followed by more average ones, purely statistically (Ch 17).

**Regression fallacy** — Inventing a causal story for what is only regression to the mean (praise/punishment "working") (Ch 17).

**Remembering self** — The self that keeps score, tells the story, and makes decisions based on memories, not moments (Ch 35, Ch 36).

**Representativeness** — Judging probability by similarity to a stereotype or prototype, ignoring base rates and sample size (Ch 14, Ch 15).

**Reference point** — The status quo or expectation against which outcomes are coded as gains or losses (Ch 26).

**Risk policy** — A standing rule to treat repeated small risks broadly ("always take the favorable gamble"), reducing narrow-framing errors (Ch 31).

**Substitution** — Answering an easier heuristic question in place of the hard target question, without noticing (Ch 9).

**Sunk-cost fallacy** — Continuing a failing endeavor because of resources already invested (Ch 32).

**System 1** — Fast, automatic, effortless, associative, intuitive thinking; always on (Ch 1).

**System 2** — Slow, effortful, deliberate, rule-governed thinking; lazy and capacity-limited (Ch 1, Ch 3).

**Theory-induced blindness** — Once you accept a theory, its flaws become nearly impossible to see (Ch 25, Ch 26).

**U-index** — The fraction of time a person spends in an unpleasant emotional state (Ch 37).

**Utility (experienced vs. decision)** — Experienced utility is the pleasure/pain actually felt; decision utility is the weight given to an outcome when choosing (Ch 25, Ch 37).
