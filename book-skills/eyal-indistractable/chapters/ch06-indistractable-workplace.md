# Chapter 26–28: Part 5 — How to Make Your Workplace Indistractable

## Core Idea
Chronic tech overuse at work is a *symptom*, not the disease. The disease is a dysfunctional culture lacking psychological safety. Fix distraction by giving people a safe forum to talk about it — which surfaces every other buried problem too.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Distraction Is a Sign of Dysfunction**: Two conditions predict workplace depression (Stansfeld & Candy): **high job strain** (high expectations + low control) and **effort-reward imbalance**. Both reduce to a *lack of control*, which people numb with devices.
- **Cycle of Responsiveness** (Leslie Perlow): Off-hours responsiveness → others expect it → more requests → more pressure → an always-on norm. Tech perpetuates it, but a dysfunctional culture *causes* it.
- **Psychological Safety** (Amy Edmondson): "A belief that one will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes." Google's Project Aristotle found it underpins the other four team dynamics (dependability, structure/clarity, meaning, impact).
- **Creating psychological safety (Edmondson's 3 steps)**:
  1. Frame the work as a **learning** problem, not an execution problem.
  2. **Acknowledge your own fallibility** ("nobody has all the answers").
  3. **Model curiosity** — ask lots of questions.
- **Start Small + Open Dialogue**: Don't overhaul the company. Pilot one change (e.g. "one predictable night off per week") with one willing team, meet regularly to remove roadblocks — the meeting itself becomes the forum for open dialogue.

## Key Concepts
- **Job strain**: High demands + low control → depression-like symptoms → distraction.
- **Effort-reward imbalance**: Little pay/recognition return for hard work.
- **Learning zone** (Edmondson): High motivation + high psychological safety, where teams perform best.

## Mental Models
- If a company *can't* talk about tech overuse, it's also hiding deeper problems — tech overuse is the canary.
- Leaders must **model** focused work (schedule email/Slack blocks, phone-free meetings) — culture follows the top.

## Worked Example
**Leslie Perlow at Boston Consulting Group.** BCG's always-on culture drove attrition. The managing partner refused to let Perlow touch his teams but let her "wander" and find a willing partner — Doug, a young partner with kids. Perlow's experiment: give Doug's team *one predictable night off per week.* For years consultants had killed such ideas with "We're in the service business" / "What if a client needs us?" But with a safe space to ask "Why do things have to be this way?", the team found simple solutions — and the meetings blossomed into a forum for airing far more than disconnection. The result stopped the cycle of responsiveness, raised job satisfaction and retention, and spread firm-wide (even to the skeptical Boston office). **Slack** shows the steady-state version: the slogan "Work hard and go home," after-hours DMs discouraged, built-in Do Not Disturb, and feedback channels (#exec-ama, #beef-tweets) where leaders acknowledge with an "eyes" emoji and a check when fixed — psychological safety operationalized.

## Anti-patterns
- **Blaming the technology**: Banning Slack/email treats the symptom while the low-control culture festers.
- **Suffering in silence**: A workplace where you can't raise tech overuse is one where insights die unspoken.
- **Piling on more hours** as a response to underperformance: deepens strain and pushback.

## Key Takeaways
1. Tech overuse is a symptom; dysfunctional, low-control culture is the disease.
2. High expectations + low control → depression-like symptoms → distraction.
3. Psychological safety is the antidote and the top driver of team effectiveness.
4. Fix it by starting small and opening a safe dialogue, not by overhauling everything.
5. Leaders must exemplify focused, disconnected work.
