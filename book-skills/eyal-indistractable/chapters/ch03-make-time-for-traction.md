# Chapter 9–12: Part 2 — Make Time for Traction

## Core Idea
You can't call something a distraction unless you know what it's distracting you *from*. Turn your values into time by **timeboxing** a calendar template across the three life domains — then sync it with the stakeholders who share your time.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Life Domains**: (1) **You**, (2) **Relationships**, (3) **Work**. You sit at the center; the other two depend on your health. Allocate time across all three so your calendar reflects who you want to be.
  - How: Decide how much time each domain deserves to be consistent with your values, then build a weekly "perfect week" template.
- **Timeboxing (Implementation Intentions)**: Decide *what* you'll do and *when*. Eliminate all white space on the calendar. Success is not measured by *what* you did but by *whether you did what you planned*.
  - Why it works: Constraints beat a blank schedule + infinite to-do list, which torment you with choice. It's the only way to tell traction from distraction.
- **Reflect & Refine (weekly 15-minute review)**: Book 15 min weekly to ask:
  1. **Reflect**: "When did I do what I said, and when did I get distracted?"
  2. **Refine**: "What changes to my calendar will give me time to better live my values?"
  - Treat each week as a mini-experiment; be a curious scientist, not a drill sergeant.
- **Control the Inputs, Not the Outcomes**: You can't guarantee outputs (falling asleep, having a breakthrough), only the *time you put in*. Show up in the right place at the right time. "The body gets what the body needs."
- **Schedule Syncing with Stakeholders**: Share your timeboxed schedule with managers/family. Sync as often as your schedule changes (usually weekly). It clarifies the trust pact and stops others stealing your time with pseudowork.

## Key Concepts
- **Residual beneficiary**: The chump who gets whatever's left over. Don't let loved ones become the residual beneficiaries of your time.
- **Pseudowork**: Tasks that look like work but aren't aligned with top priorities (busywork, over-emailing, needless meetings) — often unconscious pushback against overload.
- **Values**: "How we want to be, what we want to stand for" (Russ Harris). A guiding star you navigate by, never a box you check off.

## Mental Models
- Treat appointments with yourself as unbreakable as a meeting with your boss.
- "**The time you plan to waste is not wasted time**" — scheduled scrolling or a nap is traction if you planned it.
- If you don't plan your day, someone else will.

## Worked Example
**April**, an ad-sales exec with $15M quota and two months to close a third of it, felt she was "too slow" and tried to fix it by working more hours — neglecting every other domain. Her real problem wasn't productivity; it was the lack of a timeboxed schedule plus a self-limiting belief. She subdivided her day: focused proposal-writing first (uninterrupted), then a block for client calls, then an afternoon block for email. When she shared the schedule with her manager David, he was *relieved* — he'd rather know when to reach her, and agreed she could skip low-value meetings as long as she protected revenue time. They set a standing 15-minute Monday sync. Outcome: more control, less phone-tethering at night, and a week that respected her values. **Schedule syncing turned an implicit, resentful overload into an explicit, mutual agreement.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Does your calendar reflect your values? If not, you can't live them.
2. Timebox every hour across You / Relationships / Work — judge success by adherence, not output.
3. Reflect and refine weekly; commit to the schedule until the next revision.
4. Control inputs (showing up), not outcomes you can't guarantee.
5. Sync your schedule with stakeholders as often as it changes — visibility prevents distraction from others.
