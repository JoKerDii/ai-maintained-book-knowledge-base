# Section 2: Messages and Message Supports (Lessons 9–22)

## Core Idea
The foundation of every interview is a small, deliberate set of messages — and the "supports" (stories, statistics, sound bites) that let you repeat their themes endlessly without sounding repetitive. This is the most-referenced toolkit in the book.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Message (definition)**: A one-sentence statement combining one of *your* most important points with one of your *audience's* most important needs or values. Aim for exactly **three** messages.
  - When to use: As the spine of all communication — media, speeches, website, brochures, casual conversation.
  - How: Not a slogan (a few words) — a fully formed sentence. Often includes a call to action.
- **CUBE A** — the five elements every message needs:
  - **C — Consistent**: Repeat the same themes across every platform. Audiences need **7–15 exposures** (sometimes more) to move from unawareness to action. Vary words, never themes.
  - **U — Unburdened**: Free of wordiness, jargon, and abstractions. Aim for ≤2 commas, ≤30 words, concrete images.
  - **B — Brief**: One sentence each. The average TV news quote is **7.3 seconds (~18 words)**; print quotes run 8–20 words.
  - **E — Ear-worthy**: Written for the ear, not the eye. Short words, short sentences, everyday words, contractions; speak each message aloud to test it.
  - **A — Audience Focused**: Incorporate the audience's needs (Maslow: safety, family, health, respect; plus time, work-life balance, convenience) and values (patriotism, compassion, self-reliance).
- **Four Message Types**: **Fact/Result**, **Problem/Solution**, **Advocacy/Call-to-Action**, **Benefits**. Mix and match across your three messages.
- **Message Supports (the three-legged stool)**: **Stories, Statistics, Sound Bites**. Build **at least two of each** for every message → ~21 ways to stay on message without repeating yourself.

## Key Concepts
- **Effective frequency**: The number of exposures needed before a message drives action — Phillips' rule is 7–15.
- **Message disconnect**: When your words say one thing and you deliver them flatly; covered more in Ch 5.
- **Sound bite**: A short, witty/memorable phrase expressing a message. Most great ones are planned in advance, delivered as if improvised.
- **Three story plots** (from *Made to Stick*): Challenge (David vs. Goliath), Connection (bridging a gap), Creativity (a breakthrough).

## Mental Models
- Think of statistics as *context*, not raw numbers: "It would take 122 Fenway Parks to hold every American with Alzheimer's." Make numbers personal, avoid percentages, use ratios, give relative distance.
- Think "think smaller" for stories: one customer, one community — not a "big" story.
- Use the message-support stool to **balance**: if you're a "statistics person," add a story, and vice versa (profession predicts preference better than gender).

## Anti-patterns
- **Adding a second sentence to a message**: Breaks Brief. Headline writers compress wars into a phrase; you can compress your point into one sentence.
- **Writing for the eye**: Long, multi-clause, jargon-laden sentences that can't be spoken aloud.
- **Citing raw numbers**: "4.5 million Americans have Alzheimer's" evokes nothing without context.
- **Reciting messages verbatim**: Infuriates the audience; that's what supports prevent.

## Worked Example
**The Pennsylvania obstetrician message + supports** (used throughout the book):
- **Message** (Problem/Solution): "By loosening Pennsylvania's unreasonable malpractice laws, thousands of pregnant women will be able to find a local obstetrician who can help in case of a medical emergency."
- **Story**: "Jane Jackson, 26, from Altoona, was in her seventh month when she went into labor alone… the paramedics couldn't help when her baby couldn't breathe. Her son died. A closer obstetrician likely would have saved him."
- **Statistic**: "More than 18,000 women of childbearing age in Pennsylvania live at least 100 miles from the closest obstetrician."
- **Sound bite**: "Having your doctor 100 miles away is like keeping your Band-Aids at a friend's house — useless when you need them most."
In a 3-question sample interview, the spokesperson answers Q1 with the message, Q2 with story→message, Q3 with statistic→sound bite — fully on message, never repetitive.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build exactly three one-sentence messages, each marrying your point to an audience need/value.
2. Run every message through CUBE A — especially "speak it aloud" for Ear-worthy.
3. For each message, prepare ≥2 stories, ≥2 statistics, ≥2 sound bites (~21 on-message answers total).
4. Give statistics context; make them personal, use ratios, avoid bare percentages.
5. Plan sound bites in advance; deliver them as if improvised.
