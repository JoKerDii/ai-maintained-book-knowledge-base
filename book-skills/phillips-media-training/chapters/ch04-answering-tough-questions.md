# Section 4: Answering the Tough Questions (Lessons 33–47)

## Core Idea
You will rarely be asked the "right" question. The ATMs give you a repeatable system to answer *any* question — off-topic, hostile, or trap — while getting your message out and avoiding the costly "seven-second stray."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The ATMs** — the book's central technique for tough questions:
  - **A — Answer**: Answer every question, often in a single word/phrase ("Yes," "No," "That's not quite right"). First, do no harm.
  - **T — Transition**: A short bridge phrase from their question to your message ("It's important to remember that…", "Here's what we know…").
  - **M — Message**: Your message and message supports.
  - **s — sell** (lowercase): An optional closing call to action — use only in *some* answers.
  - **Ratio**: Spend just 10–20% on A+T, 80–90% on M+s. Don't use the ATMs on every answer (looks inauthentic). When the transition can serve as the answer, drop the "A."
- **The Seven-Second Stray**: One off-message moment in an otherwise perfect interview becomes the only quote that airs — repeatedly. Your message is your "lifeboat"; keep swimming back to it.
- **Rewrite the Hostile Question**: Silently restate a nasty question in neutral terms, then answer that: "I disagree with your question's premise, and am happy to share a few of our accomplishments…"
- **The Peter Jennings Rule** (for unknown answers): Tell the public what you *do* know, not what you don't. "That's unclear, but here's what we know at this hour…"

## Key Concepts
- **Three dangerous reporter types**: (1) *The Friendly Guy at the Bar* — warmth aimed at loosening your tongue; his loyalty is to the story. (2) *The Quiet Type* — silence that baits you into filling the void; stay quiet or ask "What else can I answer?" (3) *The Jerk* — hostility designed to provoke; ignore tone, answer calmly.
- **Stay Cool**: Anger, sarcasm, and walking away all lose. Audiences reward grace under pressure; their sympathy shifts to you if the interviewer seems unfair.
- **The Ambush**: Win by denying the reporter a "money shot" — stay calm, don't cover the lens, restate your message while walking forward.

## Mental Models
- Reframe your interior monologue from "Oh no, tough question" to "I'm glad you asked — this lets me discuss that." Openness defangs interviewers.
- Treat tough questions as **opportunities to demonstrate competence**, not threats.

## Worked Example — the ten trap-question types and how to escape
- **Don't know the answer** → Peter Jennings Rule (say what you know).
- **Speculation / hypothetical** → "I can't speculate, but here's what I can tell you…" (Geithner's "No risk" on the U.S. credit downgrade became a months-long highlight reel.)
- **Personal opinion vs. company** → "I'm speaking on behalf of the agency, and can tell you what *we* believe…" (Whole Foods' Mackey's "personal" op-ed still triggered boycotts.)
- **Paraphrase** → Reject it unless 100% accurate; don't repeat the loaded word (Mittermeier refused to call his board "radical").
- **False choice (this/that, yes/no)** → "Asking me to choose between my child's health and happiness is like choosing between air and water."
- **Left field** → pledge to learn more, talk about what you do know, or zoom out and generalize.
- **False premise** → reject immediately: "I don't agree with the premise of your question," then ATMs (drop the A).
- **Demand for a guarantee** → talk about what you *can* guarantee: "Here's what I can guarantee: we will put every available safeguard in place…"
- **About your competition** → "I'll let Company X speak for itself, but here's why our product is a hit…"
- **Indirect attribution ("people say," "there's a rumor")** → dismiss quickly, don't engage the anonymous claim, return to message.

## Anti-patterns
- **Taking the bait** (BP's Tony Hayward: "I'd like my life back") — the seven-second stray that became the symbol of the whole crisis.
- **Using the ATMs on every answer** — reads as canned and evasive.
- **Answering a false-choice or guarantee question literally** ("There are no guarantees in life") — produces a dreadful quote.
- **Walking off / sarcasm / visible anger** — guarantees the clip gets replayed.

## Key Takeaways
1. Answer → Transition → Message → sell; 10–20% getting there, 80–90% message.
2. One seven-second stray drowns an hour of on-message answers — always swim back to the lifeboat.
3. For every trap question there's a move: reject false premises, reframe false choices, guarantee what you can, deflect anonymous attributions.
4. Stay cool — anger, sarcasm, and walk-offs always lose; the audience rewards grace.
5. Rewrite hostile questions in your head and answer the neutral version.
