# Section 1: Eight Ground Rules for Working with the Media (Lessons 1–8)

## Core Idea
Before you say a word, you make strategic choices — whether to talk at all, how fast, and on what terms. These eight ground rules govern your relationship with reporters so you don't hand them ammunition or forfeit your share of the story.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Rule of Thirds**: A news story has three voices — yours, your opponent's, and the reporter's. Refuse to comment and you go "0-for-3"; your opponent fills your third and the reporter slants toward whoever talked.
  - When to use: Deciding whether to accept any interview about you/your company.
  - How: If the story runs with or without you, participate — 1-for-3 beats 0-for-3. Decline only if the topic is irrelevant, off-brand, or genuinely gains you nothing. **Second part of the rule: don't make your opponent's case** — never volunteer "balance" (e.g. "their product has nice features, but…"); the reporter can quote just the concession.
- **Comment Without Commenting**: Decline to answer a specific question by explaining *why* you can't, instead of saying "no comment."
  - When to use: Confidential records, impending layoffs, labor actions, sensitive deals, competitive info, lawsuits, death/injury before family notification.
  - How: "We don't release financial figures on specific products, because it could give competitors an edge. What I can tell you is the product was significantly more profitable than we anticipated."
- **Know Your Rights**: You can't demand to see a story pre-publication, but you can offer to fact-check, sometimes request questions in advance, tape the interview, and limit its time.

## Key Concepts
- **"No comment"**: The most damning phrase in a spokesperson's lexicon — reads as "Guilty!" Any phrasing conveying the same evasion does equal damage.
- **Off the record**: Information that can't be used in any form — but journalists can't agree on the definition, so the term is unreliable. Banish it.
- **On background**: Information usable, but the source can't be named or quoted.
- **Not for attribution**: Usable and quotable, but attributed to a veiled source ("a senior official") — negotiate how you're described.
- **On the record**: The default. Assume everything you say is on the record.

## Mental Models
- Think of every interaction with a reporter — phone, email, hallway small talk, before and after the "official" interview — as fully on the record. There is no such thing as an "official" start/stop.
- Treat a returned call's *timing* as strategy, not courtesy: call back fast (before the story is written) to shape it, not just before deadline to be inserted into it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Saying "no comment"**: Reads as a confession; reporters frame the story to make you look guilty.
- **Meeting the deadline but waiting until the last minute**: The reporter has already written 95% of the story; your quote gets slotted in with no influence on the angle.
- **Going off the record without preparing for the risk**: Reporters break agreements, editors overrule them, courts can compel disclosure. If you can't afford to be named, stay on the record.
- **Making your opponent's case for "balance"**: Hands the reporter a damaging out-of-context quote.

## Worked Example
**Mark McGwire before Congress (2005)** — Asked whether he took steroids, McGwire repeated "I'm not here to talk about the past" nine times. He never confessed, but the evasion *was* the confession in the public's eyes: he was branded a cheat, became a pariah, and Hall of Fame voters have rejected him ever since. The lesson: evasion that reads as "no comment" convicts you as surely as an admission. Contrast President Obama on *Larry King* deflecting an Arizona-immigration-lawsuit question — he transitioned to *why* he couldn't answer ("that's the job of the Justice Department… I wouldn't put my thumb on the scales") and escaped clean.

## Key Takeaways
1. Almost always do the interview if the story is about you — 1-for-3 beats 0-for-3.
2. Never say "no comment" or anything that smells like it; explain *why* you can't answer instead.
3. Return reporters' calls fast — before they write — not merely by deadline.
4. "Off the record" is meaningless; assume everything is on the record, before, during, and after.
5. You may offer to fact-check, tape the interview, or limit its time — set limits when arranging, not conducting, the interview (or it looks defensive).
