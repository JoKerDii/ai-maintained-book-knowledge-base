# Section 7: Crisis Communications — The 10 Truths of a Crisis (Lessons 78–92)

## Core Idea
A crisis itself rarely does the most damage — the *handling* does. Most crises are predictable, so they can be planned and drilled. Ten "truths" govern how the media will cast you and how to come out with your reputation intact.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Crisis (definition)**: An event, precipitated by a specific incident, that attracts critical media attention for a definite period — more than ordinary bad press.
- **Eight crisis categories** (mostly predictable): Natural disasters, Unnatural disasters, Product, Policy, Process, Employee, Personal, Wrongdoing. "The number of *types* of things that can go wrong is limited."
- **The Four Stages of a Crisis** (Jordan-Meier): (1) Breaking news / "what happened?"; (2) Make-it-or-break-it reputation-forming (media focus on victims & response); (3) Finger-pointing (minimized by doing stage 2 well); (4) Fallout/resolution.
- **The 10 Truths**:
  1. **You're going to suffer — at least at first**, but well-handled crises can *gain* value (Oxford study). Contain it; don't let a specific problem "jump the fire line" into a referendum on your competence (Toyota vs. Saturn).
  2. **You'll be cast as a good guy or a bad guy** — the media want to know you "get it." David-vs-Goliath: the "little guy" gets sympathy; big institutions must do everything right (JetBlue's Passenger Bill of Rights = good guy).
  3. **You must communicate immediately** — often within the hour. "Be present in your own coverage." If you don't talk, others fill the void (Exxon Valdez: Rawl waited a week).
  4. **The media will side with the victims** — facts alone don't win; right facts + wrong tone = clueless/heartless (the $800-forceps "cost-benefit ratio" hospital quote).
  5. **The spokesperson you choose speaks volumes** — err high rather than low; don't always start with the CEO (keep an "up" to escalate to); use the head/heart principle.
  6. **Your receptionist needs media training** — frontline staff, security guards, even executives' spouses can become the reporter's confirming source.
  7. **Burying bad parts of the story makes it worse** — tear the Band-Aid off; don't let the "drip, drip, drip" of revelations spawn new stories (Susan G. Komen / Planned Parenthood).
  8. **Social media can make or break you** — the public may learn of *your* crisis before you do; correct misinformation within ~30 minutes ("Twitter is the new police scanner").
  9. **You need to apologize the right way** — skip the hedged "if/then" half-apology; lead with the full one, no excuses, pledge specific action (Penn State Board's tone-perfect statement).
  10. **Your lawyers can make the crisis worse** — legal advice (say nothing) can pit financial exposure against reputational ruin; find counsel who supports the PR function.

## Key Concepts
- **Jump the fire line**: When a limited, specific crisis becomes a broad indictment of your whole brand.
- **Head/heart principle** (Jordan-Meier): The spokesperson must deliver the right words in the right way — "totally believable when expressing concern."
- **Present in your own coverage**: Show up and speak so reporters use *you* as the primary source, not a disgruntled ex-employee or competitor.
- **When you're not guilty** (3 tactics): be "super" open; bring a lawyer (threaten libel if a false story is imminent); offer your own proof / parallel investigation (Taco Bell's aggressive ad rebuttal).

## Mental Models
- "Once you hear the thunder, it's too late to build the ark" — prepare and drill *before* the crisis.
- A crisis is an opportunity to *demonstrate* competence and compassion, not just survive.
- Ask in a legal-vs-PR conflict: What's the right thing to do? Have I weighed both perspectives? Can I marry the best legal and PR advice?

## Worked Example
**How to prepare (5 steps) + a real-time drill**: (1) Assemble a crisis team across departments; (2) brainstorm potential crises through the eight categories; (3) reduce to the five most damaging *and* most likely; (4) develop a written response per crisis (five 2-hour monthly meetings); (5) run real-time drills. The drill for a plant explosion runs the scenario in stages: start at "breaking news" (only know an explosion occurred) → pick a spokesperson, develop messages, do an on-camera mock interview, review the video → advance two hours to "reputation-forming" (2 dead, 9 injured, cause unknown) → advance to next day (employees claim management ignored equipment warnings). Practice each stage, revise the action plan, and reassemble the team yearly.

## Anti-patterns
- **Going silent / refusing to comment early** (Exxon Valdez) — reads as guilt; outsiders define the narrative.
- **Facts without humanity** — the "$800, there's a cost-benefit ratio" forceps quote.
- **The "if/then" half-apology** ("If you were offended, I'm sorry") — inflames, forcing a second apology days later.
- **Letting lawyers run communications** — wins the case in three years, loses the brand now.
- **Burying or drip-feeding bad facts** — keeps the crisis alive and destroys credibility.

## Key Takeaways
1. Most crises are predictable — plan and drill the five most likely-and-damaging in advance.
2. Communicate immediately and be present in your own coverage; silence reads as guilt.
3. The media side with victims — get the tone right, not just the facts.
4. Apologize fully the first time; no excuses, pledge concrete action.
5. Prep frontline staff; choose the spokesperson carefully; don't let legal eclipse reputation.
