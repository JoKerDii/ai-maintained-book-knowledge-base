# Glossary — The Media Training Bible

**Ambush interview** — A reporter showing up unannounced or blindsiding you with an off-topic question mid-interview; win by denying them a defensive "money shot" (Ch 4).

**ATMs** — Phillips' core system for tough questions: **A**nswer, **T**ransition, **M**essage, **s**ell. Spend 10–20% on A+T, 80–90% on M+s (Ch 4).

**Audience focused** — The "A" in CUBE A: a message must incorporate the audience's needs and values, not just your points (Ch 2).

**Bites interview** — The most common TV format; producers use only a few sound bites. Look at the interviewer, not the camera (Ch 5, Ch 6).

**Brief** — The "B" in CUBE A: one sentence per message; the average TV quote is 7.3 seconds / ~18 words (Ch 2).

**Comment without commenting** — Declining to answer by explaining *why* you can't, instead of saying "no comment" (Ch 1).

**Consistent** — The "C" in CUBE A: repeat the same themes across all platforms; 7–15 exposures move an audience to action (Ch 2).

**Crisis** — An event, precipitated by a specific incident, that draws critical media attention for a definite period (Ch 7).

**CUBE A** — The five elements of an effective message: Consistent, Unburdened, Brief, Ear-worthy, Audience focused (Ch 2).

**Deskside briefing** — A casual one-on-one briefing at a journalist's office; treat everything as quotable (Ch 6).

**Diaphragmatic breathing** — Breathing from the stomach (not chest) for a fuller, less nasal voice and better breath control (Ch 5).

**Ear-worthy** — The "E" in CUBE A: write for the ear, not the eye — short, everyday words, contractions; speak it aloud (Ch 2).

**Edited interview** — Any interview aired/printed in clips; you can restart answers, but avoid "as I mentioned earlier," "in conclusion," dates, and the reporter's name (Ch 6).

**Effective frequency** — The number of exposures before a message drives action; Phillips' rule is 7–15+ (Ch 2).

**File cards** — Index cards distilling each message + supports to trigger phrases, so you sound extemporaneous on air (Ch 8).

**Head/heart principle** — Jordan-Meier's test for crisis spokespersons: deliver the right words in the right (believably caring) way (Ch 7).

**IFB (interruptible feedback)** — The TV earpiece through which you hear the studio; test it before air (Ch 6).

**Jump the fire line** — When a specific, limited crisis becomes a referendum on your whole brand's competence (Toyota) (Ch 7).

**Live-to-tape** — Prerecorded but aired unedited; treat as live, but ask if you can restart on a mistake (Ch 6).

**Message** — A one-sentence statement marrying one of your key points to one of your audience's needs/values; aim for three (Ch 2).

**Message disconnect** — When your words and body language contradict; the audience believes the body (Ch 5).

**Message support** — A story, statistic, or sound bite that reinforces a message (the "three-legged stool"); build ≥2 of each per message (Ch 2).

**Money shot** — The dramatic, guilty-looking visual a reporter wants in an ambush; deny it by staying calm (Ch 4).

**"No comment"** — The most damaging phrase in a spokesperson's lexicon; reads as guilt. Never use it or its equivalents (Ch 1).

**Off the record** — Info that can't be used in any form — but the term is defined inconsistently by journalists; unreliable, so banish it (Ch 1).

**On background** — Info usable but the source can't be named or quoted (Ch 1).

**On the record** — The default state of all communication with reporters (Ch 1).

**Peter Jennings Rule** — When you don't know the answer, tell the public what you *do* know ("That's unclear, but here's what we know…") (Ch 4).

**Phoner** — A print or radio interview conducted by phone; stand, use a headset and landline, gesture (Ch 6).

**Present in your own coverage** — Showing up and speaking so reporters use you as the primary source in a crisis (Ch 7).

**Rule of Thirds** — A story has three voices (you, opponent, reporter); refusing to comment makes you go "0-for-3." Don't make your opponent's case (Ch 1).

**Seven-second stray** — A brief off-message moment that becomes the only quote that airs — repeatedly; your message is your "lifeboat" (Ch 4).

**Sound bite** — A short, memorable/witty phrase expressing a message; usually planned in advance, delivered as if improvised (Ch 2).

**Split screen / reaction shot** — TV formats where you appear while not speaking; act as if you're always "on" (Ch 6).

**Strong language** — Declarative words ("here's what we know") replacing hedges ("we think," "we hope") (Ch 3).

**Target person** — One specific individual you visualize and speak to, representing the whole audience (Ch 3).

**Ten Truths of a Crisis** — Phillips' crisis framework (Ch 7), e.g. communicate immediately, side with victims, apologize the right way.

**Twelve-Year-Old-Nephew Rule** — Strip jargon by explaining as you would to a bright 12-year-old (simplify, don't "dumb down") (Ch 3).

**Unburdened** — The "U" in CUBE A: free of wordiness, jargon, abstractions (≤2 commas, ≤30 words, concrete images) (Ch 2).

**Uptick** — Rising pitch at the end of statements, making them sound like questions and eroding credibility (Ch 5).

**Verbal filler** — "Uhhh," "ummm," "like," "you know"; kill with pre-answer pauses and the 30-second-object drill (Ch 5).

**Walk and talk** — An interview conducted while walking; visually interesting but distracting — slow down or stop to make a key point (Ch 6).

**Why + What** — Answer open-ended "what" questions by leading with *why your work matters*, then what you do (Ch 3).
