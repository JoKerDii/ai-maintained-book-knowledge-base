# Chapter 8: Instinct and Creativity: From the Sacred to the Sublime

## Core Idea
Art is ancient, universal, and expensive — yet wears no survival value on its sleeve, so it triggers the same **adaptation vs. byproduct (spandrel)** contest as religion. Greene's resolution: art may have been adaptive as a training ground for imagination and a binder of groups, but significance does not require adaptive utility, and art's real payload is a species of truth — felt, not propositional — that factual knowledge structurally cannot deliver.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Sexual selection as fitness indicator (Darwin → Miller, Dutton)**: Artistic display is a costly, hard-to-fake advertisement of the resources, health, and cognitive horsepower that let one squander time on non-survival activity.
  - When to use: explaining a trait that is expensive, conspicuous, and correlated with mate choice rather than survival (the peacock's tail; bowerbird dens; manakin dances).
  - How: (1) confirm the trait carries a survival cost; (2) identify the choosy sex and the scarce reproductive resource; (3) show the trait reliably signals underlying quality (**costly signaling**); (4) check the preference and the trait co-inherit.
  - Failure mode: is artistic talent actually a health signal? Why do females make art? Might art ride on raw intelligence, which natural selection already favors — making sexual selection redundant? Boyd's verdict: sexual selection was an extra gear for art, not the engine.
- **"Cheesecake for the mind" (Pinker)**: All arts but the language arts are adaptively useless confections that press pleasure buttons evolved for other purposes; music is an auditory parasite free-riding on sensitivity to information-rich sound (harmonic series signal a single identifiable source; thunder, footfalls, cracking branches reward attentiveness).
  - When to use: as the default null hypothesis whenever a pleasurable universal is proposed as an adaptation.
  - How: identify the ancestral sensitivity, show the modern stimulus is a supernormal concentration of it, and show the feedback loop to fitness has been severed.
  - Why it matters: it is a claim about *one* narrow question — differential gene transmission — not a value judgment. Failure mode: readers hear a demotion and argue back with aesthetic devotion, which is not evidence either way.
- **Art as cognitive playground + social binder (Dissanayake, Carroll, Boyd)**: In ancestral context art was not extracurricular; ritualized collective drumming, singing, and movement produced shared emotional states that forged communal solidarity, while artistic thinking exercised analogy, metaphor, and counterfactual imagination.
  - When to use: when an activity looks useless in isolation but was historically inseparable from group life.
  - How: reconstruct the practice as embedded (who participated, how often, alongside what), then ask what capacity it trained and what bond it produced. Innovation is the foot soldier; group cohesion is the army of implementation — art sits at the nexus.
- **Symbolic immortality (Rank, after Becker)**: Accept literal mortality and displace the urge for eternity onto self-authored works. Coping with death through creating is a pathway to sanity, not pathology — recasting the tortured-artist cliché. Its cost: eternity shrinks to the duration of civilization; its compensation: unlike literal immortality, the symbolic version is real.

## Key Concepts
- **Sexual selection**: Selection driven by reproductive access rather than survival; can favor traits that are net costly to staying alive.
- **Fitness indicator**: A display whose expense certifies the underlying quality it advertises.
- **Costly signaling**: The general logic that only signals too expensive to fake are credible.
- **Adaptation vs. byproduct (spandrel)**: Selected-for function versus free rider on other selected traits.
- **Supernormal stimulus / "cheesecake for the mind"**: A concocted brew of megadoses of agreeable stimuli exceeding anything in the natural world.
- **Artistic truth**: Non-propositional, unmeasurable truth that shifts one's sense of the world — Harburg's "feel a thought."
- **The sublime**: Experience so enveloping it feels like stepping beyond time (Greene's exemplar: the Bach *Chaconne*).
- **Proximate vs. ultimate explanation**: "We do it because it feels wonderful" answers the immediate impetus only, like "we eat ice cream because we like sweet things."
- **Poetic immortality**: Persistence of one's imprint — literally in light racing across the cosmos, figuratively in works that outlast their maker.

## Mental Models
- **Use the peacock's-tail test when** a behavior looks maladaptive: ask whether it is being paid for out of the reproductive budget rather than the survival budget.
- **Think of the arts as a flight simulator for the whole mind**: story simulated social scenarios; music, image, and movement train flexibility of thought and fluency of intuition — the same faculties that produced the spear, cooking, and the wheel.
- **Use "adaptive value ≠ significance" when** an evolutionary verdict feels like a demotion: biological adaptation is not the sole standard of worth, and lifting ourselves above survival concerns is itself remarkable.
- **Think of art as the mirror for the soul and science as the mirror for the face** (Shaw): objective inquiry requires looking past each observer's peculiarity; artistic truth requires looking straight at it. An examined life need not be an articulated life.

## Anti-patterns
- **Just-so storytelling about the Pleistocene**: courtship rituals and public artistic practice 100,000 years ago are largely conjecture; Mick Jagger's conquests tell us nothing about early hominin mating.
- **Adaptationist overreach**: wanting art to be adaptive because that would dignify it — a preference for a conclusion, not an argument for it.
- **Stopping at the proximate cause**: "art moves us, and who wouldn't want that" explains the appetite, not its origin.
- **Explaining away vs. explaining**: classifying music as cheesecake says something about gene transmission and nothing about music's worth; treating the former as a verdict on the latter is a category error.
- **Demanding a single explanation**: for behavior this complex, amalgams of mechanisms (story, ritual, religion, reciprocity, art) are likelier than one winner.
- **Requiring language to certify experience**: insisting every meaningful encounter be articulated discards precisely the truths art delivers.

## Worked Example
**Music, run through all three hypotheses.** Take Beethoven's Ninth — premiered in 1824 by a composer almost entirely deaf, conducting several measures behind the orchestra, turned around by the contralto to see handkerchiefs waving.
*Sexual selection*: the performer who best sings or dances signals a body and mind firing on all cylinders and the surplus resources to spend on non-survival display; choosy mates select for it, and offspring inherit both the talent and the taste. Objection: the signal's honesty is unproven, and it may be inseparable from general intelligence.
*Byproduct*: music hijacks aural machinery built for real work. Harmonically related frequencies indicate a single physical source, so ancestors who attended pleasurably to organized sound gained environmental awareness. Music concentrates that stimulus into a joyride with the fitness loop severed — cheesecake.
*Adaptive-by-cohesion-and-cognition*: prolonged group drumming, singing, and movement generate a shared, larger-than-life emotional state that melds participants into a committed whole and builds the mutual emotional predictability cooperation requires; meanwhile composing and listening exercise analogy, pattern, and imagination.
Greene leans toward the third while conceding the evidence is thin — and then makes the move that matters: whichever wins, it does not touch what Helen Keller received with her hands on a radio speaker in 1924, sensing the "immortal symphony" and recognizing that its deaf composer wrought joy for others out of his pain. That is artistic truth, and no adaptive verdict adjudicates it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Art is old, universal, and costly — three facts that force an evolutionary question but do not by themselves answer it.
2. Three rival accounts: sexual selection (an extra gear, not the engine), byproduct/cheesecake, and adaptive cohesion-plus-cognitive-training.
3. Significance does not require adaptive utility; refusing to conflate the two is the chapter's central discipline.
4. Artistic truth is a distinct layer from objective truth — non-propositional, unmeasurable, and inaccessible by direct and conscious methods (Proust), yet it genuinely expands what is knowable (Hirshfield, Bellow, Conrad).
5. Mortality is art's engine as well as religion's: the examined life examines death, and symbolic immortality is the only eternity likely on offer.
6. Objective inquiry demands looking past individual perspective; artistic truth demands inhabiting it — which is why science and art are complementary rather than competing accounts of reality.
