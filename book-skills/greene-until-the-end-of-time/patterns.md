# Patterns — Reasoning Moves from *Until the End of Time*

Greene offers explanatory methods, not procedures. Each pattern below is a reasoning move: a way of setting up a question so the physics, biology, or philosophy can answer it.

## Entropic Two-Step Reasoning
**When to use**: Any time order appears where the second law seems to forbid it — a star forming, a cell metabolizing, a brain thinking, a civilization building.
**How**: (1) Identify the local entropy *decrease* (the structure that got built). (2) Find the energy flow that paid for it. (3) Locate the waste sink — where the more-than-compensating entropy was dumped (heat, radiation, excreta, expanding space). (4) Confirm the total went up. If you cannot name the sink, your accounting is wrong, not the second law.
**Trade-offs**: Powerful and universal, but it explains *permission*, not *cause* — knowing a structure is entropically affordable does not tell you why this structure rather than another. Pair it with a selection argument. Also: it only works if a sink actually exists; in the far future (Ch 9) or inside a black hole (Ch 10) the sink is the binding constraint.

## Choosing a Level of Description (Nested Stories)
**When to use**: When someone claims one level of explanation is the *real* one — "it's all just particles," or conversely "you can't reduce this."
**How**: (1) Identify which layer the question actually lives on (particle, chemical, biological, neural, mental, cultural). (2) Answer in that layer's own vocabulary and grammar. (3) Check the answer is *consistent* with the layers beneath, not *replaceable* by them. (4) Keep the linkages explicit — Cervantes's neurons and Don Quixote's yearning are connected but not interchangeable.
**Trade-offs**: This is reductionism *without* eliminativism, and it costs you a unified theory of everything-that-matters. The discipline is refusing both moves: no dismissing a higher story as "just" lower-level activity, and no exempting a higher story from physical constraint.

## Evaluating an Adaptationist vs. Byproduct Claim
**When to use**: Any assertion that a human behavior — storytelling, art, music, religion — "evolved because it does X."
**How**: (1) State the proposed adaptive benefit precisely. (2) Ask the byproduct question first: could this be a package-deal consequence of something else already selected for (theory of mind, agency detection, pattern sensitivity, pleasure circuits)? (3) Apply the "just so" story test — does the account make any prediction that could fail? (4) Separate **proximate** from **ultimate** explanation: "we do it because it feels wonderful" answers only the immediate impetus. (5) Accept that the debate may stay open, and check whether your downstream conclusion actually depends on which side wins.
**Trade-offs**: Byproduct explanations are cheap to generate and hard to falsify, so the test cuts both ways. Greene's own practice in Ch 11 is instructive: he leaves the origin question unresolved and rests his conclusion on the observed *function* — a longing to be part of something larger — which holds under either account.

## Reasoning About Far-Future Timescales
**When to use**: Any claim about the fate of stars, life, mind, or civilization beyond a few million years.
**How**: (1) Convert everything to the Empire State timeline — floor *n* = 10^n years. (2) Predict in **broad strokes, not fine detail**: commit to structural claims (stars burn out, protons may decay, black holes evaporate), refuse detail-level extrapolation of technology or civilization. (3) Replace "likely vs. unlikely" with **waiting time vs. horizon** — over unbounded time, probability × duration is what matters, so no nonzero-probability process may be discarded. (4) Ask the thinker question for any proposed future mind: can it still expel the waste heat that new thought requires?
**Trade-offs**: The method's honesty is its limitation — it yields a skeleton, not a scenario. It also resurrects self-undermining conclusions (Boltzmann brains); treat self-undermining as evidence the model is incomplete rather than as a result to accept.

## Testing What Anchors Value (The Doomsday Move)
**When to use**: When you need to know whether a commitment rests on personal continuation or on humanity's continuation.
**How**: (1) Imagine a terminal prognosis for yourself; note the reaction (typically: heightened intensity, significance conferred on ordinary moments). (2) Now use Scheffler's variant — thirty days after your natural death, everyone remaining is obliterated — which excises your own premature mortality. (3) Re-ask activity by activity: would I still do the research, finish the book, send the kids to school? (4) Whatever survives step 3 is intrinsically motivated; whatever collapses was resting on descendants.
**Trade-offs**: Responses genuinely differ (Frankfurt thinks art and science survive; Greene thinks they don't), so the exercise reveals your own structure of value rather than a universal fact. Use imminence only as **artificial urgency to catalyze an authentic response** — the intuition then transfers to the far future, since long and short have no absolute meaning.

## Calibrating Belief Instead of Declaring It
**When to use**: Contested claims where "do you believe X?" invites tribal rather than evidential answers.
**How**: Replace the yes/no with "what is my calibrated confidence, given the evidence, and what would update it?" Then check the **register**: a claim read literally and contradicting established science is simply false; the same claim read as poetry, symbol, archive, or community practice remains inside rational discourse — a belief in the *value* of the content rather than its veridicality.
**Trade-offs**: The register move can be used dishonestly, to retreat from a falsified literal claim while keeping its rhetorical force. Name which register you are in before arguing.

## Diagnosing Low Entropy as Evidence
**When to use**: On encountering any strikingly ordered configuration — biological, cosmological, or social.
**How**: Treat low entropy as evidence of an organizing influence, not of luck; then find the influence (gravity, selection, an engineer, an energy gradient). Where entropy appears simply to vanish, look for the ledger entry (Bekenstein's answer to Wheeler: it went into horizon area).
**Trade-offs**: A statistical fluke *is* possible and, given enough time or enough regions, eventually mandatory — see the past hypothesis and the multiverse. The pattern tells you where to look first, not what you will find.
