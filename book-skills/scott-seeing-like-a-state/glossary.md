# Glossary — Seeing Like a State

**Abstract citizen** — the standardized, interchangeable planning subject with no gender, tastes, history, values, or traditions; not an oversight but "the necessary first premise of any large-scale planning exercise" (Ch 10).

**Appropriation and control** — Scott's claim that legibility schemes typically optimize extraction and supervision rather than production (Ch 6, Ch 7, Ch 8).

**Authoritarian high modernism** — high-modernist ideology joined to a state willing and able to use coercive power against a society that cannot resist; the lethal configuration (Ch 3).

**Bricolage** — Lévi-Strauss's term for mētis's mode of innovation: recombining existing elements rather than inventing from first principles (Ch 9).

**Brasília** — Costa and Niemeyer's planned capital; Scott's central case of a city that works only because of the unplanned settlement surrounding it (Ch 4).

**Cadastral survey** — the parcel-by-parcel land map that assigns each holding to a single taxable owner; it does not describe a tenure system, it creates one (Ch 1, Ch 2).

**Canned institution** — a social form that is symbolically self-sufficient and permits little or no modification by its participants; opposite of mētis-friendly (Ch 10).

**Cultivated natural capital** — fish farms and tree plantations replacing wild stocks and old-growth; gains appropriability and short-run yield, loses "redundancy, resiliency, and stability" (Ch 10).

**Epistēmē** — universal, deductive, verifiable knowledge; with technē, the pole against which mētis is defined (Ch 9).

**Facts on paper vs. facts on the ground** — the gap between the state's record and lived reality; when the record is enforced, the ground is remade to match (Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 6).

**Four-element recipe for disaster** — (1) administrative ordering of nature and society, (2) high-modernist ideology, (3) an authoritarian state willing to use coercive power, (4) a prostrate civil society unable to resist. All four are necessary (Intro, Ch 3).

**Freehold tenure** — individual, alienable, registered land ownership; a legibility technology that erases overlapping and common-use rights (Ch 1, Ch 2).

**Grève du zèle** — French term for the work-to-rule strike (Ch 9).

**High modernism** — muscle-bound faith in scientific and technical progress, mastery of nature, and rational design of social order. A *faith* that borrows science's legitimacy — "must not be confused with scientific practice" (Intro, Ch 3).

**Institutional neurosis** — the apathy, withdrawal, and loss of initiative that single-purpose, controlled environments produce in their inmates (Ch 10).

**Kolkhoz / sovkhoz** — Soviet collective and state farms; machines for grain requisition and control more than for production (Ch 6).

**Landrace** — locally adapted, open-pollinated crop varieties; the genetic reservoir on which modern hybrids silently depend (Ch 8, Ch 10).

**Legibility** — the state's rendering of complex local practice into a standard grid that can be centrally recorded, monitored, and manipulated. Scott's master concept (Intro, Ch 1, Ch 2).

**Mētis** — practical, local, experiential intelligence acquired by doing; Odysseus's quality. Most valuable where situations are mutable, indeterminate, and particular (Ch 9).

**Mētis-friendly institution** — multifunctional, plastic, diverse, adaptable forms that are completed by their participants and that renew their skill. Models: language, common law, democracy (Ch 10).

**Miniaturization** — the high modernist's retreat, after plans miscarry, into a controllable micro-order — model city, model village, model farm — then cited as proof the design scales (Intro, Ch 7).

**Monocropping** — single-species, even-age planting; the signature of thin simplification in both forestry and agriculture (Ch 1, Ch 8).

**Normalbaum** — the "standard tree" of German scientific forestry, an abstraction of usable timber volume that eventually reshaped the actual forest (Ch 1).

**Polyculture / polycropping** — mixed planting; less productive short-run, "demonstrably more stable, more self-sufficient, and less vulnerable to epidemics and environmental stress" (Ch 8, Ch 10).

**Prostrate civil society** — a population lacking the capacity to resist state plans; the fourth element, and "the leveled social terrain on which to build" (Intro, Ch 3).

**Permanent surnames** — fixed heritable names imposed for taxation, conscription, and property tracking; a canonical legibility case (Ch 2).

**Standardized weights and measures** — abstract universal units replacing relational local measures that encoded soil quality, labor, and yield in the unit itself (Ch 1, Ch 2).

**State simplification** — an abridged map representing only the slice of society that interests the official observer; the basic given of modern statecraft (Intro, Ch 1).

**Synoptic view** — the single at-a-glance perspective a common metric makes possible; what the "partially blind" premodern state lacked (Intro, Ch 1).

**Taylorism** — scientific management by decomposition of work into specified repetitive movements; "not just a strategy of production, but also a strategy of control and appropriation" (Ch 6, Ch 9).

**Technē** — codified, universal, hard-and-fast technical knowledge, deducible from first principles; settled knowledge, but incapable of explaining its own discovery (Ch 9).

**Thin simplification** — a schematic model too impoverished to serve as instructions for a functioning order; the thinner the formal scheme, the less resilient it is (Ch 9, Ch 10).

**Two maps test** — Map 1 traces the routes a design provides; Map 2 traces all unplanned activity. Map 2 usually supersedes Map 1 and does load-bearing work (Ch 10).

**Ujamaa villagization** — Nyerere's compulsory Tanzanian resettlement into gridded standard villages; well-intentioned, and Scott's clearest case of aesthetics and miniaturization overriding local agronomic knowledge (Ch 7).

**Vanguard party** — Lenin's conception of the revolutionary party as engineer and general staff of revolution; countered by Luxemburg and Kollontai (Ch 5).

**Visual/aesthetic order vs. functional order** — the high-modernist habit of reading rational order "in remarkably visual aesthetic terms," so that what *looks* regimented is presumed to work (Intro, Ch 3, Ch 4, Ch 7).

**Waldsterben** — "forest death"; the German term for the second-rotation collapse of scientifically managed monocrop forests (Ch 1).

**Work-to-rule** — following every rule and job description exactly until work halts; Scott's proof that formal rules depend on uncodified informal practice (Intro, Ch 9).
