# Chapter 2: Cities, People, and Language
*Part 1 — State Projects of Legibility and Simplification*

## Core Idea
The same simplifying optic applied to forests and land is applied to cities, personal names, official language, and traffic: each is retrofitted so a central authority can find, count, tax, conscript, and police from outside. Legibility is a two-edged tool — it enables vaccination, poverty relief, and rule of law with exactly the same capacity that enabled the 1941 Amsterdam map of Jewish residents.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The project of legibility (five characteristics of state facts)**: State simplifications are *interested*, *documentary*, *static*, *aggregate*, and *standardized*.
  - When to use: to audit any administrative dataset or dashboard before trusting it as a picture of reality.
  - How: for each fact ask — whose interest defined it? does it exist only as a written record? does it freeze a moving process? is it an average masking variation? what distinctions were collapsed to make it comparable? Note the incentive Porter identifies: institutions prefer *precise and standardizable* measures over *accurate* ones, since accuracy that cannot be reproduced elsewhere is useless to them.
  - Why it works: only aggregable facts permit comparison, monitoring, and discriminating intervention. Failure mode: thin categories operate as if all similarly classified cases were homogeneous — all autoworkers alike, all Catholics alike, all Normalbäume alike.
- **Grid vs. warren (legibility of space)**: Compare an unplanned settlement (Bruges) with a planned grid (Chicago) on outsider navigability.
  - When to use: assessing any space, org chart, or codebase for who holds navigational advantage.
  - How: apply the native-tracker test — would an outsider need a local guide? If yes, the community retains a margin of political autonomy. Then note the three consequences of the grid: (1) its order is visible only from above, never at street level; (2) formal order guarantees nothing about lived experience; (3) abstract uniform lots become a currency-like commodity, ideal for surveyors, planners, and speculators alike.
- **Permanent surnames as identity infrastructure**: Assign fixed, heritable names so individuals can be tied unambiguously to kin, property, and obligation.
  - How: compile a catalog of names; distribute by jurisdiction; refuse to accept any document, petition, or deed not using the official name; enforce through schools, clergy, and officials until the official name displaces the local one.

## Key Concepts
- **legibility**: The capacity of a central viewer to read a society without local mediation; illegibility is correspondingly a reliable resource for political autonomy.
- **Haussmannization**: The 1853–69 retrofit of Paris — demolition, boulevards, sewers, rail — for military security, public health, commerce, and imperial display.
- **permanent surnames**: Fixed heritable patronyms, in almost every case a state project, prerequisite to tax rolls, conscription lists, censuses, and enforceable deeds.
- **vernacular vs. official naming**: The rich, context-dependent local name set ("it depends") versus the single fixed name inscribed in the register.
- **cadastral survey**: The parcel-by-parcel property map linking every plot to a taxpaying legal person.
- **standardized weights and measures**: Uniform units without which markets, price comparison, and equitable assessment are impossible.
- **customary/local measures**: Situational, interested, historically specific units, perfectly clear inside the community and opaque outside it.
- **facts on paper vs. facts on the ground**: Registers, deeds, and identity documents versus actual practice — but in law, paper wins, and an error in a document can outlast an unreported truth.
- **synoptic view**: The mirrored-sunglasses vantage — a quasi-monopoly on the picture of the whole, shared by monasteries, barracks, factories, and bureaucracies alike.
- **state simplification**: Not simple-mindedness but two specific moves — casting facts in replicable class terms, and collapsing distinctions that would otherwise be relevant.
- **thin simplification**: A category like "gainfully employed" that is a yes/no fiction over exceptionally varied working lives.

## Mental Models
- Use the native-tracker test whenever you want to know where informal power sits: if outsiders need a guide, insiders hold leverage.
- Think of an official language as a toll gate — once every petition, deed, and court case must "pass through" it, competence in it becomes the currency of advancement and everyone else is rendered mute and marginal.
- Use the Legrand Star as a warning about topology: hardwiring all routes through the hub makes A-to-hub trivial and A-to-B expensive; you have optimized for the center's convenience and severed lateral ties.
- Think of the state's categories as an authoritative tune most of the population must dance to: what began as a surveyor's or clerk's invention ends by organizing daily experience, because it is embedded in the institutions that structure it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Judging a plan from the helicopter**: Scale models and aerial views are assessed for sculptural order from a perspective almost no resident will ever occupy; formal order is not social order.
- **Facade compliance**: Haussmann's zoning regulated visible surfaces, so builders raised elegant frontages over crowded, airless tenements. Regulate what you can see and you get what you can see.
- **Displacement as side effect**: Every ambitious order has an evil twin — Haussmann's spacious center presupposed pushing the poor to the periphery, producing Belleville, a new illegible insurrectionary quarter, and eventually the Commune.
- **Blinding your own hindsight**: Claveria's Philippine registers failed to record prior names, so officials could no longer trace property or tax history back across the reform. Migration without a mapping table destroys the past.
- **Reading Scott as anti-planning**: He is not. These typifications are *indispensable* to statecraft; legibility is what let the CDC discover new epidemics early and lets a state vaccinate, relieve poverty, and tax fairly rather than billet troops on villages.

## Worked Example
Until roughly the fourteenth century most Europeans had no permanent surname. A man was John; if more was needed, a second designation followed from trade, place, father, or trait — and it died with him, so a grandson's name bore no trace of his grandfather. This was legible locally and opaque fiscally. Florence's 1427 *catasto* tried to fix subjects, wealth, and ages in one register; it revealed that state initiative *created* surnames rather than recording them, and that surname density tracked state reach — a third of Florentine households declared a second name, a fifth in secondary towns, a tenth in the countryside, with the poorest districts holding out until the seventeenth century. The census collapsed under cost and resistance. In England, a tithe collector facing a male population where six Christian names covered ninety percent had no choice: if the subject offered no second name, the clerk invented one. Most such names had no social existence outside the parchment. What made them real was interlocking incentive: once Edward I tied inheritance and copyhold to state-validated claims, taking your father's name became part of claiming his property, and the taxed subject himself now wanted to be identified accurately — to avoid paying twice. Naming, like mapping, meant taxes, so it drew revolt; the 1381 rising followed a decade of poll-tax registrations. The colonial version compresses generations into a decade: Claveria's 1849 Philippine decree distributed pages of an alphabetized *catalogo* to each town, so that whole provinces still wear the alphabet like a garland across the map, and no document, petition, or deed using any other name was valid. Compliance was partial — Magsaysay and Macapagal survive — but within a century Claveria's invented names were simply the names Filipinos had.

## Key Takeaways
1. Use standardization when the alternative is crude, blunt intervention: an illegible society blocks plunder *and* public welfare equally, and forces the state to overshoot or undershoot.
2. Assume any identity or naming scheme you deploy will outlive its stated purpose and be reused for purposes you did not choose — build it as though the next operator is not you.
3. When you migrate to a new identifier, retain the mapping to the old one; otherwise you destroy your own institutional memory.
4. Expect a legible system to create positional advantage at the apex and among the intermediaries fluent in the new code — lawyers, clerks, notaries, schoolteachers. Design for that redistribution, don't be surprised by it.
5. Regulate outcomes, not surfaces; facade-level rules produce facade-level compliance.
6. Treat resistance to a standardization project as information about whose interests the old system served, not merely as friction to be overcome.
7. Never assume local practice conforms to your model — there is always a shadow system beside and beneath the official record.

## Connects To
- **Ch 1**: Same technique, different substrate — the Normalbaum and the cadastral parcel become the grid block and the permanent surname.
- **Ch 3 (high modernism)**: Haussmann is the hinge; his retrofit foreshadows the paradoxes of authoritarian **high modernism** examined later.
- **Org design**: Standard titles, levels, and job families are surnames for employees — they enable fair comparison and pay equity, and they flatten exactly the idiosyncratic work that made someone valuable.
- **Software architecture**: A monorepo with uniform conventions is Chicago; an accreted legacy system is Bruges. The grid costs charm and buys onboarding speed; hub-and-spoke topology repeats the Legrand Star's lateral-traffic penalty.
- **Platform policy**: Real-name and identity-verification requirements are surname campaigns — they deliver accountability and abuse reduction, and simultaneously strip the anonymity that protects dissidents. The tool does not choose the purpose.
