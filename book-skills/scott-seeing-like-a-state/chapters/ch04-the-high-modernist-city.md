# Chapter 4: The High-Modernist City: An Experiment and a Critique
*Part 2 — Transforming Visions*

## Core Idea
Le Corbusier's urbanism is **authoritarian high modernism** made buildable: functional segregation, geometric order, and rule by the Plan, justified as science but resting on an aesthetic preference. Brasília is that city nearly built — and Jane Jacobs' street-level critique shows why it produced formal order and social death at once.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Visual/aesthetic order vs. functional order** (Jacobs): no necessary correspondence exists between tidy geometric regularity and a system that meets daily needs. Most complex systems show no surface regularity; their order lies deeper.
  - When to use: whenever a design is defended by how clean it looks in a diagram, org chart, or dashboard.
  - How: name the functions the thing must serve, in the plural; ask what function each apparent irregularity performs before calling it waste; judge by those functions, not by symmetry; test from inside, not above. Jacobs' image: an army on the parade ground is visually ordered and doing nothing.
  - Why it works / failure mode: planners inferred functional order *from* visual order, which drove them to single-use zoning, since mixed use looks like confusion. Fails if used as reflexive defense of every mess — Jacobs insists slums must change, but by unslumming themselves rather than by clearance.

- **Functional segregation as planner's simplification**: separate each zone to one purpose — work, residence, commerce, traffic — so each can be optimized against one variable. Le Corbusier's second principle is "the death of the street."
  - When to use: on any "let's split this cleanly by concern" proposal.
  - How: ask what the separation is *for*. Scott's answer is honest — it is far easier to plan a zone with one purpose, as it is easier to plan a forest yielding only timber. The clarity is real but bought by bracketing every other purpose. Roads survive this; homes do not — cooking for friends is not "food preparation."

- **"The Plan: Dictator"**: Le Corbusier's first principle. The despot is not a man but the exact plan, drawn up away from the mayor's office and the cries of the electorate, ignoring existing usages and whether the constitution permits it.
  - When to use: to recognize technocracy's core claim — a unique expert solution exists, so deciding by politics would produce the *wrong* answer and no compromise is possible.

## Key Concepts
- **The death of the street**: the doctrine eliminating the street as a place of public life, leaving only roads for motorized traffic.
- **Superquadra**: Brasília's residential block of ~1,500–2,500 residents with its own nursery and school, meant to produce collective life and largely producing isolation.
- **Brasilite**: "Brasília-itis," residents' mock clinical term for a daily life stripped of distractions, conversations, flirtations, and small rituals.
- **The public visiting room**: Holston's term for the Brazilian praça, a framed space accessible to all classes and flexible enough that users bend it to *their* purposes.
- **Utopian miniaturization**: building a small, fully controlled exemplary model of the future in the hope it transforms the whole.
- **Eyes on the street**: Jacobs' safety mechanism — an almost unconscious network of voluntary controls kept by nonspecialists whose main business is something else.
- **Macro-order, micro-confusion**: Holston's irony that practical knowledge of Brasília *decreases* as systematic rationality rises; **legible** from the center, unnavigable on foot.
- **Facts on paper vs. facts on the ground**: the Brasília of the planning documents against the real one of resistance, subversion, and political calculation.

## Mental Models
- **Think of a city as a language, not a building.** Planned languages exist and are precise within their designed purposes, but language is a general tool bent to countless ends by its history. A city planned from zero is necessarily thin; only time and millions of residents thicken it.
- **Use the kitchen test.** The kitchen is the busiest room because it is the most versatile. Versatility, not specialization, draws people.
- **Think of diversity as portfolio resilience.** A mixed neighborhood hedges across many commercial bets; single-use districts, like monocrops, may catch a boom and are especially susceptible to stress.
- **Use "who could anticipate?" as the humility check.** Of one Louisville building's dozen successive uses Jacobs asks who could have provided for them: "Only an unimaginative man would think he could; only an arrogant man would want to."

## Anti-patterns
- **Designing from the air**: Le Corbusier's cities are drawn as seen from miles out to sea. Disorder visible only from altitude may not be disorder at ground level.
- **Stipulating the user's needs**: he posited an abstract subject needing so many square meters of air and light, never admitting the subjects might have something valuable to say or needs that were plural.
- **Building for a clientele you intend to train**: CIAM proposed teaching schoolchildren scientific housing so that in time they would "freely" choose what he had planned.
- **Confusing great building projects with great social achievements**: it is beyond anyone's imagination to create a community. Fix the buildings, leave the people.
- **Clearing "unslumming" slums**: clearance destroys the neighborhood networks Jacobs calls a city's irreplaceable social capital, which return only slowly and by chance.

## Worked Example
**Brasília, end to end.** Kubitschek promises "fifty years of progress in five." Niemeyer runs a competition won by Lucio Costa on rough sketches: a monumental axis, an arc of terraced embankments crossed by a straight avenue, a bounding triangle. The site is empty plateau a thousand kilometers from Rio, all state-owned — no property owners to negotiate with, no "orthopedic" compromises, a clean tablecloth. Housing, work, recreation, traffic, and administration are each spatially segregated as CIAM prescribed, and because the city's only business is administration, the planning problem is already simplified. Real advances follow: 25 square meters of green space per resident, uniform housing differing only in units allotted, a nursery and school in every **superquadra**.

Then the subtractions. The corridor street and the praça — the two venues of Brazilian public life since colonial days, where children played and adults strolled and were seen — are designed out. There *is* a square: the Plaza of the Three Powers, so vast it dwarfs a military parade and makes Tiananmen look cozy. Meet a friend there and it is like meeting in the Gobi, and there would be nothing to do. All public space in Brasília is officially designated public space; the sidewalk café, the street corner, the neighborhood square do not exist. Residents arrive amazed to find "a city without crowds"; they say it "lacks street corners." Traffic jams were eliminated, and so were the pedestrian jams one informant called the point of social conviviality. They name the condition **brasilite**. The superquadra facades are identical, without even balconies to individualize; Holston asks nine-year-olds who live in them to draw "home" and not one draws an apartment. Sectors are coded in unmasterable acronyms, and practical knowledge of the city falls as its systematic rationality rises.

What made Brasília livable was unplanned. Sixty thousand construction workers, the *candangos*, were expected to build the city and quietly leave. Kubitschek had rhetorically elevated them into proletarian heroes; they took that rhetorical space and used it — squatting, organizing, demanding services and secure title — and in a competitive political system where no politician could ignore a voting bloc, they were heard. By 1980, 75 percent of Brasília's population lived in settlements never anticipated, while the planned city had not reached half its projected population. The two Brasílias, rich and poor, were not a footnote but symbiotic: that much order at the center required an unplanned periphery to sustain it. Le Corbusier's own built capital, Chandigarh, repeats the pattern — rectilinear axes replacing curves, empty **chowks** replacing crowded bazaars, hawkers banned, another unplanned city at the margins.

Against all this Jacobs begins where Le Corbusier never stood — on the sidewalk. Watching from a second-floor window as a man cajoles a young girl, she sees the butcher's wife, the deli owner, two bar patrons, a fruit vendor, and a laundryman appear on the pavement before any police officer is needed. That is functional order with no visual order whatsoever, and no plan could have produced it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Beauty in the diagram is not evidence of fitness in use; demand the functional argument separately from the aesthetic one.
2. Single-purpose zoning is chosen because it makes planning tractable, not because it makes the result better — be honest about which motivates your decomposition.
3. Cross-use carries invisible load. Safety, commerce, and trust were byproducts of mixing; separate the functions and you delete the byproducts without noticing.
4. Whatever you make **legible** from the center you may make unnavigable from within.
5. Plan for the unplanned, and expect the margins to do the work the plan refused — Brasília became livable through settlements it never authorized, in a system open enough for squatters to be heard.
