# Chapter 10: Conclusion

*Part 4 — The Missing Link*

## Core Idea
Condensed into one sentence: "the progenitors of such plans regarded themselves as far smarter and farseeing than they really were and, at the same time, regarded their subjects as far more stupid and incompetent than *they* really were." The constructive answer is not less planning but **mētis-friendly institutions** — multifunctional, plastic, diverse, adaptable — and interventions designed for a contingent future.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The four rules of thumb for less disaster-prone planning** — Scott's explicit design heuristics.
  1. **Take small steps.** "In an experimental approach to social change, presume that we cannot know the consequences of our interventions in advance. Given this postulate of ignorance, prefer wherever possible to take a small step, stand back, observe, and then plan the next small move." Haldane's image: drop a mouse down a mineshaft and it walks away; "a rat is killed, a man broken, a horse splashes."
  2. **Favor reversibility.** "Prefer interventions that can easily be undone if they turn out to be mistakes." Irreversible interventions have irreversible consequences. Aldo Leopold: "The first rule of intelligent tinkering is to keep all the parts."
  3. **Plan on surprises.** Choose plans that allow the largest accommodation to the unforeseen — land that can grow several crops, housing with flexible floor plans, a factory layout that admits new processes and product lines.
  4. **Plan on human inventiveness.** "Always plan under the assumption that those who become involved in the project later will have or will develop the experience and insight to improve on the design."
  - When to use: any intervention into a system whose second- and third-order effects you cannot enumerate.
  - Why it works / failure mode: not that planners' models were wrong, but that scope defeated foresight — "the magnitude of their initial intervention was so great that many of their missteps could not be righted."

- **The two maps test** — for any site, imagine two overlays. Map 1: the routes the planners provided (workplace to residence, goods delivery, shopping access). Map 2: a time-lapse tracing of all *unplanned* movement — pushing a baby carriage, window shopping, strolling, hopscotch, shortcuts, watching the passing scene. Map 2 is far more complex, and "the older the neighborhood, the more likely that the second map will have nearly superseded the first."
  - When to use: assessing whether a designed system is actually viable.
  - How: name the activities your design does *not* represent, then ask which of them the design depends on. "Planned Brasília is, in a thousand ways, underwritten by unplanned Brasília."

- **The mētis-friendly institution litmus test** — "To any planned, built, or legislated form of social life, one may apply a comparable test: to what degree does it promise to enhance the skills, knowledge, and responsibility of those who are a part of it?" Institutionally: how deeply is that form marked by the values and experience of those who compose it? The purpose is to distinguish "canned" situations that permit little or no modification from situations open to the development and application of **mētis**.

- **The resolute singularity of the high-modernist optic** — "its simplifying fiction is that, for any activity or process that comes under its scrutiny, there is only one thing going on." In the scientific forest only commercial wood; in the planned city only efficient movement; in the housing estate only shelter, heat, sewage, water. Even a road from A to B is also a site for leisure, social intercourse, and enjoying the view between A and B.

## Key Concepts
- **The postulate of ignorance** — begin from incomplete knowledge; Scott's example is the Yale nutrition circular that recommended a maximally varied diet *on the basis of what its authors did not know*.
- **The abstract citizen** — the standardized, interchangeable planning subject with "no gender, no tastes, no history, no values, no opinions or original ideas, no traditions." Not an oversight but "the necessary first premise of any large-scale planning exercise."
- **Institutional neurosis** — the apathy, withdrawal, and loss of initiative that single-purpose, controlled environments produce in their inmates.
- **The self-fulfilling logic of social engineering** — narrow environments "foster a less skilled, less innovative, less resourceful population," which then genuinely does need close supervision; the plan produces the subjects it assumed.
- **Formal order as subsystem** — "all socially engineered systems of formal order are in fact subsystems of a larger system on which they are ultimately dependent, not to say parasitic."
- **Cultivated natural capital** — replacing wild fish stocks or old-growth forest with fish farms or plantations buys appropriability and short-run yield at the cost of "redundancy, resiliency, and stability."
- **"Mētis to the rescue"** — the anomalies (slums, barter, illegal trade, landraces) that turn out to be integral to the formal order.

## Mental Models
- **Think of thinness as a resilience measure.** "The more schematic, thin, and simplified the formal order, the less resilient and the more vulnerable it is to disturbances outside its narrow parameters."
- **Use a fixed routine only when the task environment is repetitive, stable, and predictable** — there it may be exceptionally efficient. "In most economies and in human affairs generally, this is seldom the case, and such routines are likely to be counterproductive once the environment changes appreciably."
- **Think of a single metric as an invaluable tool that becomes a problem only when hegemonic.** Economics' resolving power comes from converting quality into one quantity: "Providing one understands the heroic assumptions required to achieve this precision and the questions that it cannot answer, the single metric is an invaluable tool. Problems arise only when it becomes hegemonic."
- **Think of language, common law, and democracy as the model institutions** — structures of continuity that are never still, continually remade by the mētis of their participants.

## Anti-patterns
- **Planning that assumes a static world** — routinely ignoring "the radical contingency of the future."
- **Mistaking aesthetic order for functional order** — cookie-cutter layouts of the collective farm, the ujamaa village, the Ethiopian resettlement "were designed, above all, to facilitate the central administration of production and the control of public life," not to work.
- **Designing for abstract citizens** — the resolution gained is bought by erasing exactly the attributes that make the design usable.
- **Single-purpose institutions in variable environments** — they approach sensory-deprivation tanks and, at the limit, asylums and workhouses.
- **Treating the invisible hand as the escape hatch** — Scott's explicit caution: the market "is itself an instituted, formal system of coordination... similarly dependent on a larger system of social relations which its own calculus does not acknowledge," including contract and property law, coercive enforcement, antecedent norms of trust and cooperation, and finally a finite, nongrowing ecosystem.

## Worked Example
*The two war memorials.* Maya Lin's Vietnam Memorial is a gently undulating site marked, not dominated, by a low black wall listing the fallen — ordered neither alphabetically nor by unit but chronologically, so that those who died in the same engagement stand together. It makes no prose or sculptural claim about the war. Visitors touch the names, make rubbings, and leave mementos — poems, a woman's high-heeled shoe, a glass of champagne, a poker hand of a full house, aces high — so many that a museum was built to hold them. "The memorial virtually requires participation in order to complete its meaning."

Contrast the Iwo Jima statue: heroic, larger than life, explicitly patriotic, referring to conquest and unity in victory. Moving in its own right, and appropriate to a war viewed with near-unanimity — but "more symbolically self-sufficient." Visitors "receive its message rather than completing it."

The lesson generalizes past memorials. A design that leaves room for its users to inscribe meaning uses *and renews* their mētis — "use it or lose it." A design that is symbolically self-sufficient is "canned": efficient where purposes are narrow, simple, and invariable; stultifying and brittle everywhere else. Applied to housing, this means flexible building designs and adjustable floor plans from the outset; applied to neighborhoods, promoting the diversity that ensures vitality; and above all it means planners and zoning bodies must *not* see their task as making neighborhoods hold to their designed forms through thick and thin.

## Key Takeaways
1. When you cannot enumerate second-order effects, take a small step, observe, then step again — and prefer the version you can undo.
2. Design in slack for the unforeseen and for later participants who will know more than you do.
3. Before shipping a plan, draw the second map: the activities your design does not represent but silently depends on.
4. Judge an institution by whether it grows the skill and responsibility of the people inside it — "canned" vs. mētis-open.
5. Prefer diverse, multipurpose, decentralized forms where the environment is variable; fixed routines are legitimately efficient only where it is stable.
6. Scott tempers his own condemnation on four grounds: the orders being supplanted were often manifestly unjust; high modernism came genuinely cloaked in emancipatory ideals (equality before the law, citizenship, rights to subsistence, health, education, shelter); representative institutions force accommodation; and ordinary people's day-to-day resistance remakes any plan given time. He also denies "the incontestable benefits either of the division of labor or of hierarchical coordination for some tasks" are in question.
