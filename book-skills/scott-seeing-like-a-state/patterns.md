# Patterns & Techniques — Seeing Like a State

Concrete moves Scott identifies: the state's techniques for producing **legibility**, the failure mechanisms they set off, and the counter-techniques he endorses.

---

## State Legibility Techniques (Part 1)

## Scientific Forestry
**When to use**: You need a single, countable output from a complex living system.
**How**: Define the abstraction (*Normalbaum* — the standard tree measured in cubic meters of usable timber); clear the "debris"; plant a single fast-growing species (Norway spruce) in even-age, straight rows; harvest on a fixed rotation.
**Trade-offs**: Spectacular first-rotation yields — the forest is now readable at a glance and the ledger balances. Second rotation collapses by 20–30%; German foresters coined *Waldsterben* ("forest death"). The recovery effort ("restoration forestry" — artificial nesting boxes, imported ant colonies, reintroduced spiders) is an admission that the cleared "debris" was the system. **Canonical case for every thin simplification that follows.**

## Cadastral Survey & Freehold Tenure
**When to use**: The state must identify who owes tax on which land.
**How**: Map parcels; assign each to a single named owner; give the categories the force of law.
**Trade-offs**: Makes taxation assessable and titles enforceable — but it does not describe the tenure system, it *creates* one. Overlapping, seasonal, and common-use rights that the grid cannot render simply cease to exist legally. **Facts on paper displace facts on the ground.**

## Permanent Surnames
**When to use**: The state needs to track individuals across time — taxation, conscription, corvée, criminal records, property.
**How**: Replace patronymics, occupational names, and locally sufficient naming customs with a fixed, heritable, officially registered surname.
**Trade-offs**: Foundational to citizenship, inheritance law, and public health registries. Also foundational to conscription lists and to rounding up designated populations — the same technique, opposite valence.

## Standardized Weights and Measures
**When to use**: You need to compare, aggregate, or trade across localities.
**How**: Replace relational, purpose-bearing local measures (a *journal* = a day's work; a farm "of two cows") with universal abstract units.
**Trade-offs**: Enables markets and the **synoptic view**. Destroys information — local measures encoded soil quality, labor input, and yield expectation in the unit itself. The abstract unit is precise about less.

## Haussmannization / The Grid City
**When to use**: The center wants a city it can see, supply, move through, and police.
**How**: Drive straight boulevards through the medieval tangle; standardize frontages; make the plan readable from above.
**Trade-offs**: Real gains in sanitation, circulation, and fire control. Also — explicitly — troop movement and the suppression of barricades. Legibility serves whoever holds the center.

## High-Modernist Design Patterns (Parts 2–3)

## Miniaturization
**When to use**: (Scott's warning, not endorsement) When a plan miscarries, its carriers retreat to a controllable micro-order.
**How**: Build a model village, model farm, or model city; subsidize and supervise it lavishly; display it as proof of concept.
**Trade-offs**: The demonstration succeeds *because* of the subsidy and supervision that cannot scale. Treating it as evidence for a national rollout is the mechanism by which pilots become catastrophes.

## Taylorism
**When to use**: Output is single-purpose, measurable, and the environment is stable.
**How**: Decompose work into repetitive, specified movements; move knowledge from the floor to the planning office.
**Trade-offs**: Genuine efficiency in stable, repetitive settings. But it is "not just a strategy of production, but also a strategy of control and appropriation," and it degrades exactly the improvisational capacity the plant depends on when conditions shift.

## Monocropping / Industrial Agriculture
**When to use**: Maximum bushels per acre at least cost, under controlled conditions.
**How**: One crop, uniform genetics, mechanized operations, one-variable-at-a-time trials on experimental plots.
**Trade-offs**: Yield gains are real. But the experimental plot's controlled conditions are themselves a simplification; hybrids persist only because of the diversity and immunities of antecedent landraces; and questions of soil structure, community, sustainability, and taste are excluded rather than answered.

## Villagization / Collectivization
**When to use**: (Diagnostic) The state wants population and production it can count, supply, and requisition.
**How**: Move dispersed households into gridded, roadside, standard-plan settlements; consolidate holdings; set quotas centrally.
**Trade-offs**: Optimizes **appropriation and control**, not production. Ignores the soil, rainfall, and grazing knowledge that determined the original dispersed pattern. Survives, when it survives, on the private plots and informal economy the plan forbids.

## Counter-Techniques Scott Endorses (Part 4)

## Work-to-Rule as Diagnostic
**When to use**: To test whether documented process equals actual process.
**How**: Ask what would happen if everyone followed every rule and job description exactly and did nothing else.
**Trade-offs**: If the answer is "work halts," you have proven the formal scheme is parasitic on uncodified practice — and any plan derived from the documentation is thin. Cheap to run as a thought experiment; the real thing is a strike.

## Small, Reversible Steps
**When to use**: Any intervention whose second- and third-order effects you cannot enumerate.
**How**: Take a small step → stand back → observe → plan the next small move. Prefer interventions that can easily be undone. "The first rule of intelligent tinkering is to keep all the parts" (Leopold).
**Trade-offs**: Slower and less impressive than a comprehensive rollout. Buys the ability to be wrong survivably — the thing every case in the book lacked.

## Designing for Surprise and for Human Inventiveness
**When to use**: Whenever the future is contingent — i.e. nearly always.
**How**: Choose land that can grow several crops; housing with adjustable floor plans; factory layouts that admit new processes. Assume later participants will improve on the design.
**Trade-offs**: Costs option value up front; refuses the resolution that comes from fixing requirements.

## The Two Maps Test
**When to use**: Assessing whether a designed system can stand alone.
**How**: Draw Map 1 (routes the design provides) and Map 2 (a time-lapse of all unplanned activity). Identify which Map 2 activities the system depends on.
**Trade-offs**: Surfaces the informal dependencies before they are inadvertently designed out.

## Building Mētis-Friendly Institutions
**When to use**: Whenever quality depends on the engaged participation of the people inside.
**How**: Apply the litmus test — does this form enhance the skills, knowledge, and responsibility of its participants? Prefer multifunctional, plastic, diverse, adaptable forms over single-purpose ones. Models: language, common law, democracy, the Vietnam Memorial.
**Trade-offs**: Where the purpose is "narrow, simple, and invariable over time," codified hierarchical routines may well be adequate and most efficient in the short run — Scott concedes this explicitly, along with "the incontestable benefits either of the division of labor or of hierarchical coordination for some tasks."
