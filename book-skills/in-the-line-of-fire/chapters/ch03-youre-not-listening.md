# Chapter 3: You're Not Listening!

## Core Idea
Results-driven people rush to answer before they fully understand the question ("Ready, Fire, Aim!"). The fix is a deliberate two-step that drives a wedge between question and answer: **Listen correctly, then answer properly.**

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"Ready, Fire, Aim!"** — the critical blunder: pulling the trigger before the target is in your sights. Answering before the key issue is crystal clear forces a desperate scramble and a wrong answer → audience thinks "You're not listening!"
  - When to use: catching yourself about to answer too fast.
- **Wrong Q&A (vs. "Rude Q&A")**: The conventional prep of a long list of tough questions + a parallel list of *canned answers*. Weissman renames it "Wrong Q&A" — if your answer is formulated before you understand the question as asked, it likely won't match.
- **The two-step remedy**: ① Listen correctly (Ch 4: Active Listening) ② Answer properly (Ch 5–6).

## Key Concepts
- **"I feel your pain"**: Clinton's four words — implication isn't just "I heard you" but "I *care* about you." The leitmotif that won 1992.
- **The deliberate delay**: the book intentionally withholds "how to answer" for several chapters to break the rush-to-answer reflex.

## Mental Models
- A question is a football: don't run before you've caught it (sets up Ch 4's "Yards After Catch").
- The "How?" that stops you cold is usually the real key issue — don't dodge it.

## Worked Example
**George H. Bush vs. Marisa Hall, 1992 town-hall debate** — the book's central cautionary tale. Hall asks: *"How has the national debt personally affected each of your lives? And if it hasn't, how can you honestly find a cure for the economic problems of the common people if you have no experience in what's ailing them?"* Bush (caught earlier glancing at his watch) answers "everybody," missing "you personally." It takes **four attempts** before he admits he doesn't understand. Then Clinton walks toward her — *"Tell me how it's affected you again… You know people who've lost their jobs?"* — she says *"Well, yeah, uh-huh."* That involuntary assent ("You were listening!") cast the die. Bush's "Ready, Fire, Aim!" answer was a turning point toward his defeat; Carville: *"I would have paid to have that question asked!"*

(Note the historical arc: after 1992, follow-on questions were eliminated — de facto in 1996, de jure in 2000/2004 — to prevent another Marisa Hall.)

## Key Takeaways
1. Never answer before the key issue is 100% clear — avoid "Ready, Fire, Aim!"
2. Listen correctly *then* answer properly; the gap between is sacred.
3. The fatal perception is "You're not listening!" (and cousins "That's not what I asked").
4. Repeating the questioner's own words signals "I heard you, I care."
