# Chapter 8: The Cohabitation Effect

## Core Idea
Living together is *not* a good test for marriage. Couples who cohabit before engagement are, on average, less satisfied and more likely to divorce — because they *slide* into a shared life instead of *deciding*, then get *locked in* by mounting switching costs.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Cohabitation Effect**: Couples who "live together first" are, on average, less satisfied with their marriages and more likely to divorce than couples who don't. Crucially, it's a **pre-engagement** cohabitation effect: couples who move in together *after* a clear, public commitment (engagement/marriage) do *not* suffer it.
- **Sliding, Not Deciding**: Most couples say cohabitation "just happened" — dating → sleeping over → sleeping over a lot → moving in, with no ring, ceremony, or even a conversation. Partners often have different, unspoken agendas (women: access to love; men: access to sex), and both hold *lower standards for a live-in partner than for a spouse.*
- **Lock-In (consumer lock-in, behavioral economics)**: The decreased likelihood of searching for or switching to a better option once you've invested.
  - **Setup costs**: The initial investment (shared furniture, pets, Wi-Fi, a lease, a weekend routine). The bigger the setup cost, the less likely you leave.
  - **Switching costs**: The time/money/effort to change. We *underestimate* them upfront because they're hypothetical and future — but "the switching costs seem bigger up close than they did from far away."

## Key Concepts
- **Cohabitation as prophylaxis**: The belief that moving in first prevents divorce (~2/3 of twentysomethings believe it) — the exact opposite of the evidence.
- **Approximating marriage ≠ testing it**: Cohabiting couples usually *don't* enter the areas that actually stress a marriage (mortgage, kids, in-law holidays, shared finances). It's "an intersection between college roommate and sex partner," not a marriage rehearsal.
- **Quicksand, not a quick exit**: Couples imagine "if it doesn't work out there's a quick exit." Lock-in turns the easy exit into quicksand.

## Mental Models
- **Deciding beats sliding.** A life built on a "Maybe We Will" doesn't feel as committed as one built on the "We Are" of engagement or "I Do" of marriage — especially for men.
- **Age raises switching costs.** What felt easy to leave at 25 feels impossible at 30 ("getting married sooner started to feel more important than whether it would work later").
- Choose your partner because you *want to be together*, "not because staying together is convenient or because breaking up is inconvenient."

## Anti-patterns
- **Moving in for convenience/economics without a conversation** about commitment level and what it means.
- **Testing "how serious he'll get" by living together** — while never actually treating living together seriously.
- **Locking into marriage to avoid "dividing up the furniture"** — especially once friends start walking down the aisle.

## Worked Example
Jennifer, 32, married Carter (a job-hopping musician) after 3+ years cohabiting — and was hunting for a divorce lawyer six months later. "I spent more time planning the wedding than I spent happily married." Working backward through "How did this happen?", they surfaced the sliding: "It was just easier… cheaper and more convenient… a quick decision, but if it didn't work out there was a quick exit." Her real criteria had been "good sex, fun weekends, cool crowd, cheaper rent" — while worries about Carter's absent career "rattled in the back of her head." Then lock-in: "We had all this furniture. We had our dogs and all the same friends… It just made it really difficult to break up." Turning 30 changed every switching cost ("I stayed with Carter because I couldn't afford to get a new couch"). She even admitted: "I almost didn't care whether it was going to work out. At least I would have gotten married when everybody else was. I would have been on track." The imagined "quick exit" became "quicksand."

## Reference: Lessening the Cohabitation Effect
- Ideally, **don't cohabit before an engagement.**
- Since that's not always realistic: **get clear on each person's commitment level *before* moving in.**
- **Anticipate and regularly evaluate the constraints** (setup/switching costs) that might keep you from leaving even if you want to.
- **Test the relationship in other ways** — a wider variety of activities than dating and sex (see Ch 10's Nicaragua principle).

## Key Takeaways
1. Cohabiting *before engagement* is associated with worse marital outcomes — it's not a safe "test."
2. The mechanism is sliding, not deciding — plus lock-in from setup and switching costs.
3. Cohabitation approximates roommate-plus-sex, not the stresses that actually test a marriage.
4. We underestimate switching costs upfront; they balloon up close, and age inflates them.
5. Make commitment a *decision*, not a drift — talk about it explicitly before moving in.
6. Stay (or marry) because you choose each other, not because leaving is inconvenient.
