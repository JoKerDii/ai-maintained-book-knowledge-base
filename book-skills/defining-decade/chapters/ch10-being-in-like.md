# Chapter 10: Being in Like

## Core Idea
Compatibility isn't a crime — it's a good plan. Beyond "being in love," aim to be **in like**: alike in ways that matter (especially personality) and genuinely liking who the other person is. The obvious similarities are deal breakers, not match makers — and high Neuroticism is toxic to any relationship.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Being "In Like"**: Two things — (1) being *alike in ways that matter* and (2) *genuinely liking* who the other person is. Similarity is the essence of compatibility: similar couples react the same way to a rainy day, a big party, a Sunday morning — forestalling enormous friction. "Opposites attract" maybe for a hookup; similarity sustains.
- **Deal Breakers, Not Match Makers**: Obvious similarities (age, education, religion, politics, attractiveness, intelligence) are qualities you *weed out* on — nonnegotiable filters. But their presence doesn't *make* you happy. "They may bring us together, but they don't necessarily make us happy."
- **The Big Five (personality — the real match maker)**: **O**penness, **C**onscientiousness, **E**xtraversion, **A**greeableness, **N**euroticism. About 50% inherited and relatively stable. It's not what you *like* or *have done* — it's *how you are in the world*, "the part of ourselves we take everywhere, even to Nicaragua." Personality similarity predicts relationship satisfaction, yet it's where couples tend to be *least* alike (because it's subtle and hard to categorize).
- **The Neuroticism Rule**: Being high on Neuroticism (anxious, stressed, critical, moody) "is toxic for relationships" — *more* predictive of unhappiness and dissolution than personality *dissimilarity*. High-N people view differences negatively → criticism and contempt, "two leading relationship killers." How you respond to differences matters more than the differences themselves.

## Key Concepts (The Big Five, low ↔ high)
- **Openness**: practical/conventional/routine ↔ curious/creative/adventurous.
- **Conscientiousness**: easygoing/careless/spontaneous ↔ disciplined/organized/dutiful.
- **Extraversion**: reserved/energized alone ↔ outgoing/novelty-seeking/energized by others.
- **Agreeableness**: antagonistic/suspicious ↔ cooperative/warm/trusting.
- **Neuroticism**: secure/resilient/takes things at face value ↔ tense/anxious/quickly sees the negative.

## Mental Models
- **Similarity helps early; diversification helps later.** A young couple's job is a shared vision (similarities validate, differences threaten). By the 40s+, with kids/home/work, a *diversification* of skills and interests helps — "differences can keep life fresh."
- **Be picky about the right things.** "Be picky about things that might matter in twenty years — extreme differences in values, goals, or personality — or whether you love each other," not everyday discrepancies.
- **A culture of criticism**: Friends "just like you" can decide everyone else does it wrong, seeing differences as deficiencies. Sometimes differences are just differences — even strengths.
- **Marriage is a commitment, not a guarantee.** You never know with complete certainty; "you weigh the evidence and you listen to yourself" — to what *matters*, not every dissatisfaction.

## Anti-patterns
- **Mistaking plain-sight commonalities for compatibility**: Eli and his girlfriend were both good-looking, Jewish, Democrats, same friends, good sex — and a poor personality match. Deal-breaker matches ≠ match-maker fit.
- **Hoping the other person will change** to become more similar (evidence for personality convergence over time is mixed).
- **High-Neuroticism nitpicking**: Courtney's lilies, unread books, "not funny enough at parties" — magnifying minor differences into verdicts.

## Worked Example
**Eli** was "sent by his girlfriend." Over months he voiced doubts — she didn't laugh much, buried herself in her dissertation, was slow to warm to his boisterous family — then softened each with "but she's sweet." Jay decoded them via the Big Five: Eli reads high Openness/Extraversion, lower Conscientiousness/Neuroticism; his girlfriend the near-opposite (low O/E, high C/N); both high Agreeableness (why they lasted). "Sometimes the only thing wrong with another person is that he or she is a poor match for your own personality." The **Nicaragua test** (Jay's rule: *third-world travel is the closest thing to being married and raising kids* — glorious highs, no escape, money tight, someone gets sick) exposed the mismatch: under stress they became extreme versions of themselves; the relationship ended soon after.

**Courtney**, 28, kept dodging a proposal, cataloguing Matt's flaws (too short, unread book, corny in-law scrapbooks, no lilies). Jay saw not incompatibility (they were reasonably similar) but Courtney's own high Neuroticism and a best-friend "culture of criticism." Using her own postpartum "pizza story" (unfairly raging at her husband over food when both were exhausted — different stress responses, not a character flaw), Jay reframed differences as normal. After a knee injury during which Matt cared for her selflessly, Courtney realized: "I'm the complainer and he doesn't even complain about that." She saw her *own personality* — not Matt — was the real challenge, and that now was the time to change it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Aim to be "in like": similar in ways that matter and genuinely liking the person.
2. Obvious similarities are deal breakers (filters), not match makers (happiness).
3. Personality (Big Five) is the real match maker — and where couples are least alike.
4. High Neuroticism predicts breakup more than any dissimilarity; how you handle differences matters most.
5. Be picky about 20-year things (values, goals, personality, love), not daily discrepancies.
6. Similarity smooths the early years; complementary differences help the later ones.
