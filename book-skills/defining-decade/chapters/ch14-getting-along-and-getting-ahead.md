# Chapter 14: Getting Along and Getting Ahead

## Core Idea
Personality changes more in the twenties than at any other time in life — and it changes for the better through *investing* in adulthood ("getting along and getting ahead"), not avoiding it. You don't feel like an adult and *then* join the world; joining the world is what makes you feel like one.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Twenties as the Prime Window for Change**: Contrary to the conventional wisdom that childhood/adolescence sets personality ("Give me the child until he is seven…"; Freud ended development at puberty), research now agrees: **personality changes more during the twenties than at any time before or after.** After 30, traits are relatively stable. This matters because the change coincides with when careers and relationships are being decided.
- **Getting Along and Getting Ahead**: In the twenties, positive personality change comes from *making adult commitments* — to bosses, partners, leases, roommates. "Feeling better doesn't come from avoiding adulthood, it comes from investing in adulthood." Being a cooperative colleague or successful partner *drives* the maturation; settling down helps you feel settled.
- **Goals as the Building Blocks of Adult Personality**: Even simply *having* goals increases happiness and confidence. In a study following ~500 adults college→mid-30s, increased goal-setting in the twenties led to greater purpose, mastery, agency, and well-being in the thirties. "Goals are how we declare who we are and who we want to be."

## Key Concepts
- **The maturation trend**: Across the twenties we typically become more emotionally stable, conscientious, responsible, socially competent, agreeable, happier, more confident — but "these changes don't happen for everyone." They come from investment, not from waiting.
- **Commitment > avoidance**: Employed twentysomethings are happier than unemployed ones (Pew); stable relationships (last or not) reduce social anxiety and depression and build interpersonal skill.
- **The cost of staying uncommitted**: Chronic singleness across the twenties usually doesn't feel good (80% of the persistently single were dissatisfied with their dating lives) and is especially hard on men's self-esteem near thirty.

## Mental Models
- **The "life transplant."** Sam wanted "a brain transplant"; Jay's answer: "That brain transplant is going to come from a *life* transplant. By joining the world, you could feel a lot better."
- **You have it backward.** "Feeling better doesn't come from avoiding adulthood" — you can't wait to feel like an adult before acting like one; the acting produces the feeling.
- **Be forgiving about what "settled" or "successful" means.** Even *working toward* a good job or relationship makes you happier; you don't need to have arrived.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Funemployment" and living out of a backpack**: Riding out unemployment "for fun" while feeling anxious, angry, and "at loose ends" — mistaking avoidance for freedom.
- **Believing a steady job and a place to care for "would only make me feel worse"** — the opposite of the evidence.
- **"I can't change / I need a brain transplant"**: Fatalism, when the twenties are precisely the window for change.

## Worked Example
Sam found out over Cheerios at 12 that his parents were divorcing; for six years he switched houses daily, hauling a backpack — the "every-other-night shit." In his twenties he replayed it: a scattered résumé (a new job nearly every year), "funemployed," crashing at five different places, drinking before parties to dodge "What do you do?", saying "the older I get, the less I feel like a man." Jay: "I'm not sure you're giving yourself much to feel like a man about." She prescribed a **life transplant** — a job and a steady place to sleep (citing Pew: employed twentysomethings are happier) — but Sam resisted, sure responsibility would "just add to the things I forget to do." The turn came through a **goal he actually wanted: a dog.** He'd lost a childhood dog after the divorce and blamed himself. When he got an apartment and a dog, "he came to life" — the rhythm and meaning had been missing for years. He walked dogs for money, assisted an obedience coach, then started his own canine daycare ("Dog Days"), entered a serious relationship, and volunteered raising guide-dog puppies. He'd forgotten that *caring for something* was a real strength — investing in adulthood matured his personality and his life.

## Key Takeaways
1. Personality is most malleable in the twenties — the change is real and consequential.
2. Positive change comes from investing in adulthood (getting along and getting ahead), not avoiding it.
3. Adult commitments — jobs, partners, leases — trigger personality maturation.
4. Goals alone boost purpose, mastery, and well-being now and in your thirties.
5. Even *working toward* good work and love raises happiness — you needn't have arrived.
6. Chronic avoidance/singleness usually feels bad, especially for men near thirty.
