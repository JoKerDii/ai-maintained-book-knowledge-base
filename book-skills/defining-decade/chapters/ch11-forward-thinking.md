# Chapter 11: Forward Thinking

## Core Idea
The twenties are the brain's *second and final* critical period, when the frontal lobe (the seat of forward thinking) finishes wiring itself — largely in response to what you do every day. It's a time of "great risk and great opportunity": use it or lose it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Frontal Lobe / Forward Thinking**: The most forward and most recently evolved part of the brain, behind the forehead — the "executive functioning center." Where reason regulates the emotional brain, where we process probability and time, tolerate uncertainty, anticipate consequences, and plan. It's "where we do our forward thinking."
- **The Second Critical Period (brain plasticity)**: The brain has two growth spurts. First: infancy (overproduction of neurons, then *pruning*). Second: adolescence through the twenties — thousands of new connections sprout, mostly in the frontal lobe, over-preparing us for the uncertainty of adult life (finding a niche, choosing a mate, parenting). The frontal lobe doesn't fully mature until sometime between **20 and 30**.
- **"Survival of the Busiest" / Use It or Lose It**: "Neurons that fire together, wire together." Connections you use are preserved and quickened; those you neglect are pruned away. "We become what we hear and see and do every day."

## Key Concepts
- **Uneven twentysomethings**: The emotional brain is fully online while the frontal lobe is still developing — so smart, accomplished people can be great at school yet struggle to start careers, choose partners, or calm themselves.
- **School smarts ≠ forward thinking**: Being smart in school is solving problems with correct answers and clear time limits. Adult dilemmas (which job, where to live, whom to partner) have no right answers — the frontal lobe lets us "act on better shades of gray."
- **Social recovery (Phineas Gage)**: After a tamping iron destroyed his frontal lobe, Gage became reckless — but years of the *regular routines* of stagecoach driving let his brain "relearn" deliberate, forward-thinking skills. Evidence of plasticity: the brain changes in response to the environment.

## Mental Models
- **The still-developing brain is a reason to engage, not to wait.** "Dumbing down the twenties is hardly the way to go." Even if the brain could wait, "love and work can't."
- **Forward thinking comes with practice and experience, not just age** — which is why some 22-year-olds are future-oriented and some 34-year-olds aren't.
- Think of twentysomething jobs, relationships, and setbacks as **the language of adulthood** your brain is primed to learn right now.

## Anti-patterns
- **Lying low until the brain "matures on its own"**: Waiting with the urban tribe or parents for answers to appear. "That's not how the brain works."
- **Treating the late-maturing frontal lobe as an excuse** for special services or postponed action.

## Worked Example (the Phineas Gage arc)
In 1848, 25-year-old railway worker Phineas Gage had a tamping iron blown through his frontal lobe. He lived, walked, and talked — but was "no longer Gage": reckless, vacillating, indifferent to others and consequences. His case gave early evidence that the *front* of the brain governs how we act (personal and social decision-making), even when intellect is intact. The less-told second half: Gage later worked *years* as a stagecoach driver — rising early, readying horses, driving 4 a.m. departures over rough roads. Historian Malcolm Macmillan calls this a **"social recovery"**: the daily routines let his frontal lobe relearn deliberate, forward-thinking behavior. Jay's lesson for twentysomethings: engaging with real jobs and relationships is exactly the "external stimulation" that wires the forward-thinking brain — the twenties are when that wiring is most possible.

## Key Takeaways
1. The frontal lobe (forward thinking) matures between ~20 and 30 — the twenties are its critical period.
2. Twentysomethings are "uneven": emotional brain fully on, executive brain still wiring.
3. School smarts and forward thinking are different skill sets; adult problems have no answer key.
4. The brain wires to what you do daily — "survival of the busiest," use it or lose it.
5. Forward thinking comes from practice and experience, not from waiting for age.
6. Engaging now (jobs, relationships, setbacks) teaches the brain the language of adulthood while it's most primed to learn.
