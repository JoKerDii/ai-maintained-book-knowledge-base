# Chapter 12: Calm Yourself

## Core Idea
The twentysomething brain reacts more strongly to negative information, so early work feels like being "a leaf in the wind." Learning to calm yourself — by *reappraising* difficult moments with your frontal lobe rather than fleeing, worrying, or outsourcing — is itself a mastery experience that grows roots.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Negativity Bias of the Young Brain**: MRI studies show twentysomething brains react more strongly to negative information than older brains do (more amygdala activity). Young adults find bad news more memorable than good — so criticism lands hard.
- **Flashbulb Memories & the Novelty Detector**: The brain is built to remember what catches us off-guard (a built-in novelty detector stimulates memory for the new/surprising/emotional). The twenties, full of firsts, produce more vivid memories than any stage — "a jarring but efficient and necessary way to grow."
- **The Positivity Effect (what age brings)**: With age we grow more interested in positive information and react less to the negative — "The art of being wise is knowing what to overlook" (William James). Older adults disengage from interpersonal conflict.
- **Reappraisal**: Getting out of the amygdala and putting the frontal lobe to work — reevaluating a situation based on the *facts* rather than catastrophizing. "Reappraising lessens, and even prevents, bad feelings." (Rooted in Viktor Frankl: our attitude and reaction are "the last of our human freedoms.")

## Key Concepts
- **Leaves vs. trees**: Twentysomethings feel "only as good as the last thing that happened" — a good day lifts them, a reprimand whips them down. With age (and practice) we get roots and a trunk that sways but doesn't break.
- **Microtraumas**: The everyday jolts of the twentysomething workweek (a wrong-toned e-mail, a public "screwup"), taken intensely personally.
- **Borrowing an ego**: Reaching out to someone else (calling mom on your lunch break) to let *their* frontal lobe do the calming. Fine occasionally — but over-externalizing means you never learn to soothe yourself, which undermines confidence.

## Mental Models
- **Get some roots and stand in the wind.** Not by *suppressing* feelings (that keeps body/brain stressed and impairs memory — "that's no better than chronic worrying"), but by answering the emotional brain with reason: **"What about the facts?"**
- **Having a feeling ≠ acting on it.** You can feel anxious and still not quit, lash out, or catastrophize.
- **Jobs and relationships usually aren't that fragile.** The catastrophe ("I'll be fired and waiting tables") is rarely rational — reappraise it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Flight**: Quitting messy jobs to change the feeling — which only confirms the fear that you're a poseur who didn't belong.
- **Chronic worrying**: Imagining the worst to avoid being caught off-guard — but it keeps the body in a negatively aroused state (raises heart rate and cortisol, feeds depression).
- **Suppression**: Stuffing feelings and pretending they aren't happening — leaves you "in a fog."
- **Over-borrowing an ego**: Calling parents/friends constantly, "robbing yourself of the opportunity to calm yourself down."

## Worked Example
Danielle, assistant to a huge TV-news name, called every Monday 8 a.m. begging to quit ("Just tell me I can quit… then I can make it one more day"). Her boss screamed daily (even "Where the hell am I?!" when *he* got lost). She felt like a fraud awaiting firing, experiencing work "like living in London during the Blitz." Jay explained the young brain's negativity bias and the leaf/tree image — "That's exactly how I feel. Like a leaf. He's like God." Danielle tried the wrong fixes: first *flight* (wanting to quit), then *chronic worrying* (sessions overflowing with errors and firing scenarios; lunch-break crying calls to parents). Jay reframed: those calls were "borrowing an ego," robbing her of self-soothing; suppression was no better. The tool was **reappraisal** — when something goes wrong, answer the amygdala with the frontal lobe: *What about the facts?* Jobs aren't that fragile; work isn't as personal as it feels. (Jay grounds it in her own "onion-chopping supervisor" and midnight-ER stories: getting through surprisingly hard moments is survivable.) The chapter ends mid-struggle — Danielle still doubting — setting up Ch 13's answer: confidence from mastery.

## Key Takeaways
1. The young brain over-weights the negative — criticism at work hits disproportionately hard.
2. You feel like a leaf now; roots come from practice, not from changing jobs.
3. Don't flee, chronically worry, or suppress — each keeps you stressed and confirms your fears.
4. Reappraise: answer the emotional brain with facts from the frontal lobe.
5. A feeling isn't a command — you can feel anxious without acting on it.
6. Borrowing an ego occasionally is fine; over-relying on it blocks self-soothing and confidence.
