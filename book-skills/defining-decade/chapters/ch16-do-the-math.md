# Chapter 16: Do the Math

## Core Idea
The twenties can feel like "living beyond time" — a cave with no markers, where the future feels far away and abstract. The antidote is to *do the math*: make a concrete timeline that works backward from what you want, bringing the future close enough to act on now.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Present Bias**: People of all ages discount the future, favoring today's rewards over tomorrow's ($100 now over $150 next month) — the root of procrastination, addiction, and not saving. Twentysomethings are *especially* prone (frontal lobe still developing) and are actively encouraged into it by clichés ("You have all the time in the world," "You're only young once").
- **Do the Math / The Timeline**: Work *backward* from what you want. Identify things you want (or don't want) in your 30s/40s/60s and count backward through the required steps. "Most twentysomethings can't write the last sentence of their lives, but when pressed, they can identify things they want… and work backward from there." (Modeled on novelist John Irving: "I always begin with the last sentence; then I work my way backwards.")
- **Memento Vivi**: Where cultures use *memento mori* (skulls, dying flowers) to remember mortality, twentysomethings need *memento vivi* — reminders that they are *going to live*, that life continues past the twenties and "might even be great." A timeline is one.

## Key Concepts
- **Condensing unmarked time (Siffre's cave)**: The brain can't keep time across long, unpunctuated intervals — speleologist Michel Siffre thought two months underground was 25 days. The post-graduation twenties, stripped of semesters and syllabi, are a "disorienting, cavelike existence."
- **The future-self experiment (Laura Carstensen)**: Twentysomethings shown an age-morphed image of their *future* selves allocated more than twice as much to retirement savings ($178 vs. $74) as those who saw their current selves. Seeing the future concretely changes present behavior.
- **Distance ↔ abstraction loop**: The further away love/work seem (in time, socially, spatially), the more abstract they feel; the more abstract, the further away — "round and round it goes." A timeline breaks the loop by making the future concrete.

## Mental Models
- **"There's a big difference between *having* a life in your thirties and *starting* a life in your thirties."**
- **Bring the future close.** Present bias thrives on psychological, social, and spatial distance — hanging out with people who don't talk about the future, imagining you'll settle "somewhere else." Counter it deliberately.
- **Make your own certainty.** "Our twenties are when we have to start creating our own sense of time, our own plans about how the years ahead will unfold."
- **Memento vivi over "now-or-never."** The culture pushes "now-or-never behaviors" (partying, blowing off responsibility) that don't make us happy for long; remember you'll be around for the decades after.

## Anti-patterns
- **Tossing goals "decades into the future"** to avoid them ("married at 40, first kid at 45") — leaving them "in a far-off, abstracted place."
- **Staying distracted** (texts, chats, *Law & Order* marathons, "magical thoughts") because the future feels distant and now-tasks feel skippable.
- **Believing "people do things later than they used to"** as license to not start — confusing *having* a later life with *starting* one late.

## Worked Example
Rachel, 26, had dropped out of a public-health master's, tended bar, slept till noon, and — despite saying she'd become a lawyer — hadn't moved toward a JD in two years ("I could have their jobs if I wanted… they're no smarter than I am"). She resisted a timeline ("I tell everybody I'm getting married at forty and having my first kid at forty-five"). Jay literally got out paper and had her **do the math backward**: Rachel said she'd start law by 30 → +1 year for LSAT/apps → 3 years law school → +1 year for the bar/first job = she'd be a lawyer at ~35. Then love: pressed, Rachel actually wanted a first child by 35 and marriage before that — not 40/45. "So between 30 and 35 you envision law school *plus* marriage *plus* baby… but you say you don't want to do them all at the same time." The collision made the abstraction concrete: "Then now is the time for school." Rachel bought LSAT books, quit bartending for a law-firm job (to gather references), aimed for a score to offset shaky grades, and ~2 years later started law school. She wrote back: "I thought if I didn't participate in adulthood, time would stop. But it didn't… Now I see I need to get going — and keep going," while still savoring her "unencumbered" years.

## Key Takeaways
1. The twenties can feel timeless; the brain condenses unmarked intervals — build your own markers.
2. Present bias makes us discount the future; twentysomethings are especially susceptible.
3. Seeing the future concretely (future self, a timeline) changes present behavior.
4. Do the math: work backward from what you want, counting the required steps.
5. Distance breeds abstraction breeds distance — deliberately bring love and work close.
6. Having a life in your thirties ≠ starting one then; make your own certainty now.
