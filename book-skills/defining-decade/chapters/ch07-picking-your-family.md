# Chapter 7: Picking Your Family

## Core Idea
"You can't pick your family, but you can pick your friends" is only true in childhood. When you choose a partner, you *are* picking your family — a second chance at the family you may never have had. Choose it as deliberately as you choose a career.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Picking Your Family**: Marriage isn't only a bond between two individuals — it's choosing (and creating) a family: in-laws, future children, holidays, the people "life will be about." Western individualism (rights over duties, choice over obligation) makes us forget this as "one of our greatest twentysomething opportunities."
  - When to use: Evaluating a serious relationship — look past the partner to the whole family system you'd be joining and building.
  - How: Meet and weigh the family. Have the define-the-relationship talk about kids, in-laws, the life you each want. Let your emotional reactions ("your tears are telling us something") be data.
- **A Second Chance at Family**: For those from broken families, partnering well is not a luxury but the most important thing — "When you partner with someone, you have a second chance at family."

## Key Concepts
- **YAVIS**: Young, Attractive, Verbal, Intelligent, Successful — high-functioning clients who get the *best* social reception but sometimes the *worst* care, because their polished image hides real pain ("falling up").
- **Falling up**: Being successful young as a survival strategy — good at hiding troubles so no one sees the imperfect interior.
- **Downward drift**: The lowest-functioning also get poor care as illness draws them into lower income brackets — the two extremes are both underserved.
- **Ambition asymmetry**: People from tough families are often highly ambitious about *work* ("I have to have a really good job to survive") but fatalistic about *love* ("a really good relationship is more than I can hope for"). Exactly backward — they're the ones who most need to partner well.

## Mental Models
- **Fear can be useful.** "Twentysomethings who aren't at least a little scared about their relationships are often the ones being the least thoughtful." Emma's fear meant she was taking love as seriously as work.
- **You are now picking your family** — so a poor choice isn't just "his family isn't perfect," it's the family you'll live inside for decades.
- Don't spend "a lifetime giving something you never get" by defaulting into a family that will never envelop you.

## Anti-patterns
- **Unambitious relationship descriptions**: "It's fine," "he's fun," "we have a good time" — a woefully inadequate account that hides real problems (jealousy, screaming, no shared future).
- **Rationalizing away the warning signs**: "I can't expect my boyfriend's parents to be perfect. Mine aren't." — using imperfection to dismiss a genuine mismatch.
- **Turning only to friends/therapists/boyfriends for "moments of solace"** instead of building a real, chosen family.

## Worked Example
Emma grew up in a family that collapsed (father's debt and suicide, mother's alcoholism) but "fell up" — honors student, top school, likeable, resilient, presenting as a "vanilla twentysomething." Beneath it she felt like "the loneliest person in the whole world," undone by the "In Case of Emergency, Please Call" blank in a new address book: "Who's gonna be there for me if I have a car wreck?" Jay's response: "We need to get you a new family." Emma had been "ambitious about work but unambitious about relationships," dating a jealous, unmotivated man. After a weekend with his uninterested parents she cried — and Jay named it: "You say you can't pick your family but you can pick your friends. That was true growing up. Now you're about to pick your family, and I'm concerned you're not making a good choice." Emma had the DTR talk; at 30 he didn't want kids or family involvement. She ended it (scared but right), got serious about family, and ~3 years later married a man who gave her that second chance — involved in-laws, sisters-in-law nearby, beach vacations. "Now, emergency-contact blanks don't seem big enough."

## Key Takeaways
1. Choosing a partner is choosing (and creating) a family — evaluate the whole system.
2. For those from broken families, partnering well is the highest-stakes decision, not an afterthought.
3. Beware the ambition asymmetry: high standards for work, fatalism about love.
4. Your emotional reactions to a partner's family are meaningful data — don't rationalize them away.
5. Being a little scared about a relationship is a sign you're taking it seriously.
6. You can consciously create the family you never had — and it will define the decades ahead.
