# Chapter 15: Every Body

## Core Idea
Fertility is a twentysomething topic, not a thirtysomething one. Most twentysomethings say a good family is their top priority — so they deserve the actual facts about the biological clock *before* they become the statistics, and should factor timing into their work-and-love plans now.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Do the Math on Fertility (the facts)**: Female fertility peaks in the late twenties; declines begin ~30, drop considerably at 35, and plummet at 40. Compared with her twentysomething self, a woman is about **half as fertile at 30, one-quarter at 35, one-eighth at 40.** Driven by two age-related changes: declining egg quality and a less-effective endocrine system → lower pregnancy odds and higher miscarriage.
- **The Availability Heuristic (misjudging the odds)**: Deciding how likely something is by how easily an example comes to mind. Kaitlyn pointed to two 40-year-old friends and Hollywood moms — but didn't know the base rates, or the "half-dozen fertility treatments" and the many who quietly *couldn't*. "That doesn't make the headlines."
- **Timing affects everybody (not just women)**: A biological clock ticks in men too (older sperm linked to autism, schizophrenia, dyslexia, lower intelligence). And postponing marriage/children compresses babies into the most strained newlywed years and collides child-rearing with peak earning years and aging parents.

## Key Concepts (the sobering numbers)
- **Per-cycle odds**: ~20–25% chance of conceiving per cycle up to ~35 (≈4–5 months trying); dropping to ~5% at 40, 3% at 41, 2% at 42 (≈20+ months at 40).
- **Miscarriage**: ~1/4 of pregnancies after 35 and ~1/2 after 40 miscarry.
- **Cost of intervention (avg. per live birth)**: ~$25k for a twentysomething couple, ~$35k at 35, ~$100k at 40, ~$300k by 42.
- **Treatment failure**: After 35, intrauterine insemination fails 90–95% of the time; IVF succeeds only ~10–20%. Many clinics won't treat fortysomething women (the failures lower their advertised success rates).
- **Childlessness**: In 1970, 1 in 10 fortysomething women were childless; today 1 in 5 — and about *half* of childless couples are not childless by choice.
- **Twentysomething priorities (Pew)**: 52% named being a good parent a top goal; 30% a successful marriage; only 15% a high-paying career, 9% free time, 1% fame.

## Mental Models
- **"Now is the time to decide."** "You don't want to figure out children are important to you just when you can no longer have them."
- **Medicine is "a science of uncertainty and an art of probability"** — no guarantees either way, but the age-related shifts are real and worth understanding.
- **Later childbearing lengthens the generational gaps** — memento of the poignant costs: an 80-year-old grandparent meeting a grandchild, fewer "sunny days at the lake with Grandpa."

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming the decision can wait** ("I haven't decided that yet" at 36): culture tells women it isn't pressing — it is.
- **Reasoning from anecdotes and headlines** (the availability heuristic) instead of base rates.
- **Prioritizing the big wedding and "a couple of years together first" without doing the math** on how that sequences against fertility.

## Worked Example
Kaitlyn, 34, came in to plan her *wedding* to Ben but never mentioned children. Jay asked directly ("What about kids?"); Kaitlyn was taken aback — "I haven't decided that yet." Jay pushed: "Then now is the time to decide… Getting married is a cinch, but having kids may not be. You could have a baby first." Kaitlyn deployed the **availability heuristic** ("People have kids in their forties… I have two forty-year-old friends… Hollywood does it all the time"), and prioritized the big wedding and "a couple of years together" first. At 38 she began trying: a year of trying, two miscarriages, then a fertility specialist. She went through intrauterine insemination, several rounds of IVF, and hormone treatments — none worked. By 43 clinics would no longer treat her. Kaitlyn and Ben never had a baby; sessions that began about wedding venues ended in grief. Jay pairs this with **Billy's MRI** — a 38-year-old in the "magnet coffin," terrified not of losing his past but his *future* (his son's hand, his wife), grieving "why I spent so many years on nothing… I traded five years of partying for five more years I could have had with my son."

## Key Takeaways
1. Fertility is a twentysomething issue — the twenties are biologically the easiest time to have a baby.
2. Fertility declines sharply after 35 and plummets at 40; miscarriage rises; treatment cost soars and success falls.
3. Don't reason from anecdotes/headlines — know the base rates (the availability heuristic misleads).
4. Timing affects men and partnerships too, and about half of childless couples didn't choose it.
5. Most twentysomethings want families most of all — so factor timing into work/love plans now.
6. Decide whether children matter *before* the window closes; postponing has real, poignant costs.
