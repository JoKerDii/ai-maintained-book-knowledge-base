# Chapter 1: Life Is Poker, Not Chess

## Core Idea
Life resembles poker (hidden information + luck), not chess (perfect information, minimal luck), so outcomes are a noisy signal of decision quality. The chief error this breeds is "resulting" — judging a decision by how it turned out.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Resulting**: Equating the quality of a decision with the quality of its outcome.
  - When to use (to avoid): Any time you're grading a past decision — yours or someone else's — by its result.
  - How: Ask "Given what was knowable then, was this a good bet?" separate from how it turned out.
- **Poker vs. chess**: A model for classifying decision environments. Chess = no hidden information, little luck, outcomes tightly track decisions. Poker = incomplete information, real luck, outcomes loosely track decisions.
  - How: Ask how much hidden information and luck a domain contains; the more there is, the less you can read decision quality off a single result.
- **Redefining "wrong"**: Any prediction that isn't 0% or 100% can't be proven wrong by a single outcome. A 24% event happening doesn't make "24%" wrong.
  - How: Judge calibration over many bets, not the result of one.

## Key Concepts
- **Incomplete information**: The condition of deciding when valuable facts remain hidden (opponents' cards, the future).
- **Hindsight bias**: After an outcome, seeing it as having been inevitable ("I should have known").
- **Reflexive vs. deliberative mind** (Gary Marcus) / **System 1 vs. System 2** (Kahneman): fast/automatic vs. slow/effortful thinking; the deliberative layer is thin and overtaxed.
- **"I'm not sure"**: The accurate, and often correct, answer under uncertainty — a strength, not an evasion.
- **Calibration**: Living in the shades of grey between 0% and 100% instead of black-and-white right/wrong.

## Mental Models
- Use the "carry a ruler" model: you can't unsee the Müller-Lyer illusion, so you build work-arounds — measure against your brain's distortions rather than trying to think them away.
- Think of expertise as better guessing, not certainty: an expert lawyer or player narrows the range of outcomes but still can't know the next flip.
- Prefer "how unsure am I?" over "am I sure?" — quantify uncertainty instead of chasing certainty.

## Anti-patterns
- **Monday Morning Quarterbacking**: Condemning a decision purely because the play didn't work (Pete Carroll).
- **Black-and-white thinking**: Modeling the world only at the extremes (the two-notch doctor's scale) destroys the ability to allocate resources well.
- **Driving-drunk-and-arriving-safe logic**: Changing behavior because a bad decision got a lucky result (or vice versa).

## Worked Example
**Pete Carroll's Super Bowl call.** Super Bowl XLIX, 26 seconds left, Seahawks trailing by 4, ball on the Patriots' 1-yard line. Instead of handing to Marshawn Lynch, Carroll called a pass; it was intercepted and Seattle lost. Every major outlet called it the "worst play call in NFL history." But the interception was a ~2% outcome (0 of 66 such passes intercepted that season), and a pass gave Seattle effectively three plays to score instead of two. Carroll made a good-quality decision that got a bad result — he "got unlucky." The universal condemnation was resulting: had Wilson completed the pass, the same call would have been "brilliant." Carroll himself said it "was the worst *result* of a call ever," not the worst call.

## Key Takeaways
1. Sort your domain: the more hidden information and luck, the less a single outcome tells you about decision quality.
2. Catch yourself resulting — grading decisions by outcomes — in your own life, not just in pundits.
3. Redefine "wrong": a low-probability event occurring doesn't refute a well-reasoned probability.
4. Get comfortable saying "I'm not sure"; it's usually the most accurate answer.
5. Because losses hurt ~2x as much as equal wins feel good, resulting inflicts real emotional damage — another reason to drop it.
6. You can't out-willpower cognitive illusions; build practical work-arounds instead.
