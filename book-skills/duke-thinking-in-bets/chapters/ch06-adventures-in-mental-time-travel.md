# Chapter 6: Adventures in Mental Time Travel

## Core Idea
In-the-moment thinking makes us irrational and emotionally reactive, but we can recruit our past- and future-selves as decision buddies. A toolkit of time-travel techniques — 10-10-10, backcasting, premortems, Ulysses contracts, scenario planning — pulls us out of the distorting "zoom lens" of the present and toward decisions aligned with our long-term goals.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **10-10-10 (Suzy Welch)**: Ask "What are the consequences of each option in 10 minutes? 10 months? 10 years?" (or the past-frame: "How would I feel today if I'd made this 10 min/months/years ago?").
  - When to use: In-the-moment decisions where present emotion is high (tilt, tired, a flat tire).
  - How: Run the three horizons before acting; it cues the accountability conversation and engages deliberative mind.
- **Backcasting**: Imagine you've *already succeeded* — hold up a newspaper reading "We Achieved Our Goal!" — then work backward to identify what got you there.
  - When to use: Strategic planning; mapping the positive path to a goal.
  - How: Stand at the destination and "remember" the decisions, events, and luck that produced success. (Prospective hindsight boosts identifying reasons for outcomes by ~30%.)
- **Premortem (Klein / Oettingen's mental contrasting)**: Imagine you've *failed* — "We Failed to Reach Our Goal" — then list why.
  - When to use: Alongside backcasting, before committing to a plan.
  - How: Give everyone permission to be the productive heckler; negative visualization surfaces obstacles and makes success *more* likely.
- **Ulysses contract**: A precommitment made by past-you to bind present-you against a predictable irrational impulse (Odysseus tied to the mast).
  - How: Raise barriers (throw out junk food, ride-share to the bar) or lower barriers (carry healthy snacks); even soft versions create a decision-interrupt.
- **Scenario planning / reconnaissance**: List the possible futures of a decision, then assign probabilities — even a wide range beats not guessing.
- **Decision swear jar**: A precommitment to flag words/thoughts that signal irrationality ("I'm sure," "so unlucky," "idiot," "wrong"), triggering a stop-and-think.

## Key Concepts
- **Temporal discounting**: Overvaluing a reward now versus a bigger reward later (Night Jerry vs. Morning Jerry; $2.5B in over-discounted military lump sums).
- **Tilt**: Emotionally unhinged decision-making caused by how things just turned out; the limbic system shuts down the prefrontal cortex (from pinball).
- **Ticker watching**: Reacting to moment-by-moment fluctuations instead of the long-term trend (Berkshire Hathaway zoomed in vs. out).
- **Zoom lens / path dependence**: In-the-moment emotion both magnifies and distorts; win $100 and feel sad, lose $100 and feel happy, depending on the recent path.
- **Prefrontal cortex / deliberative mind**: Imagining past or future recruits the same neural network (hippocampus + PFC), inhibiting emotional mind.
- **Moving regret in front of a decision**: Experiencing regret *before* choosing (Thoreau's productive regret) so it changes the choice rather than just wallowing after (Nietzsche's second act of stupidity).
- **Hindsight bias (the tree)**: The advancing present is a chainsaw that lops off all the unrealized branches; the 2%–3% outcome looks 100% inevitable afterward.
- **Expected value**: Probability-weighted worth (a $50k grant at 70% is worth more than a $200k grant at 10%).
- **Decision-interrupt**: The stop-and-think moment any precommitment creates when you consider breaking it.

## Mental Models
- Use 10-10-10 (or "It's all just one long poker game") when the last half hour looms large: the flat tire won't dent your happiness a year from now.
- Recruit your aged future-self as a buddy: subjects shown an age-progressed image allocated more than twice as much to retirement ($178 vs. $74).
- Backcast for the positive space, premortem for the negative space — the two must add to 100%; premortems shrink over-optimism to a realistic size.
- Set a Ulysses contract wherever you can predict future irrationality: precommit budgets, settlement floors, automatic retirement allocations.
- Do reconnaissance because you're uncertain, not despite it: you're already guessing every time you decide — make the probabilities explicit.
- Memorialize your decision tree so you don't rewrite history: keeping the unrealized branches guards against resulting and hindsight bias.

## Anti-patterns
- **In-the-moment decisions**: Deciding while emotionally unfit (on tilt) magnifies momentary events and degrades bets — a self-fulfilling prophecy.
- **Positive visualization alone**: Dreaming of success de-energizes action; weight-loss "starry-eyed dreamers" lost 24 lbs *less* (Oettingen).
- **Ticker-watching your own life**: Judging happiness by moment-to-moment ticks rather than the long-term trend.
- **Running the chainsaw**: After an outcome, treating it as inevitable ("I should have known," "Nate Silver was wrong" when Trump's branch was 30–40%; the *Jentz v. ConAgra* hindsight-bias verdict).

## Worked Example
**Night Jerry vs. Morning Jerry, and the mast.** Jerry Seinfeld's bit — Night Guy stays up late because exhaustion is "Morning Guy's problem" — is temporal discounting in miniature: present-self wins because future-self has no vote in the moment. Mental time travel gives future-self a vote. When Duke hit six-to-eight hours at the poker table and felt the pull to keep playing, she used a 10-10-10-style routine ("How have I felt when I kept playing before? Did I play my best?") to recruit past- and future-self and mitigate the risk that, while losing her edge, she'd convince herself the game was too good to leave. Her preset loss limit was a Ulysses contract: because she'd have to explain to her pod why she bought more chips, she got to *regret the decision before making it*. The mast, the loss limit, and the age-progressed retirement image all do the same thing — collide past, present, and future selves so present-us makes a better bet.

## Key Takeaways
1. In-the-moment thinking is the enemy; recruit past- and future-selves as decision buddies to engage deliberative mind.
2. Run 10-10-10 to keep momentary events in proportion and defuse tilt before deciding.
3. Move regret in front of decisions — anticipating a bad outcome improves the choice and softens the blow.
4. Pair backcasting (positive path) with a premortem (failure modes); negative visualization makes goals more, not less, achievable.
5. Precommit with Ulysses contracts to bind predictable future irrationality; even weak barriers create a decision-interrupt.
6. Do reconnaissance/scenario planning: list possible futures and assign probabilities, even as wide ranges — you're already guessing.
7. Memorialize your decision tree to fight hindsight bias and resulting; the outcome that happened was only one branch.
8. Life is one long game — take satisfaction in making good bets, not in guaranteed outcomes.
