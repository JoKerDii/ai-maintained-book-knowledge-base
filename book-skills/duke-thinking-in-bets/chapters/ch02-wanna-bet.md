# Chapter 2: Wanna Bet?

## Core Idea
All decisions are bets — against alternative future versions of ourselves — and a bet is only as good as the belief behind it. Because we form beliefs credulously and defend them stubbornly, the fix is to treat beliefs probabilistically and stress-test them as if money were on the line.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **All decisions are bets**: Every choice risks resources on one uncertain future and forecloses others; "not betting" is itself a bet.
  - When to use: To surface the risk and opportunity cost hidden in ordinary choices (jobs, parenting, buying a house).
  - How: Name the alternative futures, the resources at stake, and your probability estimates.
- **Most bets are bets against ourselves**: We usually aren't betting against another person but against the future versions of us we're not choosing.
- **"Wanna bet?"**: A challenge (real or imagined) that triggers vetting of a belief.
  - When to use: Whenever you catch yourself stating something as certain.
  - How: Run the vetting questions — How do I know this? What's my source quality? What am I missing? What are the plausible alternatives?
- **Redefining confidence**: Express *how* confident you are (0–10 scale ≈ percentage, or a range of alternatives), not merely *whether* you're confident.
  - How: "I'm 60% that Citizen Kane won Best Picture" or "Elvis died between 40 and 47."

## Key Concepts
- **Belief-formation flaw**: We don't hear→vet→believe; we actually hear→believe→(sometimes) vet. Default is to believe what we hear (Gilbert).
- **Motivated reasoning**: Processing new information through the lens of protecting existing beliefs — seeking confirming evidence, discrediting the rest.
- **"They saw a game" effect**: Prior beliefs change how we experience the same event (Dartmouth-Princeton; "They Saw a Protest").
- **Blind-spot bias**: We spot bias in others but not ourselves — and it's *larger* the smarter/more numerate we are.
- **Belief calibration**: Updating beliefs in small percentage moves toward accuracy instead of all-or-nothing reversals.

## Mental Models
- Think of every belief as carrying a confidence percentage; then disconfirming evidence means "I was 58%, now I'm 46%" — a nudge, not an ego-shattering flip from right to wrong.
- Use "invite collaborators": saying "I'm 80%" signals truthseeking and opens the door for others to add what they know.
- Treat "being smart" as a warning, not a shield: intelligence and numeracy make you better at *spinning* data to fit your beliefs.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stating beliefs as 100% fact**: Makes you less credible and shuts down the flow of correcting information.
- **Suited-connectors reasoning**: Adopting a belief because "everyone knows" it, without ever keeping your own P&L (the low-fat diet at population scale).
- **Filter bubbles / echo chambers**: Letting confirming sources do your motivated reasoning for you.

## Worked Example
**"Wanna bet?" on Citizen Kane.** You gush that Citizen Kane "obviously won the Best Picture Oscar." A friend says, "Wanna bet?" Suddenly you're on your heels, mentally running an inventory: How do I know this? Where did I hear it? How reliable is that source? What have I been this confident about before that turned out false? The challenge forces the third step — vetting — that we usually skip after hearing→believing. Even when no money changes hands, the frame does the work: it makes you temper the claim, quantify your real confidence, and get closer to the truth. (Duke pairs this with baldness-from-the-maternal-grandfather and dog-years myths — widely believed, never vetted.)

## Key Takeaways
1. Recognize that every decision is a bet on an uncertain future, largely against your own unchosen alternatives.
2. Your default is to believe what you hear; assume your beliefs are under-vetted until challenged.
3. Being smart doesn't protect you — it can make motivated reasoning worse. Expect your own blind spot.
4. Replace "am I confident?" with a number or range; calibrate in small moves rather than flip right↔wrong.
5. Declaring uncertainty makes you *more* credible and invites others to improve your beliefs.
6. Train yourself to see the world through "Wanna bet?" — nearly nothing is 0% or 100%.
