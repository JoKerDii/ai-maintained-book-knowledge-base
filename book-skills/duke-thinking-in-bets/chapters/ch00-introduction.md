# Introduction: Why This Isn't a Poker Book

## Core Idea
How our lives turn out is determined by exactly two things: the quality of our decisions and luck. Learning to tell the two apart — treating every decision as a bet on an uncertain future — is the whole skill.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **A bet is a decision about an uncertain future**: Duke's reframe learned at the poker table. Every decision commits resources to one possible future while forgoing others.
  - When to use: Any consequential choice where the outcome isn't fully in your control.
  - How: Make the implicit explicit — name the alternatives, the probabilities, and what you're risking.
- **Decision quality ≠ outcome quality**: A good process can produce a bad result (bad luck) and a bad process a good result (good luck). Judge the bet, not the roll.

## Key Concepts
- **Thinking in bets**: A practical approach to decision-making that separates decision quality from outcome quality and treats beliefs probabilistically.
- **Decision quality**: The soundness of the process given what was knowable at the time — the only part you control.
- **Luck**: The influence on outcomes of things outside your control (others' actions, weather, genes, hidden cards).
- **Truthseeker**: Someone who wants to know what's true regardless of whether it flatters existing beliefs.
- **The learning puzzle**: Because winning/losing are only loose signals of decision quality, feedback is hard to leverage into learning.

## Mental Models
- Think of a poker hand as a compressed lab for decisions: ~20 decisions in two minutes, each with a concrete but noisy result. Real life is the same process, just slower and murkier.
- Use "moved to a new kind of lab," not "left academics": poker is where Duke studied how people actually learn and decide under uncertainty.

## Anti-patterns
- **Chasing an always-rational, emotion-free ideal**: Mistakes, emotions, and losing are inevitable because we're human; the goal is *movement toward* objectivity, not perfection.
- **Treating winning as proof of good decisions**: You can win lucky hands and lose unlucky ones; results alone don't validate a process.

## Worked Example
**The poker lab.** At 26, Duke left a cognitive-psychology PhD, moved to Montana, and started playing poker in a Billings bar to make money. A hand takes two minutes and can involve twenty decisions, each ending in a concrete win or loss. But the feedback is tricky: you can play brilliantly and lose, or badly and win. Facing "grizzled ranchers" who would systematically take her money, she had to either solve this learning puzzle or go broke. World-class players taught her the core reframe — a bet is a decision about an uncertain future — which let her find learning opportunities in noisy environments, dodge decision traps, and keep emotion out of the process. The same tools later transferred to traders, executives, lawyers, and entrepreneurs.

## Key Takeaways
1. Two things decide how life turns out: decision quality and luck. Everything in the book is about telling them apart.
2. Reframe every decision as a bet — a choice among uncertain futures, each with benefits and risks.
3. Separate decision quality from outcome quality; don't grade a process by a single result.
4. The goal is movement toward objectivity and open-mindedness, not perfect rationality.
5. Build the habit of saying "I'm not sure" — comfort with uncertainty is the foundation.
