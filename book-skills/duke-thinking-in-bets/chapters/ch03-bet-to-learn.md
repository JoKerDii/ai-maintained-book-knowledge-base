# Chapter 3: Bet to Learn — Fielding the Unfolding Future

## Core Idea
Learning from experience requires "fielding" each outcome into a luck bucket or a skill bucket — but self-serving bias makes us systematically credit wins to skill and blame losses on luck, breaking the learning loop. Treating each fielding decision as a bet, plus perspective-taking, corrects the error.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Fielding outcomes (luck bucket vs. skill bucket)**: For every result, decide whether it was driven by your decisions (skill) or things outside your control (luck) — like an outfielder deciding where to throw.
  - When to use: Any outcome you want to learn from.
  - How: Ask "Would the same decision predictably produce this again?" If yes → skill; if it hinged on things you couldn't control → luck. Most outcomes are a blend.
- **The learning loop**: Outcomes → field into luck/skill → feed skill-driven results back into belief-updating → better bets. Fielding errors sever the loop.
- **"Wanna bet?" redux + perspective taking**: Treat the fielding decision as a bet, and ask "What if this outcome had happened to someone else?" (or vice versa) to land in the truthful middle.

## Key Concepts
- **Self-serving bias**: Fielding good outcomes into the skill bucket (credit) and bad ones into the luck bucket (blame elsewhere) to protect self-image.
- **SnackWell's Phenomenon**: Consuming more of something with less of a "bad" ingredient; also a case of how hard it is to work backward from an outcome to its cause.
- **Predictably irrational fielding**: Fielding errors aren't random — they follow the credit-good/blame-bad pattern (Phil Hellmuth: "If it weren't for luck, I'd win every one").
- **Peer fielding / schadenfreude**: We flip the script for others — their bad outcomes are their fault, their good ones are luck.
- **The habit loop (Duhigg)**: Cue → routine → reward; change a habit by keeping the cue and reward but swapping the routine.

## Mental Models
- Field outcomes like an outfielder: the "throw" (luck vs. skill) is a consequential in-the-moment bet, not an afterthought.
- Substitute the routine, not the reward: keep the ego reward but earn it by being the best credit-giver, mistake-admitter, and finder-of-mistakes-in-wins (Phil Ivey).
- Use the comparison drive productively: since we benchmark against peers, "win" by being unusually good at truthseeking rather than by being right.
- Watch for the "one-degree navigation error": tiny improvements in fielding compound like interest over a long game.

## Anti-patterns
- **"If it weren't for luck, I'd win every one"**: Off-loading all losses to luck and onboarding all wins to skill — the purest fielding error.
- **Bartman-style peer blame**: Loading a bad outcome entirely onto one person's decision while ignoring massive luck and others' contributions.
- **Variable-reward chasing**: Like rats (and slot players) on intermittent reinforcement, persisting because "I'm due" instead of updating beliefs.

## Worked Example
**Nick the Greek.** A Billings player who believed the mathematically *worst* starting hand (seven-deuce offsuit) was the best because "they never see it coming," and the best hand (pair of aces) the worst because it's predictable. He played seven-deuce constantly; occasionally it won, and he fielded those wins into the skill bucket ("my brilliant surprise attack"). His far more frequent losses he fielded into the luck bucket ("bad luck"). Because he never fielded a loss as a signal to examine his strategy, he never updated his beliefs and eventually went broke. He got years of tight, immediate feedback — and learned nothing, because his fielding was systematically self-serving. The contrast is Phil Ivey, who after a half-million-dollar tournament win spent dinner dissecting his own potential mistakes.

## Key Takeaways
1. Learning depends on fielding outcomes accurately into luck vs. skill; most outcomes are a mix, not 100% either.
2. Expect self-serving bias: credit wins to skill, blame losses on luck — and flip it (unfairly) for peers.
3. Treat each fielding decision as a bet; the prospect of being "wrong" forces you to weigh alternative causes.
4. Use perspective-taking ("What if this had happened to me / to them?") to reach the truthful middle.
5. Reshape the habit: keep the ego reward, but move the routine to truthseeking, credit-giving, and mistake-finding.
6. Small gains in fielding compound; catching even a few extra learning opportunities transforms results over time.
