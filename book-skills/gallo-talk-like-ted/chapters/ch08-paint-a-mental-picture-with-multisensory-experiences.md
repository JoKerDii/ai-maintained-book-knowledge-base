# Secret 8: Paint a Mental Picture with Multisensory Experiences

## Core Idea
Deliver presentations that touch more than one sense — sight, sound, touch (taste and smell rarely). The brain doesn't pay attention to boring things and craves multisensory input; recall is far stronger when multiple senses are stimulated at once.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Multimedia / Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning** (Richard Mayer): explaining with multiple sensory inputs (auditory + visual + kinesthetic) *always* — not sometimes — produces more accurate recall than one channel alone. The effect is strongest when the audience has *low prior knowledge* (the exact case in most important presentations: pitches, launches, first-day teaching, training, selling to new customers).
- **Design around one dominant sense, incorporate 1–2 others**: sight (dominant), sound, touch.
- **Picture Superiority Effect (PSE)** / **Multimedia Principle**: concepts shown as pictures (not words) are far more likely to be recalled. Hear info → recall ~10% three days later; add a picture → recall ~65% (6x more). People recalled 2,500+ pictures at 90% accuracy days later.
- **Dual-Coding Theory** (Allan Paivio): visual and verbal info are stored separately; a picture is encoded both visually AND verbally (double-stamped), while a word is verbal only — so pictures are richer and easier to recall.
- **One theme per slide** (Bono): each statistic/point gets its own slide ("Extreme Poverty Halved"); never dump an avalanche of numbers and charts in one view.
- **Anaphora**: repeating the same opening words across successive clauses (MLK's "I have a dream"; Bono's "We are going to win because..."). An auditory rhetorical device — deliver these *without slides* so the audience focuses on the words.

## Key Concepts
- **Multitasking is a myth** (John Medina): the brain is "biologically incapable of processing attention-rich inputs simultaneously." Asking the audience to read a wordy slide AND listen is an impossible load.
- **The 40-word slide problem**: the average PowerPoint slide has 40 words; TED slides almost never approach it. Brené Brown took 25 slides to reach 40 words; Bill Gates took 15 slides.
- **The visual cortex can't tell real from imagined** (Pascale Michelon): vividly imagining something activates the same brain areas as seeing it — so metaphors, analogies, and rich verbal imagery can paint pictures even with *no* slides (Janine Shepherd's bike ride; Stacey Kramer's Tiffany-box "gift" = brain tumor).
- **Concrete over abstract**: "the brain is not designed to grasp abstractions"; use concrete, picturable examples even in sales pitches.
- **Multimodal encoding**: pictures are processed through several channels, giving deeper, more meaningful encoding than text/sound.

## Mental Models
- **Presentation as a Broadway play**: a wonderful story, intriguing characters, and relevant props/simple props that put the audience in the scene.
- **Use visuals to enhance words, not duplicate them** (TED Commandment) — slides complement the narrative; they don't replace it (Ballard's 57 slides had *zero* words: "I'm storytelling, not lecturing").
- **Think about how you want them to FEEL, not just what they should know** (Maya Angelou: "people will never forget how you made them feel"; Chris Jordan's 32,000-Barbie image lets us *feel* big numbers).
- **Silence draws out the senses** — the USB-drive demo transferred a file in 10.5s vs. 40+s silently, letting the audience *feel* the pain of waiting.

## Anti-patterns
- **Death by PowerPoint**: cluttered, bullet-point, text-heavy slides; the least memorable way to transfer info. Kill bullet points on most slides.
- **Wordy slides read while you speak**: impossible for the brain (no multitasking); the message is lost.
- **Chart after chart after chart**: eyes glaze; break up data slides with stories and photos to rest the eyes.
- **Abstract feature-speak**: "read/write speed 190/170 MB/s" means nothing; demonstrate the felt benefit instead.

## Worked Example
**Al Gore (TED 2006 → An Inconvenient Truth)** — slides (designed by Nancy Duarte in Keynote) made the science of global warming "the most powerful and clear explanation" producer Laurie David had ever seen, inspiring the Oscar-winning film and Richard Branson's pledge of 100% of Virgin's transportation profits to clean energy. Gore explained the greenhouse effect with animated sun rays, red infrared lines escaping/trapped, and photos of smokestacks — complex science made visual and simple. Compare: **Lisa Kristine (TEDx)** — reciting her slavery narrative *first* and revealing each photo *after*, forcing the audience to listen (auditory) before seeing (visual): "I'm 150 feet down an illegal mine shaft in Ghana. The air is thick with heat and dust..." And **Dr. Elliot Krane** used *touch/kinesthetic*: stroking his arm with a feather, then igniting a blowtorch beside it, to make the audience *feel* chronic pain.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build every important presentation as a multisensory experience (low-prior-knowledge audiences need it most).
2. Use pictures instead of text; aim for ≤40 words across the first 10 slides; kill bullet points.
3. One theme/statistic per slide; break data runs with stories and photos.
4. Don't force the audience to read and listen at once — the brain can't multitask.
5. When you have no slides, paint pictures with vivid, concrete words, metaphors, and analogies (the visual cortex can't tell real from imagined).
6. Add sound (vocal delivery, multiple voices, video) and touch (props, demos, participation); design for how you want the audience to *feel*.

## Connects To
- **Secret 2**: painting pictures with words = storytelling's sensory payload.
- **Secret 3**: vocal delivery (rate, pause, punch) is the auditory channel.
- **Secret 5**: props, images, and demos are also jaw-dropping moments.
- **Secret 7**: pictures let you simplify and compress into 18 minutes.
