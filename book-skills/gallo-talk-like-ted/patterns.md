# Patterns — Concrete Techniques from Talk Like TED

## The "What Makes Your Heart Sing?" Interrogation
**When to use**: at the very start of building any talk, before content or slides.
**How**: ask three escalating questions — (1) "What do you do?" (2) "What are you passionate about?" (3) "What is it about this that makes your heart sing?" The third answer, tied to identity and origin story, is the richest. Also ask "What business am I *really* in?"
**Trade-offs**: requires honest self-excavation; the true answer may be adjacent to your day job (Mullins → human potential, not prosthetics).

## Ethos/Logos/Pathos Content Audit
**When to use**: reviewing a drafted presentation's balance.
**How**: categorize every sentence as Ethos (credibility), Logos (data), or Pathos (story/emotion). Benchmark against Stevenson's 10/25/**65**. If pathos is thin, add stories, anecdotes, personal insight.
**Trade-offs**: too much pathos with no logos leaves claims unsupported — persuasion needs both head and heart.

## Pick One of the Three Story Types
**When to use**: any pitch or presentation lacking emotional connection.
**How**: choose a personal story (tied to theme), a story about another person the audience relates to, or a brand success/failure story. For personal stories, make them richly descriptive; favor an unexpected outcome (Ariely's burn unit).
**Trade-offs**: personal stories require vulnerability; brand stories require you to hunt for them everywhere.

## Hero-and-Villain Structure
**When to use**: framing a product/idea pitch.
**How**: reveal a challenge (villain) facing the industry; the brand/product (hero) rises to meet it; customers (townspeople) are freed. Ensure the hero saves the day. Tread carefully if the villain is a named competitor.
**Trade-offs**: can feel formulaic if forced; the villain must be real to the audience.

## The Twitter-Friendly Headline
**When to use**: Step One of crafting any talk.
**How**: write the single most important message in ≤140 characters; it must convey what the thing is AND what makes it unique. Test: could a listener answer "What did that person talk about?"
**Trade-offs**: a tagline is not a headline — it must be specific and informative, not clever fluff.

## Build a Message Map
**When to use**: outlining a pitch (15 seconds) or full talk (18 minutes).
**How**: one page — (1) Twitter headline at top; (2) three supporting key messages; (3) bullet prompts (stories, stats, examples) under each. Optionally three sub-points per message.
**Trade-offs**: forces you to cut to three; if you have more, group into three categories.

## The Three-Story Structure
**When to use**: as the whole outline for an 18-minute talk.
**How**: tell three stories of three people/cases that share one theme (Carter's three eco-entrepreneurs). Tie them together with an explicit closing theme.
**Trade-offs**: needs three genuinely resonant examples; weak third story dilutes.

## Deliver a Jaw-Dropping Moment (5 options)
**When to use**: once per presentation, to create an emotionally charged event.
**How**: (1) prop/demo (Gates's mosquitoes, Shaw's paint), (2) shocking statistic in context (Stevenson's incarceration numbers), (3) image/video (Raghava's brain-wave art), (4) memorable sound bite ("woolly mammoths back"), (5) personal story (Hrabowski's jail-at-12). Plan the story first; open slide software last.
**Trade-offs**: props can misfire if under-rehearsed; shock must serve the point, not gratuitous.

## Make Statistics Jaw-Drop (Contextualize)
**When to use**: any data point that is groundbreaking or paramount.
**How**: never leave data dangling — frame it in a relatable context. iPad Mini "as thin as a pencil, as light as a pad of paper"; strawberries = "10% of ag jobs on 5% of farmland." Brainstorm the framing with a colleague.
**Trade-offs**: over-framing trivial numbers wastes attention; reserve for the paramount stat.

## Be Funny Without Telling a Joke (5 techniques)
**When to use**: to lower defenses and increase likability, especially on complex/heavy topics.
**How**: (1) anecdotes/observations/personal stories, (2) analogies/metaphors, (3) quotes (do your homework — off-the-beaten-path, relevant), (4) video (offloads the funny), (5) photos (metaphor for a complex idea). Open with light observational humor, not a big laugh.
**Trade-offs**: jokes bomb for non-comedians; crass/sexist humor destroys credibility instantly.

## Humor + Shock + Statistics Formula
**When to use**: heavy, uncomfortable, or traumatic topics (Rose George on sanitation).
**How**: blend all three — humor for a mental break, shock to make them care, statistics for substance. None alone works.
**Trade-offs**: mistune the blend and you're either flippant or repellent.

## Stick to 18 Minutes (with Soft Breaks)
**When to use**: every presentation; longer ones need modification.
**How**: cap at 18–20 minutes. If longer is unavoidable, insert a soft break (story, video, demo, or another voice) every ~10 minutes to reset cognitive load.
**Trade-offs**: "too much information" is the common objection — do the exercise anyway; constraint breeds creativity.

## Verbal Highlighting (Rate, Volume, Pitch, Pauses)
**When to use**: delivery, to emphasize key words.
**How**: aim ~190 wpm; raise/lower volume, change speed, or set a key word off with a pause. Vary pace by content (Dr. Jill accelerates to 220 wpm acting out chaos; slows for gravity). Get the rate right first — nothing else matters otherwise.
**Trade-offs**: most people unnaturally slow down when presenting; match natural conversation.

## Gesture Within the Power Sphere
**When to use**: throughout delivery.
**How**: keep gestures and eye gaze in the zone from eyes → outstretched hands → navel. Use them (hands out of pockets), sparingly, naturally, at key moments. Complex thinkers use complex gestures.
**Trade-offs**: canned/imitated gestures look like an SNL caricature; hands below the navel read as low-energy.

## Power Pose + Physical Warm-Up
**When to use**: minutes before going on, to manage nerves.
**How**: hold arms outstretched ~2 minutes where no one sees you (raises testosterone, lowers cortisol). Add a physical ritual (movement, fists) to boost energy.
**Trade-offs**: overdoing the ritual publicly looks foolish; do it backstage.

## Record-and-Review Practice Loop
**When to use**: refining any presentation.
**How**: rehearse in front of diverse people; record on phone/camera; watch back for fillers ("um/ah"), distracting habits (fidgeting, hands in pockets, staying rigid), pacing, and eye contact. Leave the camera frame several times to check you're not too rigid.
**Trade-offs**: time-intensive (Palmer 4 months/105 people; Dr. Jill 200 rehearsals) but it's what makes it look effortless.

## Picture Superiority Slide Design
**When to use**: designing any slide deck.
**How**: use pictures instead of text; ≤40 words across the first 10 slides; kill bullet points; one theme/statistic per slide; break data runs with stories and photos. Slides complement, never replace, the narrative.
**Trade-offs**: takes creative effort to find/make images; but text slides are the least memorable channel.

## Paint Pictures With Words (No Slides)
**When to use**: emotional peaks, or when slides would detract.
**How**: use vivid, concrete, sensory language, metaphors, and analogies — the visual cortex can't tell real from imagined (Shepherd's bike ride; Kramer's Tiffany-box "gift"). Deliver anaphora and emotional climaxes without any slide.
**Trade-offs**: demands strong writing and delivery; a weak verbal image falls flat.

## Multisensory Touch (Props, Demos, Participation)
**When to use**: to complete the sensory journey, especially for abstract ideas.
**How**: build in a physical demo (USB-drive transfer race in silence; Krane's feather + blowtorch), a prop (Palmer's milk crate), or audience participation (Gallo's Lush soap giveaway).
**Trade-offs**: props for pure ideas take imagination; rehearse so they don't feel silly.

## Stay in Your Lane / Rehearse to a Friend First
**When to use**: to ensure authentic delivery.
**How**: after crafting and rehearsing, let go and speak from the heart; present first to a friend/spouse to let the real self emerge; "hold the space" at vulnerable moments rather than retreating to safe teaching.
**Trade-offs**: vulnerability feels risky, but audiences distrust the phony; imitation of star speakers fails.
