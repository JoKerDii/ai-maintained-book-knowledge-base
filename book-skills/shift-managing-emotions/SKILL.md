## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**Trigger vs. Trajectory (Ch 2)** — The load-bearing distinction of the book. You cannot control the **trigger** (the event plus its automatic emotional response) any more than you control the world. You *can* control the **trajectory** — every subsequent decision point to elaborate, compartmentalize, or double down. "A fire can spontaneously blaze to life, but once it does, we have opportunities to either extinguish it *or* fuel it." Roughly 40% of people believe emotions are uncontrollable; they're right about the trigger and wrong about everything after it, which costs them the whole game.

**The two check-engine lights (Ch 1)** — Diagnose emotional misfire by **intensity** (proportional to circumstance?) and **duration** (still delivering information?), never by whether the emotion is pleasant. All emotions are functional: envy → work harder; regret → don't repeat; guilt → make amends; anger → correct injustice; fear → sharpen and act; sadness → slow down, mourn, and signal others to help.

**The six shifters, nested** — Innermost, always with you: **sensory** (Ch 3), **attention** (Ch 4), **perspective** (Ch 5). Outer layers that *activate* the inner ones: **space** (Ch 6), **relationships** (Ch 7), **culture** (Ch 8). Culture filters down through all of them and is the most powerful.

**Sensory shifters (Ch 3)** — Prefer sensation when you're depleted, distracted, or stressed, because it moves emotion *before* cognitive control engages and therefore survives conditions that kill effortful tools. The governing constraint is the **law of least work**: humans are cognitive misers, so the tool you'll actually use beats the tool that scores better in a lab. (Journaling was among the most effective *and* most rarely used strategies in Kross's COVID study.) Pick your default with two questions — which sense packs the biggest punch, and which has the lowest cost — then pre-load it. "When it comes to shifting your emotions, ease is power."

**The myth of universal approach (Ch 4)** — Reject the rule that hard feelings must always be confronted. Chronic avoidance is damaging *and so is chronic approach*. Bonanno's post-9/11 study found the best predictor of resilience was the ability to do **both** express and suppress well — flexibility, not approach. Decide with the working question: ***"Is what I'm doing working? Is it making me feel better about the problem in front of me?"*** If yes, don't overthink it; if no, switch poles before switching categories. Distraction's mechanism is **time** — the psychological immune system needs runway to flatten the peak.

**Distanced self-talk (Ch 5)** — Kross's stated go-to. Silently address yourself as **"you," by name, or in third person**. It exploits **Solomon's paradox** (we advise others wisely and ourselves poorly) by casting you as someone else — the gap between "I am stressed out" and "You are stressed out" is the gap between panic and compassion. Reduces negative emotion within seconds *without* activating the effort waveform, which matters because stress produces a prefrontal "brownout" that makes ordinary reframing fail.

**The reframing paradox (Ch 5)** — Reframing (a.k.a. reappraisal, reconstrual, cognitive change; the ABCs: **A**dverse event → **B**elief → **C**onsequence) is the foundation of CBT and it routinely fails under distress. Worriers *try* and neurally fail — more effort, less benefit. So: **gain distance first, then reframe.** Use distanced self-talk, mental time travel (backward for evidence you've survived, forward for impermanence), or a second language.

**Situation modification (Ch 6)** — Prefer editing the environment to exercising willpower. Remove what drives the unwanted emotion (box up the pizza; phone under the pillow) and add what cultivates the wanted one. Kross's argument to skeptical students: the idea that self-control must be internal and hard is a myth — controlling your environment first *is* self-control, and Duckworth's data show situation-modifiers beat willpower-instructed students. Note the asymmetry on adding: loved ones' photos were **restorative, not protective**.

**Empathize then zoom out (Ch 7)** — Support requires two things and people balance them badly. Start with listening, empathizing, validating, normalizing; then add **cognitive support** — helping them see it differently. Order matters, and the **more intense the emotion, the longer before they're receptive**. Ask permission to pivot: *"I have a thought on this, are you okay to hear it?"* Then build an **emotional advisory board**: list who you go to for personal and work problems, and circle only those who do *both*. Not everybody can be everything to us.

**The social comparison crib sheet (Ch 7)** — You can't stop comparing; control the frame. Upward → motivation ("if they can, I can") or envy. Downward → gratitude ("things could be much worse") or fear. Then manage frequency, and treat any comparison as one context-specific data point.

**The cultural blueprint (Ch 8)** — Decompose any group into **beliefs/values → norms → practices**. Use it to find the cultures supporting you, diagnose the ones that aren't (the gap is almost always at *practices* — stated values with nothing to operationalize them), or change them: check the foundation and make values concrete; audit norms against those values; build practices so people can *train* for new norms. Never issue a value as an instruction ("be vulnerable"). If a culture forecloses the emotional life you need, changing it and leaving it are both legitimate.

**WOOP (Ch 9)** — The bridge from knowing to doing, merging Oettingen's mental contrasting with Gollwitzer's implementation intentions. **W**ish → **O**utcome → **O**bstacle (**within yourself**) → **P**lan (IF *obstacle*, THEN *specific action*). Both halves are mandatory: indulging in the vision alone *lowers* attainment (you get a taste of achievement and stop working), and dwelling on obstacles alone paralyzes. Give high-stakes "ifs" three "thens," as the SEALs do. Pre-select which shifter the "then" invokes, and give it a duration.

**No one-size-fits-all** — Stated in the introduction and again in the conclusion. In Kross's COVID coping study, tool effectiveness varied wildly across people and day to day. The prescription is two-step: experiment to find your combination, then use WOOP to make deployment automatic. The goal is never to eliminate emotional pain — it's information you need — but to be a **NASCAR pit crew** for shifting: smoother, more intentional, faster.
