# Patterns — Emotion-Regulation Techniques from *Shift*

## Stop the Video
**When to use**: An intrusive image or catastrophic scenario starts looping (Luisa's what-ifs about her daughter).
**How**: Treat the scenario as a tape playing. Either hit stop — redirect attention to something small and available (a book, a magazine, a task, a person who needs you) — or **re-shoot the scene**: same setting, benign outcome (Ella at the same party eating a hot dog and leaping into the pool).
**Trade-offs**: Cheap and fast. Doesn't resolve the underlying fear, so if the image keeps returning (Warning Sign 1), the loop is beating the tool and you need approach or a different category.

## Sensory Shifter Audit
**When to use**: Once, proactively, to establish your default low-cost tool before you need it.
**How**: Answer two questions. (1) Which one or two of the five senses pack the biggest punch for me? (2) Which comes with the lowest cost? Your go-to is the intersection. Then **pre-load it** — mood-specific playlists already on the phone, one tap away.
**Trade-offs**: The highest-punch channel is often high-cost (taste, sex). Naming the cost is what stops a shifter from becoming a crutch.

## Sensory Bundle
**When to use**: A single sensory channel isn't moving a big, deep, sharp emotion.
**How**: Choose an activity that braids several channels — baking bread (touch, smell, taste, sight), cooking, gardening, nature immersion, surfing. Multiple pathways activate in parallel.
**Trade-offs**: Higher time cost than a song, and often requires setup. Pays back in depth of shift; Chayce Baldwin feels grounded within seconds of hands in dough.

## Deliberate Difficult-Emotion Exposure
**When to use**: You want to process a hard emotion but on your own schedule rather than being ambushed by it.
**How**: Use sensation to open the door — the sad song you cry along to alone, the horror movie that supplies fear at no risk.
**Trade-offs**: Research here remains ongoing. Plausible benefit is feeling less ambushed later; the risk is drifting into passive emotional congruency.

## The Working Question (Approach vs. Avoid)
**When to use**: Any time you're unsure whether to confront a feeling or turn away.
**How**: Ask ***"Is what I'm doing working? Is it making me feel better about the problem in front of me?"*** If yes, don't overthink it — keep going. If no, switch poles (approach ⇄ avoid) before switching tool categories. Note that approach can hurt now and still be working.
**Trade-offs**: Requires honest self-report, which distress degrades. Use the warning signs below as external checks.

## Flexible Avoidance
**When to use**: A distressing situation you can't yet act on productively, or a recurring high-pressure role that needs counterbalance (Rodman's excursions, Bubby's selective disclosure).
**How**: Deliberately disengage — slam the laptop instead of replying, walk out of the escalating fight, let friends drag you out dancing — and give it enough runway for the psychological immune system's key ingredient, **time**, to flatten the peak. Then pivot back to expression when it's useful.
**Trade-offs**: Chronic avoidance is genuinely damaging (linked to anxiety, depression, more problems over a decade). What makes it safe is the pivot. If you can't pivot, it isn't flexible avoidance.

## Distanced Self-Talk
**When to use**: Heat of the moment, before or after high-stakes events, or any negative loop. The author's stated default.
**How**: Silently address yourself as **"you," by your own name, or in third person**. "Okay, Jerry. You've got to get going. You need to start acting." / "Come on, Ethan, you can finish writing this chapter."
**Trade-offs**: Reduces negative emotion within seconds, and — uniquely — without activating the brain waveform that tracks effort, so it survives the stress-induced prefrontal brownout that kills ordinary reframing. Feels awkward at first. Not a substitute for action in situations requiring one.

## Mental Time Travel
**When to use**: You're stuck in the present intensity of something and can't look away.
**How**: **Backward** — What similar things have I gone through? How did I get through them? What did I gain that I can use now? **Forward** — How will I feel about this in a week? A month? A year? At the end of my life, what will this mean? Give it a duration; the grief example uses ten seconds.
**Trade-offs**: Directly contradicts present-moment dogma, deliberately. Fails as a first move in acute crisis where action is required — Jerry needed the pronoun switch first.

## Language Switch
**When to use**: Emotionally loaded reasoning, if you're bilingual.
**How**: Think or talk to yourself in your second language, where words carry less emotional charge and reasoning is measurably more objective.
**Trade-offs**: Only available to multilinguals. The transferable lesson is that small linguistic shifts reroute emotional relationships — which is what distanced self-talk exploits.

## Switch Your Space
**When to use**: Internal shifters aren't moving, and the assumptions of your current environment have become invisible (Laurie Santos in New Haven, "like horse blinders").
**How**: Go somewhere that resonates — not merely away. Aim for the right *effect*. Check hyper-local options first: a reading nook, a backyard tree, your own bedroom. Sean's version was five days in his childhood home; Laurie's was a sublet in Cambridge.
**Trade-offs**: The full version (sabbatical, relocation) is neither easy nor cheap. Hometowns can also trigger regression, and for people in recovery, old places carry the wrong associations.

## Situation Modification — Remove
**When to use**: Something in your environment is reliably producing an emotion you don't want, or setting up a goal failure whose emotional aftermath will be worse than the temptation.
**How**: Take it out. Box up the leftover pizza and force it on departing guests. Phone under the pillow. TV out of the bedroom. Do it *preemptively*, before the desire-guilt-shame cascade can start.
**Trade-offs**: Half of Kross's students insist this isn't self-control. He argues that's a narrow and incorrect view — self-control can be easy, and controlling the environment first is a legitimate and superior route. Duckworth's data: situation-modifiers beat willpower-instructed students.

## Situation Modification — Add
**When to use**: Recovering from an emotional hit, not preventing one.
**How**: Put photos of loved ones in the spaces where you work and live. Look at one after a painful memory surfaces.
**Trade-offs**: Sharply asymmetric. In Kross's study the **buffering effect was nonexistent** and the **restorative effect substantial** — so this is a repair tool, not armor.

## The Spaces Audit
**When to use**: Once per major space you occupy routinely; the cumulative environments matter most.
**How**: Look around and answer four questions. What can I do today to curate this space? What can I remove to turn the volume *down* on stress? What can I add to turn it *up* on calm or joy? What local emotional oases exist, and how do I fold pit stops into my week?
**Trade-offs**: Low cost, easy to defer indefinitely. Pair with WOOP if it keeps not happening.

## Empathize-Then-Zoom-Out (as adviser)
**When to use**: Someone comes to you in distress.
**How**: Open with listening, empathizing, validating, normalizing — *Tell me more about that. That sounds hard. I'm not surprised you feel this way.* Watch for signs you're absorbing and amplifying their state. When an opening appears, gently broaden. If unsure, ask outright: ***"I have a thought on this, are you okay to hear it?"*** Accept "no."
**Trade-offs**: The more intense the emotion, the longer before they're receptive — rushing produces resistance. Validating only is the more common failure, but advice-first is the more damaging one to the relationship.

## Redirect Your Own Support Conversation
**When to use**: You're the one hurting and the conversation is sliding into co-rumination.
**How**: Ask what they would do in your situation, or what it looks like from the outside. Or front-load with an advice-giver: "Can I just vent about this for a bit, and then maybe you have some ideas?" Or educate a co-ruminator: "I came across this thing about how venting doesn't help long term. I had no idea!"
**Trade-offs**: Requires enough presence of mind to notice mid-conversation. Disclosure is neurologically rewarding, so the pull to keep venting is real.

## Emotional Advisory Board
**When to use**: Once, as an inventory; revisit as your network changes.
**How**: Two columns, personal and work. List everyone you go to about problems. Assess each on venting vs. zooming out. **Circle only those who do both.** Those are your emotional advisers.
**Trade-offs**: Not a purge. "Not everybody can be everything to us" — you can let people fully into your life without recruiting them to help you shift.

## Reframe the Comparison
**When to use**: You catch yourself comparing — which you will, since the reflex is universal from preschool on.
**How**: Don't fight the comparison; set the frame. **Upward** → "If they can achieve that, so can I" (motivation), not "look what they have" (envy). **Downward** → "Things could be much worse; I'm doing great" (gratitude), not "that could happen to me" (fear). Also manage **frequency** — reserve comparison for when you need a perspective assist — and remember it's **one data point**, context-specific.
**Trade-offs**: Incessant upward comparison undermines well-being regardless of framing; at that point pull attention away entirely. Castigating yourself for comparing just stacks negative emotion.

## Prosocial Action as a Lift
**When to use**: You need a mood shift and have no tool at hand.
**How**: Look around for who's nearby and do something small. Take lunch-making off a stressed partner's plate. Mow the reclusive neighbor's lawn. Spend a small amount on someone else rather than yourself.
**Trade-offs**: As effective as mindfulness and gratitude interventions in meta-analysis, across age, sex, and social anxiety, in rich and poor populations. Requires enough capacity to look outward, which acute distress reduces.

## Build a Ritual
**When to use**: A recurring situation where you feel out of control, or a predictable emotional spike (bedtime anxiety, pre-performance nerves).
**How**: Fix a meaning-infused sequence you perform identically each time — candle, poem, journaling, meditation, LeBron's chalk toss. Stack levers where possible: a pre-dinner dance party (sensory + attention), a headphone-free morning walk. Do it with others for solidarity and social glue.
**Trade-offs**: The control is compensatory, not literal — it doesn't change the situation. That's the point, and also the limit.

## The Cultural Blueprint (diagnose or change)
**When to use**: A group in your life is helping or hurting your emotional life and you can't tell why.
**How**: Decompose into **beliefs/values → norms → practices**. To diagnose: which layer is missing? (Usually practices.) To change: **Rule 1** check the foundation and make values concrete and specific ("build with heart," "stand for safety" — with the specifics spelled out). **Rule 2** run a norms audit against those values; norm change is easier than expected because people want to belong. **Rule 3** customize practices so people can *train* for the new norms.
**Trade-offs**: Slower than any individual tool. If the culture forecloses the emotional life you need and won't change, leaving is the legitimate alternative.

## WOOP
**When to use**: Any recurring emotional pattern you can see coming; the bridge from knowing to doing.
**How**: **Wish** (challenging but feasible) → **Outcome** (best thing about achieving it, and how it will feel) → **Obstacle** (the main challenge **within yourself**) → **Plan** (IF *obstacle*, THEN *specific action*). Pre-select which shifter the "then" invokes and give it a duration.
**Trade-offs**: Both halves are required — indulging without obstacles lowers attainment, dwelling without vision paralyzes. External obstacles break the tool. Give high-stakes "ifs" three "thens," as the SEALs do.

## The Stacked Response
**When to use**: A live emotional emergency where no single tool is sufficient.
**How**: Run several in sequence and accept partial gains from each. Kross's 5 a.m. school-threat sequence: reframe/zoom out → refocus attention into work → resist mass venting while seeking one informed perspective → care for others → sensory break and change of space when chatter sets in.
**Trade-offs**: The target is "not overtaken," never "not feeling." Fear in a threatening situation is functional and should remain.
