# Chapter 6: Hidden in Plain Sight — Space Shifters

## Core Idea
Physical space constantly operates your internal shifters — sensation, attention, perspective — whether or not you notice, and you have two deliberate moves available: **switch** your space or **modify** it. Managing emotions can begin *externally*, and editing your environment counts as self-control.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The two space moves: Switch or Modify.**
  - **Switch** — go somewhere whose sensory and social information differs. "When we change spaces, we change the information that's coming in." Your sensory experience shifts, your attention shifts, and your perspective can shift with them.
  - **Modify** — change the space you're in.
  - Both are, in Kross's judgment, "hugely underappreciated."

- **Place attachment**: emotional resonance with a specific place — not a property of the place, but of your relationship to it.
  - When to use: you need a reset your internal tools can't produce.
  - How: identify places that evoke contentment, well-being, and meaning. A secluded Maine beach, the Sleeping Bear dunes, an LA coffee shop overlooking the water. Crucially, **hyper-local versions almost always exist** — a reading nook in the attic, the oak in the backyard, a kid retreating to their room. What matters is the restorative effect, not the distance.
  - Caution: attachments cut both ways. Hometowns can trigger regression; for people recovering from substance abuse, old bars and friends' houses carry the wrong associations.
  - Downstream effect: people with nature-based special places act more pro-environmentally — picking up trash, advocating conservation.

- **Situation modification**: directly manipulating your surroundings so unwanted emotions never fire.
  - Two operations: **remove** something driving unwanted emotion, or **add** something cultivating a wanted one.
  - Evidence: Angela Duckworth's naturalistic work with high school students — when asked how to prioritize studying over texting, one wrote "I would shut off my phone and put it under my pillow so I wouldn't be tempted to touch it." When students actually used situation modification in their own lives, they studied more effectively over a week than students told to use **willpower** or given no instruction.
  - The **adding** side: in a study Kross helped design, participants recalled a deeply upsetting experience (breakup, failure, rejection, betrayal, humiliation); half saw a photo of their mother *before*, half *after*. The **buffering effect was nonexistent; the restorative effect was substantial.** In a partner version, those shown a photo before free-writing engaged in far less negative thinking. Kross responded by filling his office with framed photos of family and friends.

- **The spaces audit** (do it in the room you're in):
  1. What can you do — right now, today — to curate this space to shape your emotional experience?
  2. What can you **remove** to turn the volume *down* on stress or anxiety?
  3. What can you **add** to turn the volume *up* on calm or joy?
  4. What local, easy-to-access places are your **emotional oases**, and how do you fold pit stops into your weekly or daily routine?

## Key Concepts
- **Indirect pathway** — how the larger physical environment shapes the rhythms and parameters of daily life, and thereby emotion.
- **Direct pathway** — the immediate sensory experience of your surroundings.
- **Place attachment** — a personal emotional bond to a specific place; a "happy place" with a research literature.
- **Emotional oases** — accessible restorative places; think of yourself as a marathon runner and these as refilling stations along the route.
- **Situation modification** — deliberately altering your environment to change future emotional states.
- **Green space** (Laurie Santos's film-class sense) — the location of epiphany you decamp to for conceptual distance; it doesn't have to be green, only significant to you and restorative.
- **Propinquity effect** — proximity as one of the most influential determinants of whom you marry and befriend; a reminder that incidental features of environment shape the most important parts of a life.
- **Functional interdependence** (Thomas Talhelm) — a condition where people must cooperate to succeed, as in rice farming.

## Mental Models
- **Think of self-control as something you can do to a room.** Kross puts it to students: if I remove something from my space so I never face the temptation, is that self-control? Half say no. He argues that's a narrow and incorrect view — the pervasive myth is that self-control is an *internal* force that has to be hard. Self-control can be easy, and one way to make it easy is to control the environment before you ever have to activate anything inside you.
- **Use the Adam and Eve test.** The most famous emotion-regulation story ever told is about a forbidden fruit and a failure to resist it. You can keep fighting temptation with the tools you have — or you can remove the fruit.
- **Think of goal failure as an emotional event, not just a productivity one.** Missed goals produce frustration, self-directed anger, shame, fear about the future, and sadness. Editing the space short-circuits that whole cascade before it starts.
- **Route matters, not just destination.** Kross met his wife by falling into step behind her on a walk to class — the propinquity effect running in the background of a life.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating environmental tweaks as minor.** "But they are not minor. They can be huge." A TV in the bedroom against a sleep goal; basement clutter that eats twenty minutes off a forty-five-minute workout.
- **Relying on willpower where a physical change would do.** Duckworth's willpower-instructed students underperformed the situation-modifiers.
- **Assuming you must move far.** You may not need to leave your neighborhood, or your house.
- **Using photos as armor rather than repair.** The buffering effect didn't exist; deploy the photo *after* the hit, not as prophylaxis.
- **Confusing escape with effect.** Laurie's own correction: "It wasn't just to get away. It wasn't to avoid. It was to get… the *right* effect."

## Worked Example
**Laurie Santos.** Yale psychologist, head of Silliman College (living among students in a mansion-cum-dorm), running a lab, teaching a full slate — then Psychology and the Good Life draws over 900 signups on day one and eventually a quarter of the Yale student body, the *Times* covers it, and she's lecturing with two mics clipped to her sweater and two battery packs on her boots, tracking her outfits in an Excel spreadsheet, flying out at 4 a.m. to give a talk and back by dinner. *The Happiness Lab* launches. She is falling asleep at her desk, spending holidays with journal articles, and turning down the Vatican.

Alone in a soundproof podcast booth she realizes she's mispronounced a guest's name throughout the recording, screams at the top of her lungs, and pounds her laptop hard enough to break the keyboard. She hires a therapist, twice weekly. She starts saying no. She tries to apply her own material — take time for yourself, find the silver lining, keep a journal — **and cannot make herself do any of it.** Colleagues remind her of lessons she had just recorded, which produces only more frustration and self-criticism. The world's foremost happiness expert, with every resource, cannot use them. The breaking point is a student asking for help with a dental emergency and her first reaction being *Great, one more thing I have to do* — followed by shock at herself.

So she leaves. Unpaid sabbatical, unheard of for a head of college. She and her husband could go anywhere — Buenos Aires, Mexico City — but she remembers a college film class on **remarriage comedies**, where a character departs the usual setting for a rural "green space" to gain the conceptual distance to see what they actually want; the professor noted these spaces don't have to be green, only significant. Hers is Cambridge, Massachusetts, where she spent her young adulthood and met her husband. Geographically a stone's throw from New Haven; emotionally a universe away.

The workload barely shrinks — podcast, interviews, talks continue. What changes is the input. She wakes in a tiny third-floor sublet at the level of the treetops, feeling safe in a bird's nest; the smells and sounds of the city return her to a time when the future was full of possibility — and she realizes it still is. New Haven, she says, had been suffused with assumptions about what was important and how things had to be, impossible to shake off, "like horse blinders." Within a week: maybe she doesn't want to be head of college anymore. Maybe she doesn't have to be. She steps back, later returns to Yale.

**The cheap version.** Sean, a 29-year-old army commander, returns from ten months in Iraq to find his wife silently handing him their two small children on the tarmac and walking away — she has left him for another soldier on the base. Ten days until redeployment, no childcare, a dead car, his clothes thrown out, dark thoughts arriving. His mother calls: "Sean, pack up the kids, get on a plane. Come home." He flies to Casper, Wyoming, walks into his childhood home, is hit by nostalgia and safety at once, crawls into his old twin bed and sleeps **more than fifteen hours**. He wakes among outdated movie posters and Little League trophies feeling safe for the first time in memory. The problems are unchanged — but visible from a different angle, including the mercy in a clean break. Five days in a resonant place, twenty years later still a turning point.

**And the trivial version, same mechanism.** After a football party, Kross faces two leftover pizzas and a doctor's recent advice. He boxes them into doggie bags and forces them on departing friends over their protests. The next day: no perseveration between a slice and a salad, no late-night trip to the kitchen. He *preemptively foreclosed* on desire, guilt, and shame — an emotional intervention performed entirely with cardboard.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the spaces audit today in the room you're in — remove one thing, add one thing.
2. Name your emotional oases and schedule pit stops rather than waiting for crisis.
3. Modify before you rely on willpower; the environment is the cheaper lever.
4. Use loved ones' photos to *recover* from emotional hits, not to prevent them.
5. When switching space, aim for the right effect, not merely away — pick a place that resonates.
6. Look local first: attic nook, backyard tree, own bedroom.
7. Treat your spaces as a cumulative force. It's the rooms you're in *routinely* that matter most.
