# Chapter 9: From Knowing to Doing — Making Shifting Automatic

## Core Idea
Knowing the tools doesn't produce using them, because the law of least work punishes deliberation exactly when you're flooded. **WOOP** — Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan — pre-wires the choice so shifting becomes automatic, like buckling a seat belt.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **WOOP** (Gabriele Oettingen's mental contrasting + Peter Gollwitzer's implementation intentions, merged around 2010 by the researchers, who are married to each other).
  - **W = Wish** — what specifically you desire; challenging but feasible. *I want to be more patient with my children when they irritate me.*
  - **O = Outcome** — the best thing about achieving it and how it will feel. *I'll have a better relationship with them and be a better father.*
  - **O = Obstacle** — the main challenge **within yourself**. *When they call each other stupid I lose my temper — I grew up where people put each other down and I'm really reactive to that.*
  - **P = Plan** — an if-then binding the obstacle to an action. *IF they are fighting, THEN I'm going to zoom out, remind myself they're kids, that my wife and I acted similarly, that their brains are still developing — and get their attention without yelling.*
  - When to use: any emotional pattern you can see coming, from daily irritation to profound grief.
  - Why it works: WOO energizes the goal and names the specific blocker; P fuses each obstacle (the "if") to a specific action (the "then"), removing deliberation. Implementation intentions work on two levels — they connect goal to method, and they **bookmark the situation with context cues** so you remember what to do when it arrives.
  - Evidence: two decades of studies show gains in studying and grades, working through negative feelings, eating and exercise, self-care in depression, and relationship thriving. German researchers taught WOOP to **first graders**, walking them through it five times; the six-year-olds managed emotions better and earned better grades — with effects still detectable **three years later**.

- **Mental contrasting** (Oettingen's discovery, and the surprise inside it).
  - She expected fantasizing about desired futures to predict success. It predicted the *opposite*: cuing people to fantasize **decreased** goal attainment, because imagining the future delivered a satisfying taste of having already achieved it, which undermined effort.
  - **Dwelling** — fixating on obstacles so completely you can't see past them — was no better alone.
  - The finding: indulging and dwelling are each bad in isolation and **great linked together**. Vividly picture the future you want *and* fully visualize the problems en route, and outcomes rise sharply on both goal accomplishment and emotional wellness.

- **Implementation intentions** (Gollwitzer): if-then plans that make follow-through less effortful.
  - *IF the alarm goes off and it's 6:00 a.m., THEN I'll grab my duffel bag and go to the gym.*
  - *IF I get hungry before lunch, THEN I will have one of the apples on the counter.*
  - *IF I feel anxious about the week on Sunday night, THEN I will make a to-do list, put it away until Monday morning, and listen to a feel-good song.*
  - *IF I get overwhelmed and angry during the meeting again, THEN I will pause and think about the fact that we're all on the same team before I decide what to say.*
  - Model: the SEAL mission brief. For every "if," **three "thens."**

- **No silver bullets — the idiosyncrasy principle.** In Kross's 2020 COVID coping study, there was *wild* diversity in which tools helped: some people used more than a dozen in a day, others none; what worked for one did nothing for the next; and for many, what helped varied day to day. The prescription is therefore two-step: **experiment to find your tools, then use WOOP to raise the odds you'll deploy them under pressure.**

## Key Concepts
- **WOOP** — Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan.
- **Mental contrasting** — pairing a vivid desired future with the crucial internal obstacle.
- **Indulging** — fantasizing about the outcome without the obstacle; actively counterproductive.
- **Dwelling** — fixating on obstacles without the vision; equally counterproductive.
- **Implementation intention** — a rehearsed if-then link between a specific situation and a specific action.
- **The obstacle must be internal** — WOOP's third step asks for the challenge *within yourself*, not external circumstances.
- **Law of least effort** — the same principle from Ch 3, reappearing as the reason knowing fails to become doing.

## Mental Models
- **Be a NASCAR pit crew for your own emotions.** The SEALs found it took ten minutes to change a flat tire on the battlefield — perilously long to be stationary and exposed — so they spent two days with a NASCAR pit crew practicing until their fingers went numb, and got it under two minutes. That is the target for shifting: smoother, more intentional, faster.
- **Think of the goal as seat-belt-level automaticity.** Not thinking about what to do when the situation presents itself. Just doing it.
- **Plan for the contingencies you can, and trust the toolkit for the ones you can't.** You can't rehearse everything; what training buys is that when the unrehearsed thing happens, you reach automatically for tools used in similar circumstances.

## Anti-patterns
- **Visualizing success alone.** Positive-outcome visualization is well documented as advice and, per Oettingen, predicts *worse* attainment — it substitutes the feeling of achievement for the work.
- **Only seeing obstacles.** Dwelling leaves no forward path and no energy to reach it.
- **Naming an external obstacle in WOOP.** The step specifies the challenge within yourself; an external one gives you nothing to attach a "then" to.
- **Waiting until you're flooded to select a strategy.** "It can be hard to think about what strategy to use in a moment when you're bathed in emotion." That's the whole problem WOOP exists to solve.
- **Hunting for the universal method.** "Dogmatically searching for [one-size-fits-all solutions] is likely to be as successful as finding a single diet or exercise routine that works for everyone."

## Worked Example
**3:00 a.m. outside Baghdad.** Matt Maasdam and ten SEALs approach a bomb maker's house. They know almost nothing — how armed he is, how many civilians sleep inside — but they know bomb makers are booby-trap experts who bury explosives along the perimeter where soldiers line up, that their homes are guarded from second-story positions, and that they may find insurgents in suicide vests, trip wires, or grenades in pockets.

In the preceding days the team walked through **more than a dozen** scenarios: *If* the helicopter loses power… *If* the team takes fire en route… *If* there are five times more insurgents than anticipated… *If* someone steps on a land mine on the way out. Mission leaders drilled them Socratically — "Matt, if _____ happens, then what's your next move?" — with three "thens" per "if."

So they don't line up along the perimeter. They scatter across the front yard, planning to move on the door charge like a basketball team running a fast break: half through the door, half flooding the other openings.

Then the contingency they *hadn't* planned: the exterior walls are mostly glass. The charge blows and the walls shatter, raining giant shards from twenty feet up; from the street the house appears to explode. Nobody is hurt. Training had covered how different materials collapse, so they aren't overwhelmed — they "turned steel," letting body armor take the impact while they crouched and covered, then continued. The blast woke the neighborhood and raised the urgency, and they went in, took the bomb maker, and got out.

**The same structure applied to grief.** A friend of a friend lost a younger brother to suicide. Months later he's functioning — working, in therapy, processing — but the sadness ambushes him. Playing monster-tag with his young kids at the playground, the loss hits and he has to walk away. He doesn't want to run from the grief; he does want to mourn. He also doesn't want to miss his children's childhood doing it.

- **Wish:** "To be able to hang out with my kids and enjoy my life, even during this era of intense grief."
- **Outcome:** "When my kids are grown up, they'll still look back on this time and remember a lot of joy."
- **Obstacle:** "When the grief hits, I can't see my way out of it. I completely lose perspective."
- **Plan:** "IF I'm with the kids and I start to feel it, THEN I'm going to stop and focus on the future. I'm going to take ten seconds and imagine them as grown-ups, and me looking back on the whole sweep of my life. I'm going to remind myself that in the grand scheme of things this is just one short season."

Note the "then" is a Ch 5 perspective shifter — mental time travel with a **ten-second** duration — pre-selected while calm.

## Key Takeaways
1. Write the plan before the crisis; deliberation is unavailable when you're flooded.
2. Always pair the vision with the obstacle. Either alone hurts you.
3. Make the obstacle internal and specific enough to attach an action to.
4. Give high-stakes "ifs" more than one "then."
5. Pre-select which shifter the "then" invokes, and give it a duration ("take ten seconds…").
6. Expect the unrehearsed contingency; the point of rehearsal is reflexive reach, not exhaustive coverage.
7. Experiment first to find your personal tools, then use WOOP to make deployment automatic — the sequence matters.
8. Accept that some emotional experiences stay hard. "If emotions were a cinch to rein in, they wouldn't be useful, because we'd never permit ourselves to feel bad."
