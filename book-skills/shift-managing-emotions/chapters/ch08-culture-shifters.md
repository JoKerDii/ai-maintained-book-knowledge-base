# Chapter 8: The Master Switch — Culture Shifters

## Core Idea
Culture is the outermost and most powerful shifter — it filters down through relationships, spaces, and your internal shifters, setting even your philosophy about whether emotions should be felt or suppressed. Break any culture into **beliefs and values, norms, and practices**, and the same three components let you diagnose it, lean on it, or change it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The cultural blueprint: beliefs/values → norms → practices.**
  - **Beliefs and values** — what the people in a group care about. Kross's Brooklyn: *we don't back down, we fight back* (his parents' instruction after Big Rich punched him in preschool: "Next time you see him, you punch him back!"). The Utku Inuit, studied by anthropologist Jean Briggs over eighteen months from 1970: *never fight back*; anger expression was taboo, since a culture built for survival in close quarters in an extreme climate valued social harmony above everything — even the community's last canoe.
  - **Norms** — the spoken and unspoken rules that promote and maintain those values. Explicit (constitutional law, addressing a judge as "Your Honor") or absorbed implicitly (you just know not to bring Styrofoam to your environmentalist friends' party). Norms bind because we're social: *Breaking the rules = social exclusion = very bad. Following the rules = social inclusion = very good.* Ostracism is among the most painful experiences humans endure — historically life-threatening, and that wiring persists.
  - **Practices** — behaviors, rituals, exercises, and teachings that let the group actualize its beliefs and adopt its norms. In AA, the norm is supporting each other through crisis; the practice is **picking up the phone**.
  - Use it three ways: (1) find which cultures in your life are most supportive; (2) diagnose which are failing — often values sound great but the norms and practices that would make them real are absent; (3) repair or build the missing layer.

- **The three rules of culture change** (from the England Football Association turnaround and parallel cases):
  - **Rule 1 — Check the foundation.** If something in the group bothers you, look at the beliefs and values driving it. Dara Khosrowshahi at Uber in 2017 inherited an FBI investigation, a harassment-riddled workplace the *Times* described as "unrestrained," and a founder's messy ouster; the values behind the meteoric rise had become the cause of the fall. The fix: establish **concrete** values rather than vague ones — "build with heart," "stand for safety" — and spell out on the values page exactly what each means. Statements like these create alignment and make the otherwise unsaid visible.
  - **Rule 2 — Rethink norms.** Google's 2012 team-effectiveness study expected the answer to be *who* was in the group. Wrong. The igniting agent was the **norm** around social interaction: successful teams had norms promoting psychological safety — giving each other time to talk, awareness of others' feelings, sharing difficult moments. Google then pushed embracing disagreement, welcoming "silly" questions, and openness about failure. You don't need Google's budget: run a **norms audit** — identify current norms, cross-check them against stated beliefs and values, and change the unwritten rules that don't serve them. Norm change is easier than expected, because most people want to belong and are already inclined to follow; you mostly have to communicate them.
  - **Rule 3 — Customize your practices.** You can't announce new values and norms and expect adoption — you have to help people **train** for it. Bespoke by group; ask what would help people cope with their emotions inside this culture while staying aligned with its values.

- **Religion as the recipe, and rituals as the portable version.** 84%+ of people belong to religious groups; religious people show better cardiovascular health, less depression and anxiety, and more meaning. The active ingredients: **belief** (emotions are controllable through prayer/meditation, or a higher power will provide) producing awe, connection, and a broadened perspective that reduces self-focus; and **practices** (fasting, cleansing, pilgrimage, holidays) providing control when control is scarce.
  - **Compensatory control** — a **ritual** is a sequence of behaviors infused with meaning, performed the same way each time; the control you exert over the ritual's prescribed steps compensates for a situation that feels out of control. This is likely why people turn to rituals amid war and terminal diagnoses.
  - Build your own: locate where in your life you need help (anxiety spiking at bedtime → candle, poem, journaling). Stack levers where you can — a pre-dinner dance party (sensory + attention), a morning walk without headphones. Do them with others for solidarity; rituals act as social glue. LeBron James's chalk toss before games distracts, confers control, connects him to the crowd, and preps him for what's next.

- **The exit decision.** If a culture prevents the emotional life you need, you have two options: **move away from it** (people quit jobs, change careers, leave relationships every day — not pleasant, sometimes best) or **change it** using the three rules.

## Key Concepts
- **Culture** — the embodiment of ideas and practices passed down over time through pathways like families and institutions, giving you the best chance at thriving in your particular context. Both large (a nation) and small (a friend group).
- **Master switch** — Kross's metaphor: like a smart-home panel, one press governs temperature, lights, electronics, blinds. Culture governs what you think, how you act, and how you manage emotions.
- **Nesting dolls** — the structure of the whole book: sensation/attention/perspective innermost, then spaces, then relationships, then culture filtering down through all of it.
- **Norms** — spoken and unspoken rules maintaining a culture's values; "a little like a sheepdog herding everyone in the same direction."
- **Practices** — rituals, exercises, teachings that operationalize norms.
- **Ritual** — a fixed, meaning-infused sequence of behaviors.
- **Compensatory control** — control gained from a ritual offsetting the felt loss of control elsewhere.
- **Collectivist vs. individualistic cultures** — in collectivist cultures (e.g., Japan) where social harmony is paramount, people suppress more and view suppression positively; in individualistic ones (e.g., European Americans), expression is valued. The "suppression is unhealthy" verdict is culturally local, not universal.
- **Norms audit** — cataloguing current norms and testing them against stated values.

## Mental Models
- **Think of culture as the air you breathe.** Its invisibility is exactly why culture clashes are hard — nobody can see why either side thinks, feels, or behaves as it does.
- **Use "respond, don't react" as the culture-level distinction.** Hollis's AA sponsor's line: a *response* is intentional and constructive; a *reaction* is knee-jerk and emotionally driven, and for her historically led to the bottom of a bottle.
- **Think of a group whose values you love but whose norms are absent as brittle.** College friends who value being there for each other but have no norm of monthly contact and no practice of rotating who organizes — the culture will crack under load.
- **Practice the tool before you need it.** AA's "one-thousand-pound phone": elders suggest getting three numbers at every meeting and *practicing calling people* while you're fine, because you follow through in crisis only if you rehearsed out of crisis.

## Anti-patterns
- **Stating values without building norms and practices.** The most common cultural failure mode: the words sound great, nothing supports them.
- **"Be vulnerable" as an instruction.** Pippa Grange's insight — you must create a psychologically safe space, model the behavior, and install practices that build the ancillary skills. Announcing the value does nothing.
- **Staying in a culture that forecloses the emotional life you need**, on the assumption that culture is weather rather than a choice.
- **Assuming culture change must be solemn.** "Changing culture for better emotion regulation doesn't have to be boring. In fact, you can make it fun."

## Worked Example
**Hollis, five years sober, laid off before 9:30 a.m.** She arrives with a mocha and a laptop bag; she leaves with a box. Her inner critic is already producing zingers: *Of course I got fired. Who did I think I was, pretending I deserved a job like that?* In the kitchen she sees mortgage and utility bills on a house she bought last month — unimaginable a few years ago when she was in and out of the hospital and drinking herself into oblivion. Panic locks her up; she sits on the floor and imagines being homeless. The thought of telling anyone makes her want to crawl into the bathtub with a joint and a bottle of bourbon.

Then a cultural artifact surfaces: ***Respond, don't react*** — her sponsor Ray's line. She knows what a response looks like, because she'd done it when a friend died by suicide: called Ray, then Genelle, Arthur, Susan, Carla, Franny, Tyler, until John picked up. The first ten numbers in her contacts are AA people, each name followed by a little "a" so they cluster together.

She scrolls and dials. Four answer; she talks from the floor into the afternoon, and each supplies a different mechanism from the same culture. Ray helps her plan which meeting to attend that evening (practice). Genelle recites the Serenity Prayer and extracts a promise of ten minutes of meditation (ritual). Tyler offers "I came for my drinking, but stayed for my thinking," refocusing her attention (reframe). Carla names the two steps she needs — letting God take the wheel, relinquishing control — and when Hollis projects herself into a tent under an underpass, asks: ***"Where are your feet?"*** Hollis rolls her eyes, groans, and takes the point. They aren't there yet; don't borrow trouble.

That day she goes to a meeting, eats bad takeout, weeps with her cat, and doesn't drink. Then the next day, and the next. She keeps the house and the sobriety, and finds another job. Her credit goes to the culture: "It saved my life."

**Why it worked, decomposed.** AA **values** personal accountability and acceptance. Its **norms** — internally called "suggestions" — include staying out of romantic relationships early in recovery and treating others with patience. Its **practices** include pairing newcomers with sponsors, taking inventory of resentments that could derail recovery, and veterans handing out guidance, phone numbers, and hugs under the tenet "let us love you until you can love yourself." What makes AA effective isn't unique to AA — it's that it pulls **all three levers at once**.

**The change case.** In 2017 England's Football Association, after repeated tournament failures, hired psychologist Pippa Grange to build "a high-performance culture and psychological resilience across all men's and women's national teams." She and the men's coach began with values and norms: privilege trust and mutual understanding, and expect players to share feelings and encourage vulnerability. Then the practices — the part that did the work. Small group conversations she led, where players discussed fears and insecurities instead of maintaining the stiff upper lip; one player went public with severe depression, a risk he likely wouldn't have taken before. Plus deliberate play: non-football games building coordination and trust while adding lightness. After beating Tunisia in the 2018 World Cup, staff filled an indoor pool with inflatable unicorns. England reached the quarterfinals — first time since 1990 — and the *Daily Mail* dubbed Grange the "Mary Poppins of football." Her logic: "If you know people are in the trenches with you then there's a sense of safety in that — a sense of home and belonging."

## Key Takeaways
1. Decompose any group you're in into beliefs/values, norms, and practices; the gap is almost always at the practices layer.
2. Diagnose a struggling culture before leaving it — but recognize leaving as a legitimate move.
3. Make values concrete and specific; vague values create no alignment.
4. Audit norms against stated values; norm change is easier than expected because people want to belong.
5. Build practices that let people *train* for new norms. Never issue the value as an instruction.
6. Manufacture rituals where you need compensatory control, and stack them with sensory or attention levers.
7. Practice reaching for support while you're stable — the phone weighs a thousand pounds in crisis.
8. Remember that your judgment of suppression and expression is itself cultural, not universal.
