# Chapter 4: The Myth of Universal Approach — Attention Shifters

## Core Idea
The instruction to always confront your feelings is a myth; avoidance is a legitimate tool that the psychological immune system is built around. What predicts resilience is not approach but **flexibility** — the ability to toggle between approaching and avoiding as the situation demands.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The myth of universal approach**: the belief that we should always confront, express, and process difficult feelings because avoiding prolongs distress.
  - Provenance: Aristotle first, Freud popularized, now enforced by therapy culture and social media where "compartmentalize," "suppress," and "denial" appear as slurs.
  - Why it persists (legitimately): Foa & Kozak's "Emotional Processing of Fear" plus decades of work show chronic avoidance, denial, and downplaying are strongly linked to anxiety and depression across populations from college students to cardiac patients; a ten-year study found avoidance coping predicted future depression *and* generated more problems over time.
  - Why it's still a myth: those studies average over people who avoid *chronically*. They don't isolate people who avoid **flexibly** — strategically and instrumentally, pivoting to expression when needed. Chronic avoidance is bad; **chronic approach can be problematic too**.

- **Foa & Kozak's mental representation model**: fear occurs when you activate a mental representation — an image, memory, or thought — that you've tagged as threat. The mind loops the tape because a meaning-making system has hit a snag ("If I'm a good person, how could I have hurt my friend?"), and it won't rest until the puzzle resolves. Approaching repeatedly updates the representation; that's the logic of exposure. This is *correct* — just not universal.

- **The self-assessment test — hammer or screwdriver?**: the decision rule for approach vs. avoid.
  - Ask: ***Is what I'm doing working? Is it making me feel better about the problem in front of me?***
  - Avoidance working looks like: you say something regrettable at a party, distract with funny videos, get relief, and it doesn't resurface later.
  - Approach working looks like: you dwell on betraying a friend's confidence, and it resolves into a real insight (the gossip high wasn't worth it). Note that **a strategy doesn't have to feel good to be working** — approach can hurt now and help later.
  - If it's working, don't overthink it. If not, switch poles (approach ⇄ avoid) or switch tool categories entirely.

- **Three warning signs that avoidance isn't working**:
  1. **You try not to think about it and keep thinking about it.** You go to a movie to escape a fight and every plot point returns you to it; you believed you weren't resentful about the infidelity until it barrels into every argument. Chatter is the hallmark.
  2. **Chronic reliance on substances or compulsive behaviors** — drugs, alcohol, compulsive sex, gambling, eating. Powerful relief, temporary, almost always harmful long-term. Bright red flags.
  3. **Constant reassurance-seeking.** *Looks* like approach, functions as avoidance: obsessive medical research, unnecessary tests, repeatedly asking loved ones for opinions, re-checking locks. Luisa scouring the internet was avoiding the real fear by trying to "fix it."

- **Two warning signs that approach isn't working**: **chatter** (a negative thought loop — "stuck in a traffic circle with no exit," focused on the problem without progress), and **dredging up negatives that aren't actually bothering you** (Kross's father repeatedly urging him to "process" a divorce his psychological immune system cleared decades ago). If something doesn't keep cropping up on its own, you probably aren't harboring a wound that will fester.

## Key Concepts
- **Attention** — the mental spotlight determining what enters awareness; usually on autopilot, but consciously steerable. Approach = pointing it at the thing; avoidance = swinging it away.
- **Psychological immune system** — the built-in system that metabolizes psychological threats; its most important component is **time**. Most experiences have an emotional peak that slopes downward; distraction's job is to buy enough time for that decay.
- **Flexibility** — the capacity to deploy attention both toward and away as context demands; Bonanno's best predictor of resilience.
- **Chatter** — getting stuck in a negative thought loop (Kross's prior book); the shared alarm bell for failing approach *and* failing avoidance.
- **Expression / suppression** — the operational forms of approach and avoidance used in Bonanno's task.

## Mental Models
- **Think of avoidance as a hammer, not as malpractice.** A hammer is a bad universal tool and an excellent specific one. The error isn't owning a hammer; it's owning only a hammer.
- **Use time as the active ingredient of distraction.** Slam the laptop instead of firing back the email; walk out of the escalating fight; let friends drag you out dancing after the breakup. You aren't burying the feeling, you're letting the decay curve run.
- **Think of psychology's approach/avoid split as an instance of the human bias to sort into good and bad.** Approach tools got labeled universally good, avoidance universally bad, and the nuance was lost.

## Worked Example
Two figures who "should" have been damaged, weren't, and reveal the same mechanism.

**Dennis Rodman, June 1998.** The Bulls lead the Jazz 2–1 in the Finals. Rodman skips practice and a mandatory press conference to appear at a WCW wrestling match in Detroit next to Hulk Hogan, smoking a cigar and trash-talking. Phil Jackson isn't shocked; he'd already tolerated a 48-hour Vegas "vacation" that became four days. Steve Kerr: to get the most out of him on the court, you had to give him rope, and they gave him a lot of it. Read as emotion regulation rather than misbehavior, Rodman was using distraction and avoidance to counterbalance the pressure of his role. Result: game four, fourteen rebounds in just over twenty-nine minutes; the Bulls take the title, their sixth in eight years.

**Kross's grandmother.** A Holocaust survivor who never discussed the war — except one day a year, at Remembrance Day gatherings, where the sobbing was so raw it disturbed her grandson. He assumed she'd bottled it and would pay. Watching the unabridged Holocaust Museum interview decades later, he sees not a stoic but a woman fully suffused with grief, shot back into 1941. Then his mother supplies the missing data: running into an old friend from the Polish woods at the grocery store, Bubby talked at length on the drive home. She hadn't shut down. She **selectively engaged**. No therapy, no stages of grief, no outward "in touch with her feelings" performance — and a life bookended by happiness and fulfillment.

**The study that explains both.** George Bonanno, living a mile north of ground zero after 9/11, brought 101 NYC undergraduates in, measured baseline distress, then over three months tested something specific: sitting them before a one-way mirror with an unseen judge, he had them **express** or **suppress** emotion on cue while viewing positive and negative images. How accurately the judge read them scored their skill at each. Eighteen months later he re-measured distress. The best-coping students were those who could do **both** well. Push-ups and pull-ups: the ability to switch is the measure. Avoidance is a component of flexibility, and flexibility is the predictor of resilience.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask the working question — "Is what I'm doing making me feel better about this problem?" — before asking whether your strategy is philosophically correct.
2. If it's working, leave it alone. If not, switch poles before switching tools.
3. Treat chatter as the universal alarm: it signals failing approach and failing avoidance alike.
4. Screen your "approach" behaviors for disguised avoidance — reassurance-seeking is the most common counterfeit.
5. Give distraction enough runway for time to do its work; that's the mechanism, not escapism.
6. Don't excavate what isn't surfacing. Absence of recurrence is evidence, not repression.
7. Build the second exercise: if you're a natural approacher, practice suppressing; if a natural avoider, practice expressing. Range is the goal.
