# Chapter 7: Catching a Feeling — Relationship Shifters

## Core Idea
Emotions are permeable: they flow out of you and into you through contagion, through the conversations you have about them, and through the comparisons you can't stop making. Three levers in your relationships deliver outsized returns — **who you catch feelings from**, **how support conversations are structured**, and **how you frame social comparisons**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Emotional contagion**: "catching a feeling," starting with **mimicry**.
  - Mechanism: you unconsciously mirror tone, facial expression, head tilt within seconds; the physical movements you mimic transmit signals to your nervous system that set off a matching emotional cascade — usually with zero awareness.
  - Scope: affects work commitment and satisfaction, empathy, burnout susceptibility, conflict handling, group coordination, and negotiation outcomes.
  - Practical use: treat it as data during exposure decisions. If you tour an office and catch negative feelings from staff, that's information about what you'd be marinating in daily.

- **The empathy-then-perspective order of operations** (the structure of a helpful support conversation).
  - Two needs must both be met: (1) **empathy and validation**, (2) **perspective shift**. Most people badly imbalance this.
  - As **adviser**: start with listening, empathizing, validating, normalizing — *Tell me more about that. That sounds hard. What do you think about that? I'm not surprised you feel this way.* Watch for signs you're veering into co-rumination (you're getting as wound up as they are and amplifying it). When you see an opening, gently zoom out. If they're not ready, return to listening. If unsure, ask outright: ***"I have a thought on this, are you okay to hear it?"*** They may say no — and not every emotional conversation needs to end in a solution. Revisit later.
  - Threshold: **the more intense the emotion, the more time before someone is receptive to a broadened perspective.**
  - As **the person in need**: if the conversation is drifting into co-rumination, take control — ask what they would do in your situation, or what it looks like from the outside.
  - Or **educate them**: to a co-ruminator, "I came across this interesting chapter about how venting doesn't actually help in the long term. I had no idea!" To an advice-giver, front-load it: "Can I just vent about this for a bit, and then maybe you have some ideas for me?"

- **The emotional advisory board exercise**:
  1. Two columns on paper: **personal** and **work**. List everyone you go to about problems in each. Overlap or none, long list or short — fine.
  2. Assess each on the balance of **venting** vs. **zooming out**. Pure venting partners are great for bonding, weak for the actual problem. People who jump straight to advice are hard to talk to (Kross's workshop poll on what you call them: "Jerks!" and, from the back of the room, "Husbands!").
  3. **Circle the people who do both — listen *and* broaden your perspective.** Those are your emotional advisers.
  - Screening questions: Are they good at letting you express, but not for too long? Are they unafraid to give you straight talk? Do they usually raise perspectives you hadn't reached alone?
  - The point isn't to cut anyone. "Not everybody can be everything to us." You can let people fully into your world without recruiting them to help you shift. Businesses vet their advisory boards carefully; so should you.

- **The social comparison crib sheet** (all four cells, because direction alone doesn't determine the feeling):
  | | Framed as threat/deficit | Framed as resource |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Upward** (someone outperforming you) | envy, dejection — focusing on what they have and you don't | motivation — "If they can achieve that, so can I" |
  | **Downward** (someone doing worse) | fear, worry — "If it happens to them, it could happen to me" | gratitude, appreciation, broadened perspective — "Things could be much worse" |
  - Plus **frequency**: incessant upward comparison undermines well-being; that's when to pull attention away and reserve comparison for moments you need a perspective assist.
  - Plus **one data point**: comparisons are context-specific judgments. Others may compare themselves to you in ways that would shock you.

- **Prosocial spending / caring as a shifter**: when you need a lift, look around for who's nearby.
  - Evidence: Liz Dunn's 2008 UBC study handed out envelopes with $5 or $20 after a happiness rating; the personal-spending group had to spend it on themselves by 5 p.m., the prosocial group on someone else. Prosocial spenders got a significant happiness boost **regardless of amount**. Replicated in rich *and* poor populations, and during COVID when personal stakes were elevated. A meta-analysis of 27 experiments and 4,000+ people found kind acts boosted well-being as effectively as mindfulness and gratitude interventions, across age, sex, and social anxiety.
  - Why: caring strengthens social bonds and raises the odds you'll be helped later; kindness confers status; "evolution gifted us a squirt of joy each time we pitch in."
  - Scale it down: take lunch-making off a prickly partner's plate; mow the reclusive neighbor's overgrown lawn. Mood benefit for both parties.

## Key Concepts
- **Emotional contagion** — rapid, largely unconscious transmission of emotion between people, online and off, beginning with mimicry.
- **Co-rumination / venting** — spending too long rehashing a problem together; feels supportive, deepens the groove.
- **Emotional advisers** — the subset of your network who both validate and broaden; the people to call in real distress.
- **Cognitive support** — helping someone work through the situation rather than only commiserating; the missing half of most support.
- **Upward / downward social comparison** — comparing to someone outperforming or underperforming you; a universal reflex present from preschool onward, across income brackets and cultures.
- **Prosocial spending** — spending resources on others; a reliable, cheap emotional lift.
- **Mutualism** — the millennia-old arrangement of benefiting by relying on one another; source of belonging and purpose.

## Mental Models
- **Think of your mind as permeable, not siloed.** Emotions flow out and in. The people cycling through the background of your days are shifting you whether you consent or not.
- **Use disclosure sparingly because it's rewarding, not because it's useless.** Tamir & Mitchell (2012) found self-disclosure activates the brain's dopaminergic reward circuitry — people forwent *money* to disclose. The motivation runs deep; that's exactly why venting overruns its usefulness.
- **Think of yourself as a startup vetting advisers.** Different people for different functions; specialization is not rejection.
- **You can shift someone without a conversation.** Sensory and attention shifters work on others — a movie for reprieve, a song in the car. Useful when someone would get defensive if you named the problem. "I hit Play, but she listened."

## Anti-patterns
- **Excessive validation.** The friend who agrees so hard they dig you deeper into the outlook making you unhappy: *He's SUCH a jerk. You should be outraged. If I were you, I would tell him off.*
- **The probing questioner.** Asking for every detail, which makes you relive the emotion that had you worked up.
- **Advice first.** Jumping to solutions before listening. Bad for the problem and bad for the relationship.
- **Believing support begins and ends with spilling your guts.** "That's not what the research shows." Get it out, *then* put it in perspective.
- **Writing off all social comparison as toxic.** Kross calls this "one of the shoddiest pieces of advice I have ever given" — he'd told his daughters comparison is the thief of joy, and got served by his own tween. Worse, castigating yourself for comparing piles negative emotion on negative emotion.
- **Unchecked digital comparison.** His early-2010s work found more Facebook use → declining positive mood, mediated by **envy**. A later Facebook-internal study of 37,729 people: 22%+ had compared themselves to others and felt bad about it in the past two weeks; over a third of those said the bad feeling lasted a day or more. Extrapolated across ~3 billion users, hundreds of millions of people feel bad every day from digital comparison. A 60-year review found most comparisons are upward and most make people feel worse.

## Worked Example
**The asshole/cheerleader exercise.** Before an executive-education workshop on managing emotions in teams — Special Operations personnel, finance and auto executives, ambitious collaborative types — Kross walks in without introducing himself and asks for volunteers to lead breakout groups. Hands shoot up. He takes the volunteers to a side room and counts them off 1-2, 1-2.

The 1s will be **cheerleaders**: demonstrably supportive, positive feedback laid on thick, high enthusiasm. Easy lift for aspiring leaders; they nod.

The 2s will be **assholes**: stern, stoic, no positive feedback, glowering and eye-rolling at every question or suggested solution. Before he finishes explaining, horror dawns on their faces — they've just started building relationships with these peers, and the instruction violates the social norms they're operating under. They squirm, sigh, shift foot to foot. Only with assurances that the deception will be revealed at the end do they comply.

Each group gets a hard logic puzzle and five minutes.

Within *seconds* the rooms diverge. Cheerleader groups are loud — talking, laughing, moving around the table, spontaneously complimenting each other's ideas, patting backs. Asshole-led tables look, in Kross's phrase, like they're breaking rocks in a gulag: heads down, faces grim, silence broken by brief contrite suggestions, no eye contact. **Neither condition solves the puzzle** — but students later name this their favorite exercise of the four-day workshop, because it made visceral what they only knew intellectually: how fast emotion spreads.

**The upgrade case for downward comparison.** In a study by Micaela Rodriguez, Ozlem Ayduk, and Kross, participants dealing with a difficult problem were asked to think of someone they knew who was faring worse. Many found comfort and strength; they were more optimistic and less negative than a control group who simply reflected on their problem. One student thought of grieving, suffering family members who still got out of bed every day, and concluded: *If they can go out every day and do what needs to be done, with everything they're dealing with, then I can too.* The comparison was downward; the frame made it fuel rather than fear.

## Key Takeaways
1. Audit your emotional advisory board this week; circle only the people who both listen and broaden.
2. Run support conversations in order: empathize first, zoom out second, and calibrate the delay to the intensity.
3. Ask permission before pivoting: "I have a thought on this, are you okay to hear it?"
4. When you're the one hurting, redirect a co-ruminating conversation by asking what they'd do in your position.
5. Front-load your ask with advice-givers; educate the co-ruminators.
6. You can't stop comparing — so control the *frame* and the *frequency*, not the reflex.
7. When you need a lift and have no tool at hand, do something for someone nearby. It works as well as mindfulness or gratitude practice.
