# Patterns

Concrete techniques from across the ten articles.

## Style-Matched Persuasion (Ch 1)
**When to use**: You have one identifiable chief decision maker and a proposal that has already failed on merit alone.
**How**: Classify the person (charismatic, thinker, skeptic, follower, controller). Keep the content constant; change sequence, format, pacing, and vocabulary. Charismatic: lead with the exciting result, then undersell by naming each risk yourself. Thinker: lead with methodology and alternatives considered, reserve two meetings. Skeptic: establish credibility before you open your mouth. Follower: show precedent and a proven track record. Controller: send structured linear memos, never request the meeting.
**Trade-offs**: Misclassification costs you the proposal. If a person resists classification, treat them as a follower.

## Credibility Audit (Ch 4)
**When to use**: Before beginning any persuasion effort, especially with a new audience.
**How**: Ask two expertise questions (do I have a track record here, does the audience know it) and two relationship questions (do they see me as helpful and trustworthy, will they listen). Validate the answers with a colleague who will be honest. Where either is thin, buy it: hire an outside expert, cite independent research, run a visible pilot, or recruit a trusted sponsor.
**Trade-offs**: Slow and ego-bruising. Most managers considerably overestimate both scores.

## Listening Tour Before Framing (Ch 4)
**When to use**: You need a position that carries real shared advantage, not a rationalized version of your own.
**How**: One-on-one conversations with key stakeholders to gather perspective, explicitly not to pitch. Then frame the position in terms of the benefit they named. If no shared advantage exists, change the position.
**Trade-offs**: Months of calendar time. Frequently reveals that your original proposal was wrong.

## Active, Public, Voluntary Commitment (Ch 2)
**When to use**: You need behavior to persist without supervision.
**How**: Have the person summarize the agreement in their own words in writing, circulate it, and connect it to a value they already hold. Applied pressure voids the effect.
**Trade-offs**: Worthless if coerced. Coerced agreement is a burden, not a commitment.

## Peer Testimony Over Boss Speech (Ch 2)
**When to use**: Veterans resist a new initiative and your positional authority is the wrong lever.
**How**: Find a respected long-tenured colleague who genuinely supports the change and have them raise it at the team meeting.
**Trade-offs**: You surrender control of the framing.

## Structure the Meeting, Not the Person (Ch 3)
**When to use**: Capable people are consistently silent in mixed or hierarchical groups.
**How**: Control turn-taking, recognize speakers explicitly, do not reward whoever needs the shortest pause. Track who originated each adopted idea separately from who spoke longest.
**Trade-offs**: Less spontaneous, slower. But telling individuals to change their style asks them to change who they are.

## Ritual Test Before Attribution (Ch 3)
**When to use**: Someone's behavior seems inexplicable — a fight that evaporates, an apology with no fault attached.
**How**: Ask "is this literal or is this a form?" and interpret it in the speaker's system before assigning motive.
**Trade-offs**: Risks over-excusing genuinely bad behavior.

## Leader-Goes-First Debrief (Ch 5)
**When to use**: After a visible failure, to break a spiral of silence.
**How**: The authority figure names their own specific contribution to the failure before asking anyone else. Then redirect what surfaces from individual blame to systemic cause.
**Trade-offs**: Requires real vulnerability from the person with power; hollow versions backfire badly.

## Rehearse Intent, Never Gestures (Ch 6)
**When to use**: Preparing any high-stakes talk.
**How**: Four separate rehearsal passes, one per intent — be open (imagine speaking to a close friend), connect (imagine a distracted child, then a teenager), be passionate (ask what is at stake and what result you want), listen (predict the audience's emotional state, then adapt live).
**Trade-offs**: Rehearsed gestures lag the words, and audiences read that lag as fake. Live adaptation requires knowing the material cold.

## Springboard Story (Ch 7)
**When to use**: Driving adoption of a change after data and slides have failed.
**How**: A true, recent event with one identifiable protagonist and an authentically happy ending. Strip detail aggressively — seconds, not minutes. Close with "imagine if we did this everywhere."
**Trade-offs**: Less memorable than a fully told story. Without evidence of the positive outcome, jaded audiences generate an antistory.

## Negative-Polarity Knowledge Story (Ch 7)
**When to use**: Transferring hard-won know-how.
**How**: Lead with the mistake, explain in detail why the fix worked, invite better alternatives. Often has to be teased out by first asking about successes.
**Trade-offs**: Boring, narrow audience, and cannot be compelled.

## Engineered Yield Point (Ch 8)
**When to use**: Any pitch to a decision maker.
**How**: Pre-identify one element you will genuinely concede, surface it as early as possible, and invite the catcher's suggestions so they become a creative collaborator.
**Trade-offs**: Yield readily on everything and you become the pushover stereotype.

## Compressed Reorg Announcement (Ch 9)
**When to use**: Restructuring.
**How**: Announce whole and fast, never preview. Frame as business necessity, show the chart on the spot, state the scope of change, give a bounded window to react and a stated time when work resumes.
**Trade-offs**: No consultation period, so the framing must be right the first time. Previewing cost HP roughly a full quarter.

## Clarification Technique (Ch 10)
**When to use**: You are blindsided by a charged reaction you cannot explain.
**How**: Grant their perception, own the mismatch between your words and your intent, then return to the substance. Concede nothing about the facts.
**Trade-offs**: Unsatisfying if what you wanted was vindication.

## Fight Tactics, Not People (Ch 10)
**When to use**: Facing shouting, accusation, distortion, or digression.
**How**: Name the behavior without attacking character — describe the difficulty the tactic creates for you rather than labeling the person.
**Trade-offs**: Makes the tactic the topic, delaying the substantive issue.

## Rehearse With a Neutral Friend (Ch 10)
**When to use**: Before any conversation in a known danger zone.
**How**: Dump the content raw, restate it as if unloaded, fine-tune phrasing into temperate substitutes, check body language, write down hip-pocket phrases.
**Trade-offs**: Time cost; over-memorized scripts sound stiff.
