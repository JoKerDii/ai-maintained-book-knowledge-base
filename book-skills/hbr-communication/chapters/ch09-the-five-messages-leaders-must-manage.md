# Chapter 9: The Five Messages Leaders Must Manage
*John Hamm*

## Core Idea
Leaders leak vague, cliché-filled meaning on a handful of high-voltage topics, and employees fill the vacuum with their own interpretations — costing focus, time, and alignment. Take definitional control of five specific messages and you convert imprecise talk into organizational leverage.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Five Messages Leaders Must Manage**: five topics that "control the train" — (1) organizational structure and hierarchy, (2) financial results, (3) the leader's sense of his or her job, (4) time management, (5) corporate culture.
  - When to use: any time you are about to speak to the organization about a reorg, a quarter, your own role, deadlines, or "culture." Also as a periodic audit of what your people currently believe on each of the five.
  - How: for each of the five, (a) write down the mental model your people most likely default to, (b) write down the mental model you actually want, (c) state the replacement explicitly, in plain terms, with the rationale, (d) attach a concrete next action and a time, (e) check what got heard, then repeat until the vocabulary is shared.
  - Why it works: these five are the levers on the locomotive. Left undefined they don't stay neutral — they default to the fear-flavored reading (politics, blame, isolation, panic, perks), because that is what people imagine in the absence of clarity.

- **The Conventional Mental Model → Try This swap**: Hamm's replacement table for each message.
  - Hierarchy: *org chart as proxy for politics* → **optimize human resources**; treat the chart as a flexible map of accountability for action and results.
  - Financial results: *penalize misses, blame someone* → **perform a diagnostic to determine root cause** of any shortfall.
  - The leader's job: *the boss has the answers* → **everyone has answers — ask questions**.
  - Time management: *time is scarce, so scramble against constraints* → **time is fixed, so choose wisely within constraints**.
  - Culture: *hand responsibility to HR* → **create an environment in which everyone can help the team win**.
  - Failure mode: swapping the words without swapping your behavior. Both channels transmit — explicit definition and implicit behavior — and employees weight the implicit one more heavily.

- **84 Great Things**: seven direct reports × three important, very doable initiatives per quarter × four quarters = 84 significant accomplishments a year.
  - When to use: as the sizing rule when the team is overcommitted and everything is "priority."
  - How: cap each report at three real initiatives per quarter; refuse the 85th objective until the prior ones land.
  - Why it works: the enemy is not scarcity of hours but the temptation to start objective 85 at the expense of higher-priority goals.

## Key Concepts
- **Definitional control**: claiming and holding the meaning of a loaded term before rumor supplies one.
- **Managerial levers**: the five topics, which move the organization smoothly only when everyone shares their definitions.
- **Runaway train**: the state when a leader assumes shared assumptions and loses grip on the levers.
- **Implicit vs. explicit communication**: what your behavior, hesitancy, and pacing say versus what your words say — Fiorina's cautious floating of reorg ideas broadcast fear she never spoke aloud.
- **Results as diagnostic tool**: quarterly numbers used as a metric for long-term improvement rather than as a punitive weapon.
- **The "answer man" trap**: the leader whose identity rests on having all the answers, and who thereby stifles input and gets unreliable information.
- **Indulgent avoidance**: substituting culture perks (beer bashes, foosball, a "chief culture officer") for a winning strategy.
- **Winning defined**: culture built on explicit success criteria — e.g., "P/E ratio of 15, market share of 20%, 30% year-over-year revenue growth."
- **Communication leverage**: one CEO speaking precisely to 10 direct reports, each speaking precisely to 40 more, aligns 400 people.

## Mental Models
- **Think of a leader as a locomotive driver.** Five switches and levers control the train; a well-aligned organization moves them smoothly and everyone advances as one.
- **Use the golf-swing model when judging results.** Master aim, setup, and swing — once the ball is in the air you cannot control where it lands. Leverage is in execution quality, not in squeezing the quarter.
- **Use the firefighting-crew image when explaining a reorg.** Both teams have seven people; the winner has four hoses to your two. You aren't shifting power, you're re-allocating buckets and hoses.
- **Treat time as a neutral fact, not an enemy.** When time is tight, do fewer things very well — change the goal rather than change the deadline or demand more hours.

## Anti-patterns
- **Cliché-infused declarations** ("let's focus on key priorities," "customers come first"): every listener supplies a different definition, and none asks for clarification for fear of looking stupid.
- **Previewing a reorg for weeks**: HP lost roughly 12 weeks — a full quarter — of productivity to political jockeying and dread before Fiorina's announcement. Gather data, decide, then announce fast.
- **"These are the results I need; I don't care how you get it done"**: the CEO who ran this play got channel-stuffing, write-downs, stalled revenue, and a discount acquisition.
- **Drowning out your team with positional power**: Jim, brilliant and credentialed, ignored his hires' strategy input; the managers read the implicit message that they weren't valued and left with the intellectual capital.
- **Making time the boss**: Alan streamed a BellSouth RFP into perfectly timed tasks and lost to inferior technology because nobody invested in the customer relationship.
- **Outsourcing culture to HR**: morale programming without business metrics produces a company acquired for asset value.

## Worked Example
A 150-person software company is losing share to a close competitor and the CEO must realign resources. Rather than let "reorganization" mean job cuts, he compresses the entire message into one Monday all-hands.

Framing (business necessity, not politics): we're in a war for market share, I get paid to win it and so do you — and right now we aren't configured to win this particular battle, so I'm changing the structure of resources so we can execute more effectively.

Reassurance (scope of the change, stated plainly): most of you keep doing the job you're doing now; some of you will have a different supervisor.

Evidence: he shows the new org chart on the spot — no preview period, no leak window.

Clock (permission to react, and a deadline for it): it's 10:45; you have until noon to be annoyed, if that's your reaction. At noon, pizza. At one o'clock, we go to work in our new positions.

Contrast this with the HP pattern: months of cautious floating, twelve weeks of lost work, then more lost time as people settled in. The compact version costs about two hours. The counterfactual Hamm offers for Fiorina: hold a companywide meeting with a webcast within 48 hours of the decision, explain why the change was necessary, and ask everyone affected to submit explicit 60-day goals — which redirects attention from who's up and who's down onto organizational effectiveness.

## Key Takeaways
1. Audit the five messages before you speak on them; the default interpretation is always the fearful one.
2. Present structure as a flexible map of accountability for results — never as a power shift — and announce it fast, whole, and with a work-resumes-at time.
3. Use missed numbers as a root-cause diagnostic, communicated broadly for organizational learning; safety to discuss the miss is what produces the fix.
4. Define your job as bringing out the answers in others: withhold your theory in the meeting, because the boss's answer stifles the group's creativity.
5. When time is fixed, change the goal, not the deadline — TaylorMade shipped one club (the r7 quad driver) instead of a whole line, and made the anniversary launch.
6. Build culture from explicit success criteria and visible metrics, not from perks; state numerically what winning looks like.
7. Run the daily leader's questions: What needs to happen today to get where we want to go? Where is there confusion? What vague belief can I clarify or debunk? What have people taken for granted?
