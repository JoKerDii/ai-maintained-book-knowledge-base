# Chapter 6: How to Become an Authentic Speaker
*Nick Morgan*

## Core Idea
Gesture precedes conscious thought, and thought precedes words — so rehearsing specific body language makes you land the gesture *with* or *after* the words, which audiences unconsciously read as fake. Rehearse the underlying intent instead, and the body language emerges on its own, at the right instant.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Four Aims (Morgan's "intents"): to be open, to connect, to be passionate, to listen.**
  - When to use: preparing any high-stakes talk where you need belief, not just information transfer — town halls, sales kickoffs, investor updates, change announcements.
  - How: rehearse the speech four separate times, adopting one aim's mind-set per pass, *feeling* it rather than thinking about it, and rehearsing no gestures at all. Then keep focusing on the four aims while actually delivering.
    1. **Open** — practice by imagining you're delivering the talk to someone you're completely relaxed with: spouse, close friend, your child. Register what it *feels* like; that feeling is the target state. If imagining it fails, do the real thing with a patient friend.
    2. **Connect** — practice wanting, needing, to engage the listener. Imagine a young child you know well isn't heeding you and you must capture his attention. You don't strategize; you raise intensity or volume, or move closer. To practice *holding* attention, upgrade the child to a distractible teenager.
    3. **Passionate** — ask what you feel deeply about here: what's at stake, what result you want the talk to produce. Focus not on what you want to say but on *why* you're giving the speech and how you feel about it, and let that emotion come out in every word of this rehearsal pass. Then raise the stakes: imagine someone in the room can take everything away from you unless you win them over.
    4. **Listen** — think about what the audience will likely be feeling as you step up (excited? worried about bad news? hoping to keep their jobs post-merger?). Rehearse imagining yourself watching them closely for signs of their response.
  - Why it works: the aims are pre-verbal impulses, so working on them puts the nonverbal channel back in its natural leading position. Failure mode: over-intellectualizing. Treat it like grooving a golf swing — make tiny mental notes, but don't let them displace the feeling you're trying to be able to reproduce later.

- **The Second Conversation**: the nonverbal channel, which starts *first* — in the instant after an emotion fires in the brain but before it's articulated. When words and body contradict, audiences believe the body every time.
  - When to use: as the explanation for any talk that was technically correct and emotionally dead.
  - How: stop treating body language as an output to be scripted and start treating it as a readout of an internal state you can only change upstream.

## Key Concepts
- **The second conversation**: the nonverbal message, which fires before speech and outranks it whenever the two disagree.
- **Intent**: a pre-verbal aim (open, connect, passionate, listen) that generates authentic behavior downstream, as opposed to a rehearsed gesture.
- **Gesture-word sequencing**: natural gesture arrives fractionally *before* the associated words; rehearsed gesture arrives with or after them, which is the tell audiences unconsciously detect.
- **Rehearsed inauthenticity**: the artificial effect produced by traditional coaching ("maintain eye contact," "spread your arms," "come out from behind the podium") — the audience can see the wheels turning.
- **Winging it**: the opposite failure — unprepared delivery under stress reads as off-kilter and as though the speaker is learning as he goes.
- **Openness as the ante; connection as what keeps the audience playing**: openness only buys you into the game.
- **Listening with your whole body**: staying physically and psychologically still, as when someone is telling you something too important to miss — which produces genuine leaning-in and nodding.
- **The authenticity paradox**: real authenticity achieved through a deliberate, calculated preparation process.

## Mental Models
- **Think of body language as a symptom, not a lever.** You cannot fix the reading by editing the symptom; change the internal state and the symptom corrects itself.
- **Use the "person I'm relaxed with" substitution** whenever you feel yourself going stiff or over-serious — swap the audience in your mind for one person you're at ease with.
- **Treat the audience as a live channel, not a receiver.** They are always transmitting nonverbally; your job during delivery is to read and respond — pick up the pace, vary language, ask an impromptu question, cut a section.
- **Know your material cold so you have attention to spare.** Listening and adapting mid-speech is only possible if delivery costs you nothing.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rehearsing specific gestures**: puts them in the wrong sequence relative to words; audiences feel the mismatch without being able to name it.
- **"I don't want to look over-rehearsed, so I'll wing it"**: stress with no preparation produces incoherent body language and destroys the impression of leadership.
- **Focusing on what you want to say rather than why you're saying it**: yields a crystal-clear, dull talk.
- **Suppressing emotion about exactly the things that matter most to you**: a common learned habit that reads to audiences as coldness, no matter how strongly you actually believe the message.
- **Saving connection for the end**: audiences disengage long before you get there.
- **Assuming the audience shares your emotional premise** (your urgency, your sense of threat): the most common reason a well-built call to action falls flat.

## Worked Example
**Carol, a VP of sales, at a companywide sales meeting** (a composite of Morgan's clients). By conventional standards she does everything right: strides energetically to the podium, pauses to look at the audience, opens with a story from her days as a field rep, segues into the year's sales outlook, uses colorful slides showing growth and a strong product pipeline, accents her words with animated gestures, and closes by walking to the edge of the stage and challenging the room to commit to a stretch goal. She has rehearsed carefully in front of trusted colleagues who liked both message and energy.

The room stays flat. She can feel it and can't diagnose it.

Working the four aims one at a time surfaces four distinct, previously invisible problems:

- *Open* — as she practices imagining she's talking to someone she's completely relaxed with, her face starts lighting up with a big smile and her shoulders drop. She realizes she has been coming across as so serious that she was alienating audiences without intending to.
- *Connect* — intent on engagement, she notices she habitually waits until the very end of the speech to make contact with the room. She restructures: her next talk *opens* by reaching out to the specific audience members who drove the year's sales results, and the connection runs throughout rather than arriving as a closer.
- *Passionate* — asked what she actually feels deeply about, the answer isn't the sales forecast; it's her determination to beat a close competitor, and she recognizes that this is what fuels her energy for the job. Letting that into the talk makes her immediately more human and engaging.
- *Listen* — attending to what the audience is feeling, she discovers she had wrongly assumed her salespeople shared her sense of urgency about that competitor. So she rebuilds the front of the next presentation to spend real time on *why* stretch goals matter, before asking for the commitment.

**Two supporting cases.** A CEO passionate about his work whose audiences never responded traced it back to learning as a boy not to show emotion about precisely the things that mattered most to him; the fix was replacing that felt experience with the experience of talking to a close friend he was excited to see. And a retiring senior partner whose talk on commitment and hard work was clear but dull found the emotion underneath it — her mother, a dancer, then 92, who had instilled persistence through every obstacle and never let career pain obscure her joy in performing. She decided to acknowledge her mother in the talk; she shed most of her tears in rehearsal, and the passion turned a dutiful speech into a memorable one.

## Key Takeaways
1. Rehearse intents, never gestures — the sequencing error is what audiences detect as inauthenticity.
2. Run four separate rehearsal passes, one per aim, feeling each rather than analyzing it.
3. Openness comes first: if you read as closed, the audience reads itself as a threat to you and communication stops.
4. Find the real emotional stake — ask why you're speaking, not what you'll say — and let rehearsal be where the tears happen.
5. Front-load connection instead of saving it for the close.
6. Check your assumption that the audience already feels what you feel; usually they don't, and that gap is what you must close first.
7. Preparation and spontaneity are not opposites — know the material cold precisely so you can adapt live.
