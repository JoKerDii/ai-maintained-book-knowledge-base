# Chapter 1: Change the Way You Persuade
*Gary A. Williams and Robert B. Miller*

## Core Idea
Proposals fail less often because the content is weak than because the delivery is mismatched to the decision maker. Identify the chief decision maker's default decision-making style first, then sequence, format, and word your argument to that style.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Five Decision-Making Styles**: Executives making high-stakes decisions revert to one dominant default mode — **Charismatic (25%)**, **Thinker (11%)**, **Skeptic (19%)**, **Follower (36%)**, **Controller (9%)**.
  - When to use: Any time you must win approval from a single identifiable decision maker on a complex, consequential proposal.
  - How: (1) Determine who the chief decision maker actually is, not just who is in the room. (2) Classify the style from observed behavior — risk appetite, speed of deciding, appetite for being educated, tolerance for ambiguity. (3) Choose sequence of information, format (visual vs. data vs. narrative), the pacing across one or multiple meetings, and buzzwords. (4) Prepare a backup document for what you deliberately leave out of the meeting.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Styles are formed early in a career and reinforced by success, so they are stable and predictable. Failure mode: treating this as a personality test (it is not Myers-Briggs) or as a fixed trait — situation still matters; classification is a working hypothesis you revise.

- **Style-Matched Persuasion Strategy** (per style):
  - **Charismatic** — enthusiastic, visual, risk-seeking, short attention span. Lead with the most critical point, use simple results-oriented arguments and visual aids, deliberately *undersell* the parts that excite him, surface risks honestly, and hand over a full written report afterward.
  - **Thinker** — cerebral, risk-averse, data-hungry, toughest to persuade. Present the risk of doing nothing *first*, expose your methodology, give balanced case studies (successes and failures), name your own weak spots, and expect to spread the case over two meetings and wait weeks or months.
  - **Skeptic** — combative, suspicious of anything challenging his worldview, decides on gut and fast. Borrow credibility from someone he trusts (co-present), cite multiple reputable sources, ground everything in concrete real-world specifics, and always let him save face.
  - **Follower** — largest group, risk-averse, wants proven-but-innovative. Lead with references, testimonials, and case studies; show that others succeeded on this path; offer options so he can mix and match and feel inventive.
  - **Controller** — abhors ambiguity, implements only his own ideas, avoids accountability. Do not sell. Feed structured, linear information over months, highlight problem data, point out contradictions without resolving them, and wait for him to call the meeting.

## Key Concepts
- **Default mode of decision making**: The single style a person falls back on under high stakes, complexity, and serious consequences.
- **Order of presentation**: The sequence in which information is revealed; for a thinker it is nearly the exact reverse of the sequence for a charismatic.
- **Risk of doing nothing**: The cost of inaction — presented last to a charismatic, first to a thinker.
- **Credibility transfer**: Borrowing trust from a person the skeptic already trusts, prior to or during the meeting.
- **Face-saving correction**: Correcting a skeptic's bad information in a way that protects his ego and reputation.
- **Buzzwords**: Style-specific vocabulary that holds attention (e.g., *results/proven/clear* for charismatics; *quality/numbers/proof* for thinkers; *feel/grasp/trust/disrupt* for skeptics; *innovate/similar to/previous* for followers; *details/facts/logic/just do it* for controllers).
- **Quiet perseverance**: Waiting patiently for the decision even when initial enthusiasm suggested speed.
- **Risk-adjusted comparison**: Reframing a cheaper-looking option as more expensive once proven-ness is priced in — the counter to a follower's bargain consciousness.

## Mental Models
- Think of a proposal as **content plus delivery vector** — an unassailable argument delivered on the wrong vector loses to a weaker argument on the right one.
- Use the **mismatch base rate** as a prior: roughly 80% of sales presentations target skeptics and controllers, who are only 28% of executives. Assume your instinct is misaimed until you check.
- When someone is hard to classify, **default to Follower** — the other four types show their traits definitively; ambiguity itself is the tell.
- Treat a thinker's barrage of questions as an **attack on the process, not on you**; treat a skeptic's as personal, and stay cool either way.

## Anti-patterns
- **One-size-fits-all pitching**: Arguing to a thinker the way you'd argue to a skeptic — over half of presentations are style-mismatched.
- **Matching a charismatic's excitement**: Amplifying his enthusiasm removes the grounding he needs and erodes trust when reality lands.
- **Hiding downsides**: The charismatic will find them later, when you are not in the room to answer.
- **Drawing conclusions for a thinker**: Reads as too helpful, and costs you credibility; give them room to conclude for themselves.
- **Pushing hard on a controller**: Aggressive advocacy makes you part of the problem; supply information and let him convince himself.
- **Reading a follower as a skeptic**: Followers challenge and posture, but will only agree once shown it worked elsewhere.

## Worked Example
*MaxPro reorganization — same proposal, five deliveries.* Mary Flood, EVP of sales and marketing at an office-equipment maker, wants to decentralize national accounts into five regional teams and must win CEO George Nolan's approval.

- **Nolan as charismatic**: She pre-briefs the trusted COO and resolves his concerns by memo. In the meeting she draws the current org and its problems on a whiteboard, jumps straight to the new structure, and frames it as competitiveness. When Nolan starts talking as if it's done, she grounds him — relocation costs, likely IT resistance. She then covers implementation risk, then the risk of inaction (three major customers already shopping competitors), and closes by asking for next steps, handing him a report in both executive-summary and in-depth versions.
- **Nolan as thinker**: Two meetings. Meeting one opens with the risk of doing nothing, then her methodology for arriving at three options — Nolan pokes holes and thereby takes ownership of it. She presents case studies split roughly evenly between successes and failures, is candid about weak data and gut assumptions, accepts his reweighting of a 60-40 risk to 50-50, and leaves with a prioritized to-do list. Meeting two summarizes corrections, flags every change explicitly (he hates surprises), recommends the optimum option, and shows financials. Then she waits months.
- **Nolan as skeptic**: She recruits the COO to co-present and deliver the controversial material. Sources are named and multiplied ("I arranged a call with several market-research experts"). Case studies open with "You've probably seen this before…" Arguments stay concrete down to which building gets subleased. They close by crediting Nolan's own remark as the origin of the idea.
- **Nolan as follower**: Eight case studies, all from outside MaxPro's industry, so she can say "we could be the first in our industry to do this." Failed restructurings are omitted but held in reserve. Three options, with four case references attached to the one she prefers and two to each of the others; she shows option three is cheapest risk-adjusted. He mixes elements of options one and three — exactly what she engineered.
- **Nolan as controller**: No pitch. For four months she sends customer reports, marketing studies, and financial projections in structured, linear memos, foregrounds problem data, and flags contradictions without resolving them ("this seems to contradict last year's study; I'm not sure which to trust"). She resists scheduling a presentation. When a major customer defects, Nolan calls the meeting himself.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify the chief decision maker before you build the deck; tailor sequence and format to that one person.
2. Order of information is a lever as powerful as the information itself — reverse it between charismatics and thinkers.
3. Surface risks and weak data proactively; concealment costs more with every style, and buys trust with charismatics and thinkers.
4. Borrow credibility when you lack it, especially with skeptics — but expect to earn it eventually under aggressive questioning.
5. With controllers, the only sale is a self-sale; patience and structured information beat advocacy.
6. Followers are the largest and most easily missed group — when in doubt, assume follower and lead with proof that others succeeded.
