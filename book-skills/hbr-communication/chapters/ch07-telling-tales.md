# Chapter 7: Telling Tales
*Stephen Denning*

## Core Idea
Form follows function in organizational storytelling: match the narrative pattern to the business objective, and accept that a "badly told" story often outperforms a well-told one. To spark action, strip detail out — the gaps are what let listeners write themselves into the story.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The springboard story**: a minimalist, true, recent story about a single protagonist with an authentically happy ending, told to make listeners leap to "if that worked there, why not here?"
  - When to use: you need people to act — adopt a new practice, back an unfamiliar initiative — and argument, data, and slides have failed to move them.
  - How: (1) pick a real, recent event so it survives credibility checks; (2) use one protagonist your audience can identify with; (3) show a change that actually succeeded, at least partly — leave the unhappy counterfactual implicit rather than spelling it out; (4) include only enough detail to be intelligible and credible; (5) end by inviting the analogy explicitly ("Imagine if... Think what we could become"); (6) keep it to seconds, not minutes.
  - Why it works / failure mode: the listener has to do the imaginative work of transposing the story onto their own situation, and they only have mental room for that if the story is thin. Over-textured storytelling captures the audience *inside* the story — they leave moved but inert. The other failure mode is the untrue or unevidenced optimistic story, which jaded audiences discount immediately.

- **The Storytelling Catalog** (seven narrative patterns, each with its own required form):

  | Objective | Story must | Tell it by | Response you want |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Sparking action | Show a change that succeeded elsewhere, transposable to here | Cutting detail that would absorb attention | "Just imagine..." / "What if..." |
  | Communicating who you are | Dramatize a life event revealing a strength or vulnerability, and what you took from it | Adding vivid detail — but only if the audience has time and appetite | "I didn't know that about him" |
  | Transmitting values | Feel familiar; establish limits on destructive behavior | Using believable (possibly hypothetical) characters; living the value yourself | "That's so right" |
  | Fostering collaboration | Movingly recount an experience the listeners share | Keeping the agenda open so stories chain; having an action plan ready | "That reminds me of the time I..." |
  | Taming the grapevine | Expose the incongruity between rumor and reality, usually via gentle humor | Never mean-spirited; only when the rumor is genuinely false | "I'd never thought about it like that" |
  | Sharing knowledge | Center a problem and a mistake, explaining in detail why the fix worked | Soliciting better alternatives from the audience | "We'd better watch that from now on" |
  | Leading people into the future | Evoke the destination without predictions that will prove wrong | Requires real verbal skill — otherwise substitute a springboard story | "When do we start?" |

## Key Concepts
- **Springboard story**: minimal-detail true story that launches listeners into imagining a change in their own context.
- **Maximalist account**: the fully rendered Aristotelian version — characters, plot, reversal, denouement — engrossing but action-suppressing in business settings.
- **Antistory**: the cynical counter-narrative an audience privately constructs when a story is implausibly rosy.
- **Story antibody**: a positive story deliberately attached to a circulating negative one to neutralize it.
- **Knowledge-sharing narrative**: plotless, heroless, negative-toned account of a problem and why the solution worked; boring by design and the workhorse of organizational storytelling.
- **Practical distinction — true vs. more than true**: action stories must be true and evidenced; knowledge stories must additionally be diagnostically complete about cause and effect.
- **Beware the well-told story**: craft that serves entertainment can defeat the business purpose.
- **Beware the positive story**: uniformly upbeat narrative triggers disbelief when the goal is learning.

## Mental Models
- Use minimalism when you want the listener to *finish* the story; use richness when you want them to *inhabit* it.
- Think of a springboard story as a lever, not a picture: its job is displacement, not description.
- When the aim is knowledge transfer, invert the polarity — lead with the mistake, because people learn more from failures than successes.
- Treat the grapevine as a river to steer, not a fire to stamp out; work with its current or leave it alone.

## Anti-patterns
- **Fully dramatizing a story meant to spark action**: the audience gets absorbed in the protagonist's world and has no attention left to build their own analogy.
- **Purely positive stories used for learning**: they read like children's primers and provoke antistories about what really happened.
- **Precise predictions in vision stories**: specifics will be wrong, and being wrong costs you credibility; evoke direction instead.
- **Denying, investigating, or ignoring a rumor**: denial lends it credibility, inquiry spreads it, silence lets it spiral.
- **Ridiculing a rumor that is actually true or reasonable**: admit the substance, put it in perspective, move on.
- **Values stories unsupported by behavior**: narrative cannot install values a leader does not live daily.
- **Compelling knowledge-sharing stories**: mistakes have to be teased out, often by first asking about successes.

## Worked Example
Denning's own Zambia story, told to move the World Bank toward knowledge management after arguments and PowerPoint had failed. The version he used, roughly: *in June 1995 a health worker in a small town in Zambia, 600 kilometers from the capital, went to the CDC website and got the answer to a malaria treatment question. The striking thing is that the World Bank isn't in this picture. Our know-how on poverty isn't reaching the millions who could use it. Imagine if it were. Think what an organization we could become.*

A professional storyteller told him this was not a story at all — no plot, no characters, no telling. The maximalist rewrite was obvious: the health worker's life, the suffering patient, the false leads before she found the site, the moment of triumph. Denning declined it. Executives had neither the time nor the patience for an epic, and — more importantly — if listeners were immersed in Zambia they would not be asking "why can't we put our knowledge on the web?" The missing texture was the mechanism.

Contrast the same author's opposite case, IBM's laptop plant in the UK, where workers said managers were overpaid and out of touch. A new site director appeared unannounced in a white coat, sat on the line building ThinkPads, asked workers for help, and when asked why he earned so much more replied that if a worker fails badly he loses his job, and if the director fails badly 3,000 people lose theirs. Not a story in the traditional sense — but a seed the grapevine grew into a counter-narrative, and the atmosphere improved within weeks.

## Key Takeaways
1. Decide the objective first; the objective dictates the story's form, length, and emotional polarity.
2. To drive action, deliberately underwrite the story — leave the listener the imaginative space to transpose it.
3. Business cases are built on numbers but approved on narrative; supply the causal story behind the figures.
4. Negative, unglamorous, plotless stories are the right tool for transferring know-how.
5. Vision stories should be vivid in direction and vague in specifics (Welch's "number one or number two, or we exit").
6. Plant counter-stories rather than denials when rumors are false; when they are true, concede and reframe.
7. Any story you tell about values must be consistent with your own observable actions or it inverts.
