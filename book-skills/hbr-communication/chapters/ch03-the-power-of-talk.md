# Chapter 3: The Power of Talk: Who Gets Heard and Why
*Deborah Tannen*

## Core Idea
*How* something is said routinely overrides *what* is said, because linguistic style is culturally learned and varies systematically — so judgments of competence and confidence are often judgments of style. The managerial skill is not adopting one "best" style but recognizing style differences so that people with something valuable to contribute actually get heard.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Linguistic Style**: A person's characteristic speaking pattern — directness or indirectness, pacing and pausing, word choice, and the use of jokes, figures of speech, stories, questions, and apologies. It is a set of culturally learned signals by which we both communicate meaning and evaluate other people.
  - When to use: Whenever you are assessing someone's competence, confidence, or contribution from how they speak — performance reviews, promotion decisions, meetings, interviews, hiring.
  - How: (1) Separate the message from the signal — ask what the person said, not how confidently they said it. (2) Identify the style features in play (pause length, hedging, pronoun choice, apology frequency, directness). (3) Ask what that feature means *in the speaker's* system, not yours. (4) Adjust the structure — meeting format, credit attribution, feedback framing — rather than demanding the speaker convert.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Style is learned in childhood peer groups and feels natural, so we mistake our own conventions for universal logic. Failure mode: "fixing" the speaker — telling someone to self-promote or be assertive asks them to violate an identity, since the way we speak is who we are.

- **The Status/Rapport Dynamic (one up, one down)**: Every utterance operates on two levels — it communicates ideas *and* negotiates relationships, signaling and creating relative status and level of rapport.
  - When to use: To decode "poor communication" incidents that have no factual disagreement at their core.
  - How: Ask whether each speaker was optimizing for status (avoiding the one-down position, resisting challenges, displaying ability) or for rapport (saving face, minimizing difference, balancing needs). Boys' play groups tend to train the status dimension; girls' tend to train the rapport dimension — so cross-style conversation behaves like cross-cultural communication.
  - Why it works / failure mode: It explains why the same words land differently. Failure mode: reading it as a universal law about men and women rather than a distribution — plenty of men use rapport-oriented styles and pay the same costs.

- **Conversational Rituals**: Conventionalized exchanges where the literal words are not the point and a specific type of response is expected — greetings, ritual apologies, compliment exchanges, praise-then-criticism feedback, ritual opposition. Trouble arises when one party takes another party's ritual literally.

## Key Concepts
- **Ritual apology**: "I'm sorry" used to express concern or smooth an exchange, not to admit fault; frequent users can be read as weaker and more blameworthy.
- **Ritual opposition**: Exploring an idea through verbal combat — stating it in absolute terms, playing devil's advocate, testing by attack — after which the fight is simply over.
- **Buffered feedback**: Citing strengths before weaknesses to save face; the receiver may hear the first item as the main point and the rest as afterthought.
- **Compliment exchange**: An automatic reciprocal ritual; asking "what did you think of mine?" expects a matching compliment, not a critique.
- **Indirectness**: Saying what you mean without spelling it out — pervasive in all communication, and used by the powerful (as softened orders) as often as by subordinates.
- **Mitigation**: Softening a statement, common when subordinates raise concerns upward — and, in aviation, sometimes fatal.
- **Managing up**: Making accomplishments visible to those with power over advancement; boys' peer groups reward talking achievements up, girls' reward playing them down.
- **Turn-taking and pause length**: Regional and cultural expectations about the natural gap between turns, which determine who gets to speak at all.
- **Credit pronoun ("I" vs. "we")**: Using "we" for solo work avoids seeming self-promoting but forfeits attribution.
- **Demeanor judgment**: A higher-ranking person's power to enforce their own view of appropriate style, which usually means direct in U.S. business contexts.

## Mental Models
- Think of cross-style conversation as **cross-cultural communication**: you cannot assume the other person means what you would mean if you said the same thing the same way.
- Use the **ritual test** when someone's behavior seems inexplicable: ask "is this literal, or is this a form?" A heated budget argument that evaporates an hour later was a ritual fight, not a real one.
- **Judge the idea, not the packaging.** Predicted-grades research shows public modesty and private confidence can diverge completely — a lower self-prediction may signal a wish not to boast, not actual doubt.
- Treat an **unstructured meeting as a style filter, not a level playing field** — it silently advantages those who need no pause and don't wait to be recognized.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confidence as a proxy for merit**: The CEO who approves proposals that "sound confident" in five minutes is calibrated to one style and will be dead wrong about everyone else's.
- **Prescribing self-promotion**: Telling a researcher to "own her ideas" turned her work into a grabbing game she disliked; the fix cost more than the problem.
- **Reading question-asking as ignorance**: A physician was rated below her peers for exactly one reason — "you ask more questions" — burying knowledge the organization needed.
- **Praise-first feedback with a literal listener**: The marketing director revised nothing substantive because he heard the opening praise as the message.
- **Hedging into ritual opposition**: Softening an idea to fend off attack makes it look weak and invites the attack instead.
- **Confrontational case interviews as a competence test**: They select for comfort with ritual opposition; candidates who "test badly" often make the better consultants.
- **Assuming an indirect request registered**: "How would you feel about helping the bookkeeper out?" got "Fine" — meaning "fine, I'll think about it." A week later nothing had happened.

## Worked Example
*The flextime focus group — who got credit.* A multinational convened a group to evaluate a new flextime policy. The discussion went well; the group endorsed the policy and agreed on improvements, and every participant judged the meeting a success.

The observer left with the impression that Phil had generated most of the adopted suggestions. Typing up the notes revealed that Cheryl had made almost all of them. Phil had picked up Cheryl's points and supported them — speaking at greater length in supporting them than she had spent raising them.

This was not idea theft. Phil never claimed her ideas. Cheryl left satisfied that she had contributed and grateful for his support; she explicitly noted it was *not* one of those times a woman says something, it's ignored, and a man repeats it. The team worked, the group met its charge, the company got what it needed.

The problem surfaced only when participants were asked who had been most influential. Both other women named Cheryl. Two of the three men named Phil. Of the men, only Phil named Cheryl. Airtime, not authorship, drove the attribution — and in a performance review that gap becomes a promotion decision.

*Second case — indirectness with lives at stake.* Before the Air Florida crash at National Airport, the copilot repeatedly raised concerns indirectly: "Look how the ice is just hanging on his, ah, back there"; "this is a losing battle here on trying to deice those things"; "that don't seem right, does it? … Ah, that's not right. Well—". When the pilot answered "Yes it is, there's 80," the copilot retreated: "Naw, I don't think that's right. [pause] Ah, maybe it is." Airlines responded by training copilots toward assertiveness. But a Japanese student's counter-proposal is equally valid: train pilots to pick up on hints. Either direction works if the style is shared — the fix belongs to the system, not only the subordinate.

## Key Takeaways
1. Assume "poor communication" is a style mismatch until proven otherwise; look for the ritual before assigning blame.
2. Confidence and competence judgments are style judgments — audit yours against the actual substance of what was said.
3. Track authorship separately from airtime, or the loudest supporter of an idea will be credited with it.
4. Do not order individuals to change style; change the structure — meeting format, credit protocols, feedback conventions.
5. In one direction, state feedback so the main point cannot be mistaken for an afterthought; in the other, don't take the opening praise as the verdict.
6. Indirect requests need confirmation of interpretation, especially downward across a rank gap.
7. There is no single best way to give criticism or orders — the result depends on situation, company culture, relative rank, and how the two styles interact.
