## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Five Decision-Making Styles (Williams & Miller, Ch 1)
Executives revert to one default mode under stakes: **Charismatic** (25%, exuberant but decides on balanced information), **Thinker** (11%, cerebral, risk-averse, slowest to persuade), **Skeptic** (19%, combative, decides on gut, needs credibility transfer), **Follower** (36%, decides by precedent, wants innovative-yet-proven), **Controller** (9%, abhors ambiguity, implements only his own ideas). Keep the content of your argument constant; change sequence, format, pacing, and vocabulary per style. If you cannot classify someone, they are a follower.

### The Six Principles of Persuasion (Cialdini, Ch 2)
**Liking, Reciprocity, Social Proof, Consistency, Authority, Scarcity.** Apply in combination within a single interaction rather than serially. Never deploy one on a false premise — detection is fatal and reciprocity returns the dishonesty. A commitment binds future behavior only if it is **active, public, and voluntary**; coerced agreement is a burden, not a commitment.

### Linguistic Style and the Status/Rapport Dynamic (Tannen, Ch 3)
**Linguistic style** is a person's characteristic pattern of directness, pacing, pausing, jokes, questions, and apologies — culturally learned signals used both to communicate and to judge others. Every utterance simultaneously conveys ideas and negotiates relative position ("one up, one down"). **Conversational rituals** (ritual apology, ritual opposition, buffered feedback, compliment exchange) break when one party takes them literally. **There is no one best way to communicate.** Do not tell individuals to change style; change the meeting structure instead.

### The Four Essential Steps of Persuasion (Conger, Ch 4)
1. **Establish credibility** — from expertise (track record) and relationships (listening, integrity, acting in others' interest). Audit it before you argue; buy it if it is thin.
2. **Frame goals on common ground** — state the position in terms of tangible benefit to the audience. If no shared advantage exists, change the position.
3. **Reinforce with vivid evidence** — stories, metaphors, analogies alongside data.
4. **Connect emotionally** — match the audience's emotional state, not your own; end on hope.
Persuasion is **learning and negotiating**, never a one-shot convincing. **Four ways not to persuade**: hard sell up front, refusing to compromise, argument alone, treating it as an event.

### The Spiral of Silence (Perlow & Williams, Ch 5)
Silence → resentment → disconnection → self-protection → more fear → more silence, ending in mental opt-out or exit. Break it by **recognizing your power**, **acting deviantly** (asking the question nobody asks), and **building a coalition**. Leaders break it by disclosing their own failure first, then reframing what surfaces as systemic rather than personal. Watch for the **speed trap**: deadline pressure silences people, which produces worse decisions and more pressure.

### The Four Aims and the Second Conversation (Morgan, Ch 6)
Rehearse four **intents** — be **open**, **connect**, be **passionate**, **listen** — as separate passes, feeling rather than thinking. Never rehearse specific gestures: natural gesture precedes words, rehearsed gesture lags them, and audiences read that lag as fake. The **second conversation** is the nonverbal channel; it fires first and wins whenever the two conflict.

### The Storytelling Catalog (Denning, Ch 7)
Form follows function. The **springboard story** — true, recent, one protagonist, minimal detail, authentically happy ending — makes listeners leap to "if it worked there, why not here?" Different purposes demand different forms: knowledge-sharing stories must be **negative** in tone and plotless; action stories must be stripped of detail; vision stories must avoid specific predictions. Beware the well-told story (craft can defeat purpose) and the **antistory** (the cynical counter-narrative an implausibly rosy account provokes).

### Pitcher Types and the Creative Collaborator (Elsbach, Ch 8)
The **catcher** judges the pitcher's creativity, not just the idea, and categorizes in under 150 ms. Three successful types: **showrunner** (levels the power differential with a knowledge duet), **artist** (inverts it via thought experiments), **neophyte** (exploits it by asking boldly for help). Four self-inflicted kills: **pushover, robot, used-car salesman, charity case**. Negative cues outweigh positive ones. The strongest move is to engage the catcher as a **creative collaborator** early.

### The Five Messages Leaders Must Manage (Hamm, Ch 9)
Organizational structure and hierarchy, financial results, the leader's sense of his own job, time management, and corporate culture. Claim each term's meaning before rumor supplies one. Treat results as a **diagnostic**, not a punishment. Announce reorgs whole and fast. Cap direct reports at three initiatives per quarter (**84 Great Things**).

### The Three Stressful Conversations (Weeks, Ch 10)
"I have bad news for you" → **honor thy partner** (acknowledge your own share first). "What's going on here?" → **disarm by restating your intentions** (the clarification technique). "You are attacking me!" → **fight tactics, not people**. The DNA of conversation management is **clarity, neutrality, temperance**. Clarity is not brutality — content is not brutal, delivery is. Improvement is unilateral: prepare against your own predictable vulnerability, not their unpredictable behavior.
