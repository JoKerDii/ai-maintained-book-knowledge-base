# Chapter 2: Enough About You

## Core Idea
The coaching method is "a simple but brutal regimen" — solicit 360-degree feedback, confront the person with it, then run a fixed sequence of apologizing, advertising, following up, listening, thanking, and feedforward. It works on anyone who genuinely wants to get better, at any rung of the ladder, because interpersonal flaws are uncorrelated with seniority.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Coaching Regimen (the seven-step behavioral change process)**:
  1. **Solicit 360-degree feedback** from colleagues up, down, and sideways in the chain of command — "often including family members" — for a comprehensive assessment of strengths and weaknesses.
  2. **Confront them with what everybody really thinks**, and require three things: accepting the information, agreeing there's room to improve, and committing to change.
  3. **Apologize** to everyone affected — "because it's the only way to erase the negative baggage associated with our prior actions" — and ask those same people for help getting better.
  4. **Advertise** your effort — "you have to tell people that you're trying to change; they won't notice it on their own."
  5. **Follow up religiously** every month or so — "the only honest way to find out how you're doing," and it reminds people you're still trying.
  6. **Listen without prejudice** — without interrupting or arguing.
  7. **Respond with gratitude**: say "Thank you" without ruining the gesture or embellishing it.
  8. **Feedforward** — the "special sauce" methodology for eliciting advice on what you can do better *in the future*.
  - When to use: On one specific annoying habit, not a personality overhaul. The stated goal is narrow — "identify a personal habit that's annoying their coworkers and help them eliminate it."
  - How: Run it for **12 to 18 months**. Success is measured not in the client's own mind but "in the opinions of their coworkers."
  - Why it works: Every step is externally verifiable. Steps 3–5 move change out of private intention into public accountability, which is what makes it stick.

- **The pay-only-if-it-works contract**: Goldsmith's opening offer to a skeptical CEO — "I'll work with him for a year. If he gets better, pay me. If not, it's all free."
  - When to use: Selling behavioral change to someone who doesn't believe it's possible. It transfers all risk to the coach and forces the coach to define an observable success criterion up front.

## Key Concepts
- **360-degree feedback**: Assessment gathered from every direction around a person — superiors, peers, direct reports, and family — rather than from the boss alone.
- **Feedforward**: Soliciting forward-looking suggestions for improvement instead of backward-looking judgments of past behavior.
- **The success junkie**: The archetype client — triumphant at each rung of the ladder, loves to win at work, touch football, poker, or an argument with a stranger; charming, high-potential, and often financially independent enough to "flip off the world."
- **Screw-you money**: Financial independence that removes the ordinary consequences of bad behavior, producing "a potent mix of stubbornness and pride and defensiveness."
- **The no-correlation rule**: "There is no correlation between an individual's standing in the corporate pyramid and what his coworkers think of his interpersonal skills." Middle managers are just as likely as CEOs to be seen as arrogant, inattentive, rude, and "unfoundedly omniscient."
- **The target audience**: "People who are successful in their own minds but want to be even more successful."
- **Retaining your value**: The purpose of the work — not making clients smarter or richer, but keeping them valuable to the organization.

## Mental Models
- **Think of successful people as golfers**: "Golfers suffer all the symptoms of successful people, perhaps even more acutely." Three delusions map exactly onto executives — delusional about *success* (one round under 90 in a hundred becomes "my usual game"); delusional about *how they achieved it* (mulligans, improved lies, uncounted strokes = bosses who over-claim credit and stretch the truth); delusional about *weaknesses*, which they deny, so they practice what they're already good at.
- **Use the golfer's one noble quality as your entry point**: No matter how good they are, scratch or 30 handicap, "they all want to get better." That desire — not the flaw — is what you recruit.
- **Think of your behavioral flaws as messy corners**: "All of us have corners in our behavioral makeup that are messy" — localized, findable, and tidyable, not structural.
- **Use "commitment plus one narrow habit" when scoping a change effort**: A book aimed only at the super-successful would be "a golf instructional just for PGA Tour players" — useful to a rounding error of the population. Scope for the many.

## Anti-patterns
- **Practicing your strengths**: Spending most of your time on what you're already good at and little on the part of your game that needs work.
- **Buffing the scorecard**: Awarding yourself mulligans and moving the ball — claiming more credit for a success than you're entitled to and stretching the truth to gain an advantage.
- **Changing quietly**: Improving without telling anyone. Perception lags behavior, so unadvertised change registers as no change at all.
- **Skipping the apology**: Trying to move forward while the negative baggage of prior behavior is still attached.
- **Assuming seniority immunizes you**: The pyramid says nothing about what your colleagues think of you.

## Worked Example
*The phone call that started the practice.* A Fortune 100 CEO calls after a leadership clinic about a division head who "delivers his numbers and more every quarter" — and whom he describes in one breath as young, smart, dedicated, ethical, motivated, hard-working, entrepreneurial, creative, charismatic, arrogant, stubborn, and a know-it-all jerk. The company is built on team values; nobody thinks he's a team player. The CEO gives him a year to change or he's out — but adds that turning him around "would be worth a fortune to us." Goldsmith, who had never coached one-on-one before, sees the real question: can someone *demonstrably* successful — whose paycheck, title, and hundreds of direct reports all affirm he's doing everything right — be made *more* successful? He says, "I might be able to help." The CEO's reply: "I doubt it." Goldsmith answers with the risk-free year. **This demonstrates the book's founding premise: the flaw and the success coexist in the same person and the same résumé — so the case for change can never be "you are failing," only "the skills and habits that took you this far won't take you further."**

## Key Takeaways
1. Get real 360-degree data before attempting any behavioral change; self-assessment is the thing that's broken.
2. Scope the work to **one** habit that annoys coworkers — not a personality renovation.
3. Apologize first; it's the only way to clear prior baggage, and it enlists the people you wronged as helpers.
4. Advertise the change — people won't notice on their own.
5. Follow up monthly. Following up is the measurement *and* the reminder.
6. Listen without interrupting or arguing, and answer with a plain "Thank you."
7. Judge success by your coworkers' opinions after 12–18 months, never by your own.
