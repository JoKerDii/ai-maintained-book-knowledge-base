# Chapter 6: Feedback

## Core Idea
Feedback is the MRI, not the surgery: it tells us "where we are" and what to change, never how to change it. Confidential 360-degree feedback is the best available instrument, but it only works once you have disarmed the raters — because successful people don't want to hear negative feedback and no one wants to give it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Commitments** (the contract you extract from every rater *before* you ask them anything):
  1. **Let go of the past.**
  2. **Tell the truth.**
  3. **Be supportive and helpful — not cynical or negative.**
  4. **Pick something to improve yourself** — so everyone is focused more on "improving" than "judging."
  - When to use: Before soliciting any feedback, from any rater, whether or not a coach is involved. Also use it as a *selection filter*: run the four commitments against a list of the last dozen people you've worked with; anyone who qualifies on all four is a legitimate rater. Goldsmith likens it to "conducting voir dire to assemble a trial jury."
  - How: Ask each person explicitly to commit. If they say no (usually because they've psychologically written you off), don't argue — just exclude them from the report. "If we aren't going to try to help our colleagues, why should we be allowed to judge them?"
  - Why it works: Commitment 1 converts critics into helpers ("Forgiveness means letting go of the hope for a better past"). Commitment 4 is the subtle one — it manufactures **parity**: you and the rater become equals in the same struggle, which is what keeps them engaged over the 12- to 18-month process. Change is not a one-way street; it involves the person changing *and* the people who notice it.

- **360-degree feedback, qualified**: Confidential interviews with 8–31 coworkers (average ~15), each about an hour, covering what the client does right, what needs to change, and how an already successful person can get even better.
  - How: The **client picks the raters** (if the client isn't the CEO, the CEO also approves the list). This is deliberate — the #1 excuse for denying feedback is "the wrong people said it." Raters are told plainly: "I don't get paid if he doesn't get better," and *they* define "better."
  - Why it works / failure mode: Ownership of the rater list removes the escape hatch. Fails when you use encounter-group questions ("What do you think of me?") in a power relationship — subordinates won't risk the truth.

- **The three forms of feedback**: **Solicited**, **unsolicited**, and **observational**.
  - Solicited: the only question that works — **"How can I do better?"** (variants: "What can I do to be a better partner/colleague/leader?"). Must (a) solicit advice not criticism, (b) point at the future not the negative past, (c) imply you will act on it.
  - Unsolicited: the **blindside event** — the Johari Window's fourth pane, what is known to others but unknown to you.
  - Observational: what you notice in eye contact, body language, response time.

- **Five self-administered observational drills** (get feedback with no coach):
  1. **Make a list of people's casual remarks about you** — write down every comment about you for a day, rate each positive/negative, hunt the recurring negative. It's agenda-free, so it's honest.
  2. **Turn the sound off** — watch a meeting as a silent movie and observe how people physically deal with you (lean in or away, acknowledge or ignore). Variant: arrive first and watch how each person reacts on entering.
  3. **Complete the sentence** (Nathaniel Brandon's technique) — pick one thing to improve, then list benefits: "If I get in shape, I will…" As answers shift from corporately correct to personal, you've confirmed you picked the right thing to fix.
  4. **Listen to your self-aggrandizing remarks** — what people boast about is usually their most egregious weakness. Same alert for pseudo-self-deprecation ("I don't have any ego invested in this" means it's all about ego).
  5. **Look homeward** — test whether the flaw shows up at home. "Your flaws at work don't vanish when you walk through the front door at home."

## Key Concepts
- **360-degree feedback**: Confidential feedback solicited from everybody at all levels of the organization — the best way for successful people to identify what to improve at work.
- **Depersonalizing feedback**: Talking about the task, not the person — sane in theory, but useless when identity is fused with the work.
- **Blindside event**: A rare "road to Damascus" moment when others reveal how the world actually sees you.
- **Johari Window**: Four-pane grid (public / private / unknowable / blindside) sorting what is known and unknown to self and others; only the fourth pane is worth mining.
- **Observing with judgment**: The difference between passively noticing and noticing *with purpose* — what turns daily signals into feedback.
- **Confidential feedback**: The best solicited feedback, because nobody gets embarrassed, defensive, or knows whom to retaliate against.
- **"Kill the messenger"**: The predictable response that keeps emperors ruling without clothes.

## Mental Models
- **Think of feedback as an MRI**: it identifies the deep-tissue damage; it is not the operation and not the rehab.
- **Use "How can I do better?" whenever you'd be tempted to ask "What do you think of me?"** — the second is an encounter-group question, irrelevant to work and unanswerable in a power relationship.
- **Think of every advice-giver as handing you a gift**: the only correct reply is "Thank you." No one expects you to act on all of it.
- **Use your best friend at work as rater #1**: by definition they already satisfy all four commitments.

## Anti-patterns
- **Asking for feedback and then expressing your opinion**: Any response — however softly couched — reads as rationalization, denial, or objection. You asked for an answer, not an argument.
- **Trying to prove successful people wrong**: We accept feedback consistent with our self-image and reject what isn't. Proving works about as well as making someone change.
- **Letting the recipient dispute the raters**: If you pick the panel yourself, "the wrong people said it" is unavailable as a defense.
- **Traditional face-to-face negative feedback**: It focuses on a failed past you cannot change, and it shuts people down — no change happens inside a closed shell.
- **Stopping at feedback**: Feedback tells you what to change, not how to do it.

## Worked Example
*Goldsmith's own blindside event.* At 28, a UCLA Ph.D. candidate in Bob Tannenbaum's class, he spent three weeks lecturing the group on how shallow and status-obsessed the citizens of Los Angeles were — all that mattered to them was impressing others. Tannenbaum finally asked whom he was talking to. Goldsmith said "the group." Tannenbaum pointed out he had addressed and looked at only one person: Tannenbaum. Then the question that landed: "Is there any chance that for the last three weeks all you have been trying to do is impress me?" Goldsmith indignantly refused it — and hated Tannenbaum for six months before conceding the person with the problem was himself. **Demonstrates the two lessons of unsolicited feedback: it is far easier to see our problems in others than in ourselves, and what we successfully deny to ourselves is already obvious to everyone watching.**

*Counterpart — "Mike," the Gordon Gekko trader.* Rated dead last of 1,000 managers (0.1 percentile) for treating people with respect, but earning $4M a year, so work performance was no lever. Goldsmith instead phoned Mike's wife, then his two sons, live: all agreed he was a jerk at home too. Mike committed to change — not for money or the firm, but because he'd be ashamed if his sons got the same feedback in twenty years. Within a year his respect scores passed the 50th percentile. **Demonstrates: reach people where they live, not where they work.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Get the Four Commitments — let go of the past, tell the truth, be supportive not cynical, pick one thing to improve in yourself — before you collect a single data point.
2. Let the person being rated pick the raters (with their boss's sign-off); it kills the "wrong people" defense.
3. The only feedback question that works is "How can I do better?" — advice, future-facing, and implying you'll act.
4. Treat all feedback as a gift: say "Thank you," then shut up. Never counter with your opinion.
5. When you can't buy confidential 360s, run the five observational drills — remark lists, sound off, sentence completion, auditing your boasts, and checking your behavior at home.
6. Mine the fourth Johari pane: what's known to others and unknown to you is the only pane worth the pain.
7. Feedback is the baseline, not the cure. Next comes apologizing.
