# Coda: You Are Here Now (+ Appendix)

## Core Idea
Goldsmith won't define your "there" — but you can, by interviewing your 95-year-old self before your last breath and doing whatever that person tells you. Dying people and high-potential young leaders independently give the same three answers: find meaning and happiness now, treasure friends and family, follow your dreams.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 95-Year-Old Exercise**: Imagine you're 95 and about to die, and are given the gift of traveling back to advise the person reading this page.
  - When to use: To define your "there" — the destination the book's title presumes but never specifies.
  - How: (1) Take a breath, then a deeper one. (2) Answer on two levels — **personal advice** and **professional advice**. (3) Jot down the words the old you would say. (4) "Just do whatever you wrote down." Make it your resolution for the rest of this year and the next. You have now defined your "there."
  - Why it works: It routes around goal obsession (Ch 5) by sourcing the criteria from the only vantage point where the trade-offs are fully priced. Failure mode: treating it as a reflective exercise rather than an instruction list — the whole method is that step 4 is non-optional.

- **The Three Recurring Deathbed Themes** (from a friend's interviews with the dying), each with a direct workplace instruction:
  1. **Reflect upon life; find happiness and meaning now** — not next month, not next year.
  2. **Friends and family** — at 95, very few coworkers will be at your bedside. Appreciate them now.
  3. **Follow your dreams** — big and small. The key question is not "Did I make all my dreams come true?" but "**Did I try?**"

- **The Accenture high-potential study as convergent evidence**: 200+ high-potential leaders from 120 companies (two nominees per company), asked "If you stay in this company, why are you going to stay?" Top three answers: (1) finding meaning and happiness now, the work is exciting; (2) the people — "this feels like a team, it feels like a family"; (3) "I can follow my dreams." **The answers were never about money** — and were identical to the deathbed answers.

## Key Concepts
- **"There"**: Your destination, which Goldsmith refuses to define, dictate, or judge — doing so would be presumptuous and none of his business (see "mission neutral," Ch 13).
- **The Great Western Disease**: The belief encoded in the phrase *"I will be happy when . . ."* — when I get the promotion, the house, the money.
- **Look behind, not ahead**: Deriving present priorities from a retrospective vantage point rather than a forecast.
- **Appreciating what you have**: Many older people report being so wrapped up in what they lacked that they never enjoyed what they had.
- **Retention is not compensation**: The stated reasons top talent stays are meaning, relationships, and dreams — a direct answer to the "I know what they want" prejudice of Ch 14.
- **Global Leadership Inventory**: The Appendix instrument — a 360-style rated leadership questionnaire, not a survey of the book's twenty habits.

## Mental Models
- **Use the 95-year-old as your advisory board when a goal starts running you**: the only advisor who already knows which of your current priorities were noise.
- **Think of "I will be happy when . . ." as a disease name, not a plan**: the next promotion or corner office "won't really change your world that much."
- **Use "Did I try?" instead of "Did I achieve it?" as the scoring rule for dreams**: some dreams will always elude you; the attempt is the measurable part.
- **Think of the deathbed list and the high-potential retention list as the same list**: what makes a life worth living and what makes talent stay are the same three things.

## Anti-patterns
- **Deferring happiness to the next milestone**: the Great Western Disease; the milestone arrives and the feeling doesn't.
- **Assuming your coworkers are your people**: "very few of your fellow employees will be there waving good-bye."
- **Letting others' opinions veto your version of a well-lived life**: if your dream looks goofy or offbeat to them — "It isn't their life. It's yours."
- **Paying to retain talent that wants meaning**: money was never among the top three reasons high-potentials stay.

## Worked Example
*Two populations, one answer set.* Goldsmith's friend interviewed people who were dying and asked what advice they'd give their younger selves; three themes recurred — find happiness and meaning now, cherish friends and family, follow your dreams. Separately, Goldsmith's Accenture project asked 200+ of the most mobile young stars in 120 global companies — people who could jump to better pay in a moment — the mercenary-sounding question of why they'd stay. They answered: the work is exciting and I find meaning now; these people are my friends, I could make more money elsewhere but I don't want to leave; this place lets me do what I really want to do in life. **Demonstrates that the "soft" priorities the dying name are not a consolation prize for people out of options — they are what the highest-leverage people in the workforce optimize for while they still have every option. Retention and a well-lived life have the same levers.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Do the 95-year-old exercise in writing, on both the personal and professional level — then execute what you wrote as this year's resolution.
2. Name the Great Western Disease when you hear yourself say "I will be happy when . . ."
3. Audit whom you're actually investing in: the people at your deathbed are friends and family, not the org chart.
4. Score dreams on "Did I try?" — and count small dreams (the car, the trip, the piano, the language).
5. If you lead people, stop pricing retention in money. Ask what makes the work meaningful, whether they have friends here, and whether you're enabling their dreams.
6. Don't look ahead — look back from your old age at the life you hope to live. "You are here. You can get there!"

## Reference Tables

**What the Appendix actually is:** not a summary, not a habit checklist, and not a "mini-survey" or "Total Quality" instrument — those exact terms do not appear. It is a single reproduced assessment titled the **Global Leadership Inventory**, developed as part of the same Accenture-sponsored research project (200 specially selected high-potential leaders, 120 companies worldwide). It is a **rated leadership questionnaire in 360 format**: the stem reads "Consider your own (or this person's) effectiveness in the following areas. How satisfied are you with the way he or she (or you) . . ." — so a single form serves both self-assessment and rating another leader.

- **Scale**: five-point, **Highly Satisfied → Highly Dissatisfied**.
- **Length**: 72 numbered behavioral items, grouped under 15 named dimensions, followed by a free-text section.
- **Dimensions** (in order): Thinking Globally · Appreciating Diversity · Developing Technological Savvy · Building Partnerships · Sharing Leadership · Creating a Shared Vision · Developing People · Empowering People · Achieving Personal Mastery · Encouraging Constructive Dialogue · Demonstrates Integrity · Leading Change · Anticipating Opportunities · Ensuring Customer Satisfaction · Maintaining a Competitive Advantage.
- **Item style**: concrete observable behaviors, e.g. "Recognizes the impact of globalization on our business," "Treats coworkers as partners, not competitors," "Holds people accountable for their results," "Achieves results that lead to long-term shareholder value."
- **Written Comments**: a closing open-response section asking for strengths (two or three specifics) and for suggested areas of improvement.
- **How to use it**: as the instrument behind Ch 6's feedback step and Ch 13's Rule 2 — it produces the per-behavior percentages you rank to pick the single flaw worth attacking.
