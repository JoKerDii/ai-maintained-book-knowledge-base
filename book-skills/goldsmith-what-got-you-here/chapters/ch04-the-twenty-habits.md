# Chapter 4: The Twenty Habits (Knowing What to Stop)

## Core Idea
The behaviors holding successful people back are not deficits of skill, intelligence, or personality — they are twenty transactional interpersonal habits, and the fix is subtraction, not addition. Drucker's line is the whole chapter: "We spend a lot of time teaching leaders what to do. We don't spend enough time teaching leaders what to stop."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The "To Stop" List**: Replace your usual "To Do" list with a "To Stop" list.
  - When to use: Any time you're setting improvement goals for yourself or a direct report and the instinct is to add a new positive behavior.
  - How: Write down behaviors to cease, not actions to initiate. Then whittle the list to the *one or two vital issues* — don't work twenty.
  - Why it works: Organizations reward only doing (you get credit for punctuality, never for stopping your lateness), so the highest-leverage improvements are systematically invisible and therefore unclaimed. Avoiding a bad deal can move the bottom line more than closing a big one.

- **Shifting into Neutral (the empty-box model)**: Behavior isn't only positive or negative — some is simply *neutral*, and neutral is the goal.
  - When to use: When the feedback is "you need to be nicer / more approachable / more collaborative."
  - How: Don't try to become a nicer person (a dozen simultaneous positive acts = a personality makeover nobody completes). Instead **stop being a jerk** — an act of omission. Weak idea in a meeting: say nothing. Someone challenges your decision: say nothing. Someone tells you what you already know: thank them and say nothing.
  - Why it works: Becoming nicer means *filling* a box with small positive acts and waiting for people to notice it's full. Ceasing to be a jerk just means leaving the box *empty of negatives*. No new skills, training, or creativity required.

- **The Four Gate Questions (before you speak)**: Ask, before any comment: (1) Will this comment help our customers? (2) Will this comment help our company? (3) Will this comment help the person I'm talking to? (4) Will this comment help the person I'm talking about?
  - When to use: The candor moment — when the destructive or "helpful" remark is queued up and it happens to be true.
  - How: If the answer is no, don't say it. The question is not "Is it true?" but "Is it worth it?"

- **The Fallacy of Added Value** (Habit #2): You improve the idea by 5 percent and cut the person's commitment to executing it by 50 percent, because you've taken away their ownership.
  - When to use: Every time "Great idea, but…" is forming in your mouth.
  - How: Cut your response off at "idea." Before speaking, take a breath and ask if it's worth saying — one CEO client found half of what he was about to say wasn't.

- **Monetize the habit**: Have a colleague, assistant, or spouse charge you cash ($10–$20) each time you commit the target behavior.
  - How: Announce it publicly, pay immediately, let people bait you. Goldsmith went from the 8th to the 96th percentile on "avoids destructive comments" this way; a COO burned $420 on "no/but/however" inside an hour.

## Key Concepts
- **What to stop vs. what to do**: Organizations reward acts of commission and never honor cessation, so the stopping half of improvement goes untaught.
- **Neutrality / the empty box**: The target state is the absence of negatives, not the accumulation of positives.
- **Transactional flaws**: These are things one person does *to* others — not skill gaps, IQ gaps, or clinical personality issues.
- **The higher you go, the more your problems are behavioral**: At the top everyone is technically competent, so people skills become the only remaining differentiator.
- **Mission Neutral**: The coach's stance — neither approving nor disapproving, like a doctor who fixes the leg without asking how you broke it.
- **The fallacy of added value**: Marginal content improvement bought with disproportionate loss of ownership and commitment.
- **"Is it worth it?" over "Is it true?"**: Truth is not a license for a destructive comment.
- **There is never anyone in the other boat**: Your anger is your own flaw, not the other person's doing.

## Mental Models
- **Think of "being nicer" as an empty box, not a full one** — you win by removing, not adding.
- **Use the four gate questions when candor feels justified** — truth that helps no customer, no company, no listener, and no absent third party is just damage.
- **Think of your improvement effort as a whittling exercise**: check yourself against all twenty, discard the ones only one in twenty people mention, and work the one or two that sixteen in twenty flag.
- **Treat every incoming idea as if you were Switzerland** — for one week, respond only "Thank you" or "You've given me something to think about."

## Anti-patterns
- **Trying to fix six habits at once**: Nobody sustains that many simultaneous interpersonal changes; you get a failed personality makeover instead of one real change.
- **Self-diagnosis**: People overrate their weaknesses as badly as their strengths — they see cancer where a professional sees a muscle pull. And a tic that doesn't bother your colleagues isn't a problem worth fixing.
- **Treating these as skill, intelligence, or personality flaws**: Coaching can't teach you to hit a curveball, raise your IQ, or replace psychiatry. It only fixes behavior.
- **Justifying destructive comments with candor**: Candor becomes a weapon the moment "it's true" becomes the standard.
- **Adding value from the top of the org**: The higher you go, the more your job is making others winners rather than winning yourself.

## Worked Example
*Katzenbach at dinner.* Goldsmith watched Jon Katzenbach — ex-McKinsey director, one of the world's leading authorities on team building — plot a venture with his brilliant partner Niko Canner. Every time Niko floated an idea, Jon cut in: "That's a great idea, but it would work better if you…" followed by a war story. Niko would resume; Jon would interrupt again, back and forth like a long rally. Goldsmith finally broke his own rule about staying quiet: "Jon, will you please be quiet and let Niko talk. Stop trying to add value to the discussion." **What it demonstrates: adding value is the need to win wearing a helpful face — and expertise is no protection. The man who wrote the book on teams couldn't stop stepping on his own partner's ownership of an idea, improving the content 5 percent while cutting the commitment 50 percent.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Start a "To Stop" list instead of a "To Do" list; nobody will applaud, and it will matter more than everything else combined.
2. Aim for neutral. Ceasing to be a jerk is an act of omission and far easier than becoming a nicer person.
3. The higher you go, the more your problems are behavioral — technical excellence stops differentiating you at the top.
4. These are transactional flaws, not flaws of skill, intelligence, or unchangeable personality — which is exactly why they're fixable.
5. Before any comment, run the four gates: customers, company, person I'm talking to, person I'm talking about. If all no, don't say it.
6. Kill "Great idea, but…" — cut the sentence off at "idea." Take a breath first; half of what you were going to say isn't worth saying.
7. Don't self-diagnose and don't work the whole list. Whittle to one or two issues that many people independently name.
