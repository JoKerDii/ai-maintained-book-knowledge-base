# Chapter 13: Changing: The Rules

## Core Idea
Habitual winners "stack the deck in their favor" — they avoid high-risk, low-reward situations rather than heroically overcoming obstacles. Eight rules keep you from unstacking the deck against yourself when you attempt behavioral change.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Eight Rules of Changing** (Goldsmith introduces them as "the following seven rules" but numbers eight — cite them by number and name):
  1. **You Might Not Have a Disease That Behavioral Change Can Cure**
  2. **Pick the Right Thing to Change**
  3. **Don't Delude Yourself About What You Really Must Change**
  4. **Don't Hide from the Truth You Need to Hear**
  5. **There Is No Ideal Behavior**
  6. **If You Can Measure It, You Can Achieve It**
  7. **Monetize the Result, Create a Solution**
  8. **The Best Time to Change Is Now**
  - When to use: Before committing to a change program — as a pre-flight check on whether you've picked a real, fixable, worth-fixing problem.
  - How: Run each rule as a gate. Is this behavioral or a skill/technical gap (1)? Is it the highest-percentage flaw (2)? Am I honest about time, effort, and payoff (3)? Am I soliciting the feedback I'm dodging (4)? Am I chasing a composite ideal (5)? Can I count it (6)? Can I attach money to it (7)? Am I waiting for a calmer month that will never arrive (8)?

- **Pick the Right Thing to Change — the pure numbers game** (Rule 2): Rank documented flaws by the percentage of coworkers who name them and attack only the top one.
  - When to use: The moment feedback yields a menu of flaws and the successful person wants to fix all of them.
  - How: If 10% say you don't listen, 20% say you miss deadlines, 40% say you gossip, and 80% say you get angry — anger is the no-brainer. Everything else is "moot, off the radar."
  - Why it works: Successful people overcommit and, given unlimited options, maximize themselves into paralysis. One target removes the choice. Failure mode: people reliably ignore the in-your-face 80% problem and pick an easier flaw — the path of least resistance.

- **The five miscalculations of goal achievement** (Rule 3), drawn from diet/fitness research: **Time**, **Effort**, **Distractions**, **Rewards**, **Maintenance**.
  - When to use: Before committing, to face the reality of effort and payoff.
  - Why it works: The failure isn't willpower, it's forecasting. Note *Rewards* especially: after you improve, people don't immediately love the new you — the payoff arrives late and smaller than expected.

- **"One gun, one bullet"** (Rule 5): You can't hit more than one target with the ammunition available for long-term behavioral change.
  - How: Pick one issue and attack it "until it doesn't matter anymore" — not until you're the best in the world at it.

## Key Concepts
- **Stacking the deck**: Deliberately choosing high-odds situations (best hires, best assistants, full preparation) instead of relying on grit.
- **Miswanting vs. mischoosing**: Wanting something that turns out not to make you happy vs. picking the wrong route to a legitimate goal.
- **Mission neutral**: Goldsmith takes no position on *what* you want (that would judge your reason for living) but strong positions on *how* you choose to get there.
- **Symptom vs. disease**: Feedback can surface a headache (temporary anger at a scapegoat) rather than a tumor; treat accordingly.
- **Skill problem vs. behavioral problem**: A gap a media coach or technology mentor fixes, not a behavior coach.
- **Benchmarking hazard**: The perfect benchmark human is a composite of many people and does not exist; "the desire for 'perfect' can drive away 'better.'"
- **Soft-side values**: Rudeness, politeness, asking for input, biting your tongue — hard to quantify but trackable, and measurement drives them.
- **Monetizing the punishment / monetizing the result**: Attaching a fine to the flaw vs. attaching a reward to the fix.
- **The busy-person mirage**: The dream that in two or three months life calms down and change becomes possible.

## Mental Models
- **Think of change like a golf short game**: 70% of shots happen within 100 yards of the pin, yet everyone practices drivers. Fix the high-frequency, small-muscle thing you're avoiding, not the glamorous one.
- **Use "one gun, one bullet" when tempted to fix everything**: improvement is not zero-sum — 20,000 feedback reports show getting better at X lifts everything else, so one change is enough.
- **Think of avoided feedback as an avoided annual physical**: not seeking bad news doesn't mean there isn't any. (Goldsmith dodged his for seven years.)
- **Use "What am I willing to change now?" when you catch yourself scheduling change for a quieter future**: sanity does not prevail; tomorrow will be as crazy as today.

## Anti-patterns
- **Coaching a skill gap as a behavior gap**: David, a beloved CFO, scored low on "listening" — the real problem was that he was bad at spinning the media, so staff read the bad press as him not hearing them. Wrong coach, wasted year.
- **Overcommitting to every flaw**: Show a successful person seven flaws and they'll take on all seven, then complete none.
- **Chasing the easy adjacent goal**: substituting a personal project for the interpersonal work the feedback actually demands.
- **Benchmarking yourself against a composite**: "ready, fire, aim" self-improvement. Michael Jordan, the benchmark for basketball, was a mediocre minor-league outfielder.
- **Measuring everything at work and nothing in the soft-side or home domains**: what goes uncounted goes undone.

## Worked Example
*Matt, a CFO with more power than any predecessor, had become brusque, dismissive, and inaccessible.* Goldsmith opened with "we need to make changes"; Matt cut him off — what he really wanted was to lose twenty pounds and get a six-pack, because that's what made him grouchy. Asked which was easier, a six-pack or one less interpersonal flaw, Matt said the six-pack: "just a matter of discipline." Goldsmith didn't argue with the goal — he attacked the forecast, walking Matt through time, effort, distractions, rewards, and maintenance. The point that landed: even a perfect physique carried no guaranteed payoff with colleagues; it might make him vainer, and because the project loudly excluded them, it could dig him *deeper* into the hole — one more instance of the self-involvement his feedback flagged. Then: "Now, are we ready to talk about what your colleagues think of you?" **Demonstrates Rule 3 — don't attack the goal, attack the delusion about its cost and payoff; the quick fix is rarely the lasting fix.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Before coaching yourself, confirm the problem is behavioral. Symptoms, skill gaps, and scapegoating look identical to flaws in a feedback report.
2. Choose by percentages. The flaw 80% of colleagues name is the only one that matters this cycle.
3. Separate wanting from choosing — be mission neutral on the goal, ruthless about the route.
4. Forecast honestly across time, effort, distractions, rewards, and maintenance before committing.
5. Stop hiding from the feedback you most need (including at home: "What can I do to be a better partner?").
6. Abandon the ideal. One gun, one bullet — attack one issue until it stops mattering. Improvement is not zero-sum.
7. Count it and you'll do it (Goldsmith's family-time log went 92 → 110 → 131 → 135 days), and monetize it when you need leverage — one client promised his assistant a $2,000 bonus if the coaching worked. She collected in 12 months.
