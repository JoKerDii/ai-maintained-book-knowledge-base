# Chapter 10: Thanking

## Core Idea
Thanking is the fourth step of the change process and the one universal skill that never backfires: gratitude is a genuine emotion people *expect* when they help you, and "thank you" is the only response to feedback that cannot escalate a conflict. Master it before attempting follow-up and feedforward.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"Thank you" as a closing device**: Saying "Thank you" "creates closure in any potentially explosive discussion."
  - When to use: The instant someone gives you feedback, criticism, an idea, or a suggestion — especially when you disagree.
  - How: Say it and stop. Add nothing. The only available reply is "You're welcome."
  - Why it works: It disarms the other party structurally. After a thank you, no one can argue with you, prove you wrong, trump you, get angry, or ignore you. It's the perfect response because it forecloses every escalation route while remaining gracious.

- **The 25-Names Gratitude Drill** (the thank-you-note exercise):
  - When to use: Now — Goldsmith says do it before reading another chapter.
  - How: (1) Think across your whole career. (2) Ask "Who are the people most responsible for my success?" (3) Write down the first 25 names that come to mind. (4) Ask of each: "Have I ever told them how grateful I am for their help?" (5) Write each one a thank you note.
  - Why it works: Beyond making both parties feel good, it forces two admissions you otherwise avoid — that you did not achieve success alone, and (its "side benefit") that every name marks an area where you *needed help*, i.e. a map of your old weak spots, which may still be weaker than you think.

- **Give yourself an A+ in gratitude**: Treat gratitude as an asset to be scored, not a mood.
  - How: Thank people in emails, letters, sessions. End phone calls with "thank you" rather than "good-bye," and mean it. Goldsmith's extreme version: a list of the top 25 people in his personal and professional life, each sent a gold-embossed certificate naming them among the 25 who helped him.

## Key Concepts
- **Gratitude**: A genuine emotion, not an abstraction — it cannot be expected or exacted; you either feel it or you don't.
- **The withholding penalty**: People who do something nice for you expect gratitude and "think less of you" if you withhold it.
- **Gratitude read as submission**: Because expressing it can feel slightly humiliating or submissive, it's one of the last things children learn — and one successful adults skip.
- **Thank-you as punctuation**: The formulaic workplace "thank you" that ends a call really means "we're done here" — and people obey it, which is evidence of the phrase's polite power.
- **The connoisseur of gratitude**: Someone who has made thanking a deliberate, radical, everyday practice.
- **The side benefit of a thank you note**: Naming who helped you pinpoints the deficiency that made the help necessary.
- **Gratitude as talent**: Expressing it well travels with wisdom, self-knowledge, and maturity — it is learnable, not innate.

## Mental Models
- **Think of "thank you" as the perfect response**: It is the only reply to feedback that cannot be countered, escalated, or argued with. When in doubt, use it.
- **Use the 25 names as a weakness audit**: Every person you owe thanks to marks a place you could not do it alone.
- **Think of gratitude's absence as a major interpersonal flaw** — not as neutral. Silence is not neutral; it reads as ingratitude.
- **Use "thank you" as your default speaking turn while receiving feedback**: keep your mouth shut except to say it (this is the bridge to Ch 12's feedforward ground rule).

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting for a crisis to feel grateful**: The regret is predictable — the number one regret children have when parents die is never having told them.
- **Qualifying the thank you**: "Thanks, but…" or "Thanks, though actually…" reopens the debate the thank you just closed.
- **Assuming it's too obvious to bother with**: Telling people to write thank you notes is "obvious, almost trite" — yet the practice is astonishingly neglected. None of us do it enough.
- **Thanking only up the hierarchy**: Gratitude aimed only at powerful people reads as politics, not gratitude.

## Worked Example
*The plane that couldn't land.* On a flight to Santa Barbara the plane dropped hard; the pilot announced the landing gear had failed and they would circle until the fuel ran out, then land wheels-up. Circling and thinking "I might die," Goldsmith asked himself what he regretted — and the answer was how many people had been good to him whom he had never adequately thanked. He promised himself he would fix it if he got down. The plane landed; he thanked the crew, went to his hotel room, and wrote "gushy, mushy" thank you notes to at least 50 people who had helped him in his life. That was the moment he became a virtuoso at thanking.

*The 20-year-late note.* A lawyer friend lost a case but was praised by the judge for the writing in his briefs. He credited an undergraduate English professor who had drilled him to write succinctly. The judge asked, "Did you ever thank him?" He hadn't — in twenty years. He wrote that night. The professor, mid-way through a pile of term papers and doubting the point of grading them, wrote back that the note reminded him his work has worth.

**Demonstrates: gratitude is never wasted and never too late — a thank you note costs nothing, arrives as a gift on the other end, and "if you can get an A+ in gratitude, nothing bad will ever come of it. Only good."**

## Key Takeaways
1. Use "thank you" as your only response to feedback — it creates closure and cannot be argued with.
2. Do the drill: 25 names, one note each, before moving on.
3. Read the 25 names as a diagnosis of the weaknesses that required help.
4. Score yourself on gratitude as an asset; its absence is a major interpersonal flaw.
5. Don't wait for a near-death moment or a funeral to say it — the regret is fully predictable.
6. Thanking is the prerequisite skill for follow-up (Ch 11) and feedforward (Ch 12); both collapse without it.
