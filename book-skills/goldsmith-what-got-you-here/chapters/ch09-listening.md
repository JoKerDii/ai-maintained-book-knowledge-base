# Chapter 9: Listening

## Core Idea
Listening is not passive — it's "a highly active process—with every muscle engaged, especially the brain," and 80 percent of our success in learning from other people is decided by how well we do it. The single skill that separates the great from the near-great is the ability to make another person feel like the most important person in the room, and roughly ninety percent of that skill is listening.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The three things all good listeners do**:
  1. **Think before you speak.**
  2. **Listen with respect.**
  3. **Ask yourself, "Is it worth it?"**
  - When to use: Every interpersonal encounter, not just the ones you've graded as important.
  - How:
    - *Think before you speak* — keeping your mouth shut is an active choice, hardest exactly when you're angry, upset, confused, or shocked, which is when most people talk. Listening is a two-part maneuver: the part where you listen, and the part where you speak. **What you say is proof of how well you listened**; speaking is what establishes how you're perceived as a listener.
    - *Listen with respect* — it isn't enough to keep your ears open; you must demonstrate total engagement. Proving you can recite someone's words back while shuffling papers wins the argument and loses the person: they conclude the problem is deeper than inattention.
    - *Is it worth it?* — ask it before speaking, because it forces you to consider what the other person will feel afterward, and to **play at least two moves ahead**: (a) how they regard you, (b) what they'll do afterwards, (c) how they'll behave next time you talk.
  - Why it works: These disciplines "don't require nuance. We just need to do them." Most workplace friction is formulaic — you say something that ticks me off, I lash back — and the content is irrelevant; only the behavioral pattern matters.

- **"Is it worth it?" as a leadership filter**: A COO (later CEO) spent 18 months on becoming a better listener and open-minded boss. His one takeaway: "Before speaking, I take a breath and ask myself one question, 'Is it worth it?' I learned that **50 percent of what I was going to say was correct—maybe—but saying it wasn't worth it**."
  - Why it works / failure mode: Responding without the filter triggers a three-part chain: (1) they are hurt; (2) they harbor ill feelings toward whoever inflicted the hurt — i.e., they hate you; (3) by negative reinforcement they won't speak up next time. Sustained, this costs you your reputation as a leader and shrinks your knowledge base.

- **The most-important-person-in-the-room skill**: "The ability to make a person feel that, when you're with that person, he or she is the most important (and the only) person in the room is the skill that separates the great from the near-great."
  - How: Show interest, ask questions, and — critically — **wait for the answers** without distraction. Know something positive about the person and mention it, so you are in effect "bragging about you *to* you." Treat everyone equally instead of ranking encounters A, B, or C.
  - Why we fail: Not ignorance — everyone already believes this. "We forget. We get distracted. We don't have the mental discipline to make it automatic." The great ones simply do it all the time; there's no on/off switch for caring, empathy, and respect.

- **The count-to-fifty drill**: Close your eyes and count slowly to fifty without letting a single other thought intrude. More than half of Goldsmith's clients can't — around twenty or thirty, work, kids, or last night's dinner invades.
  - When to use: As both diagnostic and daily training, like touching your toes to test and improve flexibility.
  - Why: "If you can't listen to yourself… as you count to fifty, how will you ever be able to listen to another person?"

- **The active-listening test drive** (tiny tactics for your next encounter):
  - Listen. Don't interrupt. Don't finish the other person's sentences. Don't say "I knew that." Don't even agree with them (if they praise you, just say "Thank you"). Don't use the words "no," "but," and "however." Don't be distracted or let your eyes wander. Maintain your end by asking intelligent questions that show attention, move the conversation forward, and require *them* to talk. Eliminate any striving to impress them with how smart or funny you are — your only aim is to let them feel they're accomplishing that.

## Key Concepts
- **Listening as an active process**: Every muscle engaged, especially the brain — telling your brain and mouth *not* to do something is no different from telling them to do it.
- **The two-part maneuver**: Listening is the part where you listen plus the part where you speak; the second is the evidence for the first.
- **The negative two-fer**: While composing your reply you both fail to hear the person and construct a comment that may annoy them.
- **"Is it worth it?"**: The pre-speech question that turns "What's in it for me?" into "What's in it for him?"
- **Listening with respect**: Demonstrated engagement, not merely accurate reception.
- **The 80 percent rule**: Like Nicklaus's grip and stance, 80 percent of the outcome is settled before you exert a muscle.
- **The listening paradox**: "The more you subsume your desire to shine, the more you will shine in the other person's eyes."

## Mental Models
- **Think of a conversation as chess**: replying without asking "Is it worth it?" is a beginner's game where no one thinks beyond the move in front of them.
- **Use the receiving-line test**: if listening with respect were passive, it wouldn't make you sweat through 500 handshakes.
- **Think of your best behavior as a template, not an exception**: you already do this on a first date, on a sales call, and with your boss. The only difference between the near-great and the great is that the great do it all the time.
- **Use "Thank you" as the default response** — even to praise, even to disagreement — instead of rating, arguing, or adding your two cents.

## Anti-patterns
- **Composing your reply while they talk**: You miss the content and manufacture a response that misses the point, adds meaningless value, or injects a destructive tone. Keep it up and no one will talk to you at all.
- **Winning the "you're not listening to me" argument**: Reciting a verbatim playback proves multitasking and confirms you're a jerk.
- **Ripping someone's idea in a meeting**: They're hurt, they resent you, and they stop speaking up — you lose their performance and their ideas.
- **Ranking encounters A, B, C**: Switching attentiveness on for important people announces exactly where everyone else stands. Everyone eventually notices.
- **Treating listening as charm technique**: This isn't seduction jargon or body-language leverage; it's active listening with a purpose.

## Worked Example
*David Boies at the Spark's Steakhouse bar.* Goldsmith's friend Tom — vice chairman of a 300-lawyer New York firm, not easily impressed — was waiting for a table with his partner Dave when superstar attorney David Boies walked in, recognized Dave from past trials, and joined them for a drink. Dave stepped outside for a phone call that turned very long. Boies stayed and talked with Tom, a stranger, for 45 minutes. There was no angle: different practices, essentially zero chance their paths would cross or that either could help the other. Tom's account: "I wasn't bowled over by his intelligence, or his piercing questions, or his anecdotes. What impressed me was that when he asked a question, he waited for the answer. He not only listened, he made me feel like I was the only person in the room."

**Demonstrates the whole chapter in thirteen words: the skill isn't brilliance, it's waiting for the answer — and the great practise it precisely when there is nothing to gain, which is why it reads as genuine and why people remember it for years.**

Contrast case: the aging London executive — short, jowly, overweight, bald, past seventy — always dining with extraordinary women. Asked why she was enthralled, one answered that he never takes his eyes off her: even if the Queen walked in, he wouldn't be distracted. **Undivided attention outperforms every conventional advantage.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat listening as active: think before you speak, listen with respect, and ask "Is it worth it?"
2. Ask "Is it worth it?" before every response — roughly half of what you were going to say is correct but not worth saying.
3. Play two moves ahead: how they'll regard you, what they'll do after, whether they'll speak up next time.
4. Speaking is the proof of listening — what you say establishes how well you were perceived to listen.
5. Make the other person feel like the most important person in the room, and do it with everyone, all the time, not just when it pays.
6. Train concentration with the count-to-fifty drill until no thought intrudes.
7. Run the tactics list: don't interrupt, don't finish sentences, don't say "I knew that," ban "no," "but," and "however," and say "Thank you" instead of rating the comment.
