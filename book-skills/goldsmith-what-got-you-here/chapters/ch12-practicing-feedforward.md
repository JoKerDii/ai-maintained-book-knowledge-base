# Chapter 12: Practicing Feedforward

## Core Idea
Feedforward is "feedback going in the opposite direction": instead of reporting on how you functioned in the past, it delivers ideas you can put into practice in the future. "If feedback is past tense, then feedforward is future perfect." The entire method is: ask for two ideas, listen, say thank you, repeat.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Feedforward — the four steps**:
  1. **Pick the one behavior** you'd like to change that would make a significant, positive difference in your life. (E.g., *I want to be a better listener.*)
  2. **Describe this objective in a one-on-one dialogue with *anyone* you know** — spouse, kids, boss, best friend, coworker, even a stranger. The person chosen is irrelevant; they need be an expert neither on the subject nor on you.
  3. **Ask that person for two suggestions *for the future*** that might help you achieve the change. The only ground rule with someone who knows you: **no mention of the past. Everything is about the future.** ("Would you suggest two ideas that I can implement in the future that will help me become a better listener?")
  4. **Listen attentively.** Take notes if you like. **You may not judge, rate, or critique the suggestions in any way** — you can't even say "That's a good idea." **The only permitted response is "Thank you."**
  - Then repeat with someone else. You are not limited to one person; restricting it to one would lessen your chance of an accurate picture, exactly as with initial feedback. Feedforward "is a process that never needs to stop."
  - Origin: grew out of a discussion with Jon Katzenbach in the early 1990s, out of frustration with feedback mechanisms that forced people to relive the past.

- **Why feedforward works** (Goldsmith's reasons):
  1. Successful people don't like criticism but **love getting ideas for the future** — they have a high need for self-determination and accept ideas about concerns they "own" while rejecting ideas that feel "forced" on them.
  2. **We can change the future but not the past.**
  3. **Helping people be "right" is more productive than proving them "wrong"** — it focuses on solutions, not problems.
  4. **People do not take feedforward as personally as feedback** — it's hard to be offended by a suggestion aimed at something you asked to improve, especially when you're not forced to implement it.
  5. You **focus on hearing without having to worry about responding** — when all you can say is "thank you," you don't compose a clever reply, can't interrupt, and become a more patient listener.
  6. It **forces us to ask**, enlarging your universe of people with useful ideas.
  7. **Asking gives the other person a license to answer** — most people want to help but hold back, thinking it rude or intrusive to help someone who didn't ask.
  8. **There's no threat of pain to the giver** — they receive only gratitude, not resentment, argument, or punishment.
  9. **The giver doesn't have to be right** — they needn't prove the idea is good, because you're not judging it; you can accept or ignore it. It "eliminates fear and defensiveness."
  10. It **overcomes the two biggest obstacles to negative feedback**: bosses in dominant positions don't want to hear it (they prefer praise no matter what they say), and subordinates rarely want to give it (criticizing the boss is rarely a great career move).
  11. It creates **two-way traffic** — two colleagues helping each other rather than a superior delivering a critique.

## Key Concepts
- **Feedforward**: Ideas for the future, solicited two at a time, received in silence with thanks.
- **Future perfect vs. past tense**: Feedback is history — facts about the past but not necessarily ideas for the future.
- **"They do"**: The book's inalterable concept — *I* don't establish what you need to change, *you* don't either; **they** do. Everyone around you who knows you, cares about you, has you pegged.
- **The stranger as ideal partner**: Someone with no past with you cannot hold your past failings against you or even bring them up.
- **Specific over generic**: A coach's advice on listening is true, supportable, and undisputable — but generic. The people around you know more about how you listen than anyone else in the world.
- **License to answer**: The permission your asking confers on someone who wanted to help but felt it intrusive.
- **Leave it at the stream**: The discipline of not carrying the past forward.
- **"Look at the road, not the wall"**: What race car drivers are taught — steer toward where you want to go.

## Mental Models
- **Use feedforward whenever feedback would turn into a debate**: In frank talk we assume we're locked in a debate we must win, licensed to use every trick including dredging up the past. The ground rules remove the debate.
- **Think of feedforward as the conversation that *should* happen all day long in every workplace** — and rarely does, purely because the three restrictions (two ideas, listen, thank you) are absent.
- **Use "they do" as your compass**: Until everyone affected by your behavior is on your side and working to help you change, you haven't really begun to get better.
- **Think of feedback as reading history and feedforward as writing the plan**: You need both — feedback to scour the past and identify room for improvement, feedforward to change.

## Anti-patterns
- **Evaluating the suggestion** — even positively. "That's a good idea" breaks the rule; it turns the giver into a candidate being graded and reintroduces judgment.
- **Bringing up the past** — the one absolute prohibition; its strongest element is that it never permits the past.
- **Asking only one person**: Dramatically lessens the accuracy of the picture.
- **Waiting for an expert**: Insisting on a qualified source forfeits the specific, concrete ideas only the people around you can give.
- **Team sessions as "let me tell you what you did wrong" slugfests** rather than "let me ask you what we can do better" love-ins — this is where organizational time, productivity, and morale are lost.

## Worked Example
*The two monks and the bride.* Two monks walking home find a bride in her gown weeping by a stream, afraid to cross and ruin the dress. Their sect forbids touching women, but one monk carries her across, then returns to his companion. The second monk lectures him furiously the entire way back to the monastery. That night he wakes the first monk to resume the scolding: "How could you carry that woman?" The sleepy monk asks, "What woman?" Reminded, he laughs: "I only carried her across the stream. You carried her all the way back to the monastery."

**Demonstrates: the past is a weight you choose to keep carrying. Feedforward's structural advantage is that it makes carrying impossible — with no mention of the past permitted, both parties leave it at the stream, and the conversation can only move forward.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the loop: **pick one behavior → tell anyone → ask for two future ideas → listen → say "thank you" → repeat with someone else.**
2. Never mention the past, and never judge, rate, or praise a suggestion.
3. Ask many people; feedforward never needs to stop.
4. The source needn't be an expert on the topic or on you — strangers may be ideal.
5. Successful people reject criticism but devour ideas for the future; use that asymmetry.
6. Asking gives others a license to help they were otherwise too polite to use.
7. **They** decide what you need to change — not you, not a coach. Get everyone affected on your side or you haven't started.
