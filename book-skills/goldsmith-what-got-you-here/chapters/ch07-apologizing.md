# Chapter 7: Apologizing

## Core Idea
Apologizing is "the most magical, healing, restorative gesture human beings can make" — the centerpiece of behavioral change, because without it there is no admission of the mistake, no announcement of intent to change, and no emotional contract with the people you care about. The apology is also the shortest move in the book: two sentences, then silence.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Magic Move (the apology)**: Borrowed from *Harvey Penick's Little Red Book*, where the "magic move" is the weight transfer in the golf swing — master it and "you will hit the ball as if by magic." Apologizing is Goldsmith's equivalent: seemingly simple, hard for some, brilliant for those who can do it.
  - When to use: At the start of any change effort, immediately after feedback identifies what to fix — and in any personal relationship you've damaged. Cause and motive are irrelevant: "Whatever forces an apology out of people who normally cannot do it, I'm for it."
  - Why it works: It creates closure for both parties, and closure lets you move forward. It is also "impossible to resist a heartfelt apology."

- **The apology formula** (the complete instruction manual):
  1. You say, **"I'm sorry."**
  2. You add, **"I'll try to do better in the future."** — not absolutely necessary, but prudent: when you let go of the past, it's nice to hint at a brighter future.
  3. **And then… you say nothing.**
  - How: Don't explain it. Don't complicate it. Don't qualify it. Any extra word only dilutes it.
  - Why it works / failure mode: The apology's power is in its unhedged completeness; every added clause reads as a defense and converts contrition into rationalization. Failure mode has a name — see Morgan Stanley below.

- **Get in and get out as quickly as possible**: The only sound advice on apologizing. The sooner the apology is over, the sooner you move on to telling the world (Ch 8).

## Key Concepts
- **The magic move**: The apology, named after Penick's golf-swing fundamental — small, learnable, and the thing everything else is built on.
- **Emotional contract**: What "I'm sorry" writes between you and the people you care about — "in blood."
- **Closure**: The mutual sense of an ending that an apology gives both sides; the precondition for moving forward.
- **Diluting the apology**: Explaining, qualifying, or contextualizing it — the mechanism by which apologies fail.
- **Winking while you apologize**: Signalling to bystanders that you don't really mean it (Morgan Stanley's error).
- **Apologia**: A full written accounting that takes each charge in turn, concedes what is true, and refuses scapegoats.

## Mental Models
- **Think of the apology as the magic move**: a tiny mechanical fundamental that, once mastered, makes everything downstream work.
- **Use silence as the third sentence**: after "I'm sorry" and "I'll try to do better," every further word is a withdrawal from the account you just funded.
- **Think of contrition as an investment**: it costs you nothing — "not even your illusory pride" — but the return on investment "would make Warren Buffett green with envy."
- **Use "it's not what you say, it's what they hear"** (Red Auerbach) when tempted to defend your intent: what you remember is not the point; what they felt is.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rationalizing the apology afterward**: Morgan Stanley paid a $50 million fine over research conflicts of interest — the look and feel of an apology. The next day CEO Phil Purcell explained the firm had done nothing wrong, was paying only to end the matter, and wasn't as bad as competitors who paid bigger fines. He effectively bragged about the *smallest* fine. The SEC, the New York Attorney General, and the media all pounced. **A $50 million apology destroyed by one speech.**
- **Waiting to apologize**: Ted's silence let a 20-year friendship curdle for weeks; delay converted a small slight into a full indictment.
- **Excavating your childhood for causes**: "Looking backward, seeking out scapegoats, is a fool's errand." Promise to fix the behavior instead.
- **Being unable to say "I'm sorry" at all**: If you can't admit you're wrong, you have no mechanism for mending relationships or declaring intent to change.

## Worked Example
*Ted and Vince.* Ted was a model senior manager with one flaw: disastrous follow-up. He didn't return calls, never initiated contact, and paid attention only when there was business to be done — so every relationship started ecstatically and drifted into conflict. Coached at work through apologize–advertise–follow-up, he hadn't yet transferred it home. His neighbor and best friend of 20 years, Vince, called five times over two weeks with no callback, then stopped speaking to him. The wives brokered a plan: Ted would write a contrite letter. Ted, of course, didn't get around to it. Vince finally erupted in writing with three charges — never returning calls, ignoring him at a dinner party, never initiating contact.

Ted replied immediately, taking the charges one at a time: on the calls, "you are right, absolutely right… For this I apologize to you. And I will change that." On the dinner party, he conceded that what he remembered wasn't the point — what Vince felt was. On never initiating contact, he admitted it was the charge that pained him most because it was true and easily fixed, refused to blame his childhood, and asked forgiveness, proposing they aim not at the friendship as it was but as it should be. Vince returned the letter unopened; the wives got him to read it, and the friendship was repaired. Ted later told Goldsmith he could only apologize to Vince because he'd learned to do it at work — "Because I know it works."

**Demonstrates: the apology's power comes from conceding each charge without defense, and its reach extends past the office — the skill is portable, but only after it's been practiced.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Apologize first. Nothing in the change process works until the emotional contract exists.
2. The entire script is: "I'm sorry." "I'll try to do better in the future." Then nothing.
3. Don't explain, complicate, or qualify — every extra word dilutes the apology (see Purcell).
4. Concede what's true without hunting for causes or scapegoats; intent is irrelevant, impact is the point.
5. Do it fast — get in and get out — because the apology is only the gateway to advertising and follow-up.
6. Practise it at work and it becomes available where it matters most, at home.
