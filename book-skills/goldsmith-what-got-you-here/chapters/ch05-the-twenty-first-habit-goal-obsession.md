# Chapter 5: The Twenty-First Habit: Goal Obsession

## Core Idea
Goal obsession is not itself a flaw — it's "not transactional; it's not something you do to another person." It's the **creator** of flaws: the force that makes us pursue a goal so hard we sacrifice the larger mission it was supposed to serve, and that turns otherwise decent people into the twenty habits.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Goal obsession as a meta-habit**: The twenty-first habit gets its own chapter because it sits *underneath* the other twenty rather than beside them.
  - When to use: When someone's annoying behavior doesn't fit their character — a generous person hogging credit, an ethical person cutting corners. Look for the goal driving it before you address the behavior.
  - How: Trace the behavior back to the goal. Fix the goal (Candace's "be a superstar" → "be an effective leader") and the downstream behaviors correct themselves.
  - Why it works: The habits are symptoms. Treating a symptom while the distorting goal remains means the person substitutes a new bad behavior for the old one.

- **The two misunderstandings that produce it**:
  1. **Misunderstanding what we want in our lives** — chasing money, weight, the corner office, and neglecting the family, the health, or the colleagues the goal was for. "We start out with a road map heading in one direction but end up in the wrong town."
  2. **Misunderstanding what others want us to do** — the boss demands 10 percent growth, so we adopt "questionable, less than honest methods." The real goal was never the 10 percent; it was pleasing the boss, and we won't admit it.

- **The four classic conditions of goal obsession**: You are (1) under time pressure, (2) in a hurry, (3) doing something you've been told is important, (4) with people depending on you.
  - When to use: As a live alarm. Probable answer to "when am I in these conditions?" — *all the time*. They never go away, which is why the check must be habitual.
  - How: Step back, take a breath, and look. Ask "What am I doing?" and "Why am I doing this?" Then: "Am I achieving a task — and forgetting my organization's mission?"

## Key Concepts
- **Goal obsession**: Getting so wrapped up in achieving the goal that you achieve it at the expense of the larger mission.
- **Not a flaw, a creator of flaws**: It distorts exemplary talents and good intentions into something you no longer admire.
- **Task vs. mission**: The unit of the trap — perfect execution of the wrong-sized objective.
- **Great follow-through + short-sighted goal**: Goldsmith's recipe for disaster. Discipline amplifies the error.
- **"What have I done?"**: The dead-end realization you're trying to avoid arriving at.
- **Chasing the spotlight**: Goal obsession's most common workplace expression — needing to be front and center in every success.

## Mental Models
- **Think of goal obsession as the engine, the twenty habits as the exhaust** — you can wipe the exhaust or turn down the engine.
- **Use the River Kwai test when a project is going beautifully**: am I building a magnificent bridge for the enemy? Excellence at the task is no evidence you're serving the mission.
- **Think of "the conditions" as always-on, not occasional** — hurry, pressure, importance, and dependence are the permanent weather of professional life, so the reflection has to be scheduled, not triggered.

## Anti-patterns
- **Letting the goal define the person**: Candace's goal was "be a superstar," so credit-stealing followed logically. A goal stated as *status* rather than *contribution* will manufacture bad behavior.
- **Adopting someone else's number without examining it**: The honorable pursuit of a difficult goal set by someone else "transforms us into cheaters."
- **Working harder to fix a problem that working hard caused**: Mike, the Wall Street dealmaker — three divorces, three alimony checks, so now he genuinely does need the money, and works harder still.

## Worked Example
*Candace, the marketing executive.* Thirty-eight, happy marriage, two kids, two assistants, office full of "Marketing Executive of the Year" plaques, considered the CEO's eventual successor. One problem: her talented staff kept transferring out or leaving. Goldsmith interviewed her colleagues. Nobody faulted her ambition — they praised how clearly she set her goals. But her goal was to be a *superstar*, and it had worn the warmth out of her: "You could chill a six-pack of beer next to her heart." The universal complaint wasn't that she withheld praise — she showered her people with it. It was that when she reported a subordinate's win upward, she always took center stage. **What it demonstrates: goal obsession doesn't feel like a character flaw from inside — Candace was generous, capable, and delivering. Reframing the goal from "be a star" to "be an effective leader" makes credit-sharing rational rather than sacrificial, because her people's triumphs now count as evidence for her.**

Reinforcing cases: **Colonel Nicholson** in *The Bridge on the River Kwai* — a high-integrity officer builds a beautiful bridge for his captors, then defends it against his own side, and realizes only at the detonator: "What have I done?" He was building a bridge, not winning a war. And **Darley and Batson's 1973 Princeton study**: seminary students hurrying to deliver a sermon on the Good Samaritan literally stepped over a planted coughing "victim" — ninety percent of the ones told they were late ignored him.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat goal obsession as the root cause: when behavior is out of character, find the goal distorting it before you coach the behavior.
2. Audit the goal's *wording*. Status goals ("be a superstar," "make a lot of money") generate the twenty habits; mission goals don't.
3. Watch for goals set by others. If you'd cut a corner to hit the number, your real goal is pleasing the person who set it — say so out loud.
4. Learn the four conditions — time pressure, hurry, told-it's-important, people depending on you — and treat their presence as the cue to step back.
5. Run the pairing check regularly: making money but losing the family? On time for the sermon but not practicing it? Climbing but stomping on your supporters?
6. Discipline and follow-through are not virtues attached to a short-sighted goal; they're accelerants.
