# Chapter 14: Special Challenges for People in Charge

## Core Idea
Being the boss creates its own pathologies: staff can't decode you, they overwhelm you because you're available, you manage them as if they were you, you mistake announcing for accomplishing, you carry outdated prejudices about what they want — and you waste effort on people who can't be coached.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"How to Handle Me" memo**: A brutally honest, written self-briefing you hand your staff describing your shortcomings and how to work around them.
  - When to use: New assistant, new team, or when your quirks are silently taxing everyone.
  - How: Imagine the feedback you'd get from everyone who ever worked for you, then write it as instructions for the next person. Three tests it must pass — it has to be **honest**, your employees have to **believe it's accurate**, and they have to **believe it matters**. Fail any of the three and keep it to yourself.
  - Why it works: It converts an undiagnosed irritant into a shared, discussable operating manual and stimulates dialogue. Failure modes: (a) *fantasy* — the division chief who insisted he hated yes-men while rewarding sycophants; the memo becomes "a toxic joke"; (b) *accurate but irrelevant* — the ex-English-teacher CEO whose red-penciling of memo grammar was true to him and worthless to the troops ("Five million dollars a year is a lot to pay for an editor").

- **The Golden Rule Fallacy / "You are not managing you"**: The boss infers that the bossed are just like him and, obeying the Golden Rule, treats them the way *he* likes to be treated.
  - When to use: Whenever a style that feels generous to you (open debate, documentation rigor, tough questioning) reads to staff as domination.
  - How: Ask whether the exchange is a fair fight. You're the boss and they aren't; you have the skill, the history, and the file access, and they don't. If they can't win, they won't play.
  - Why it works: It reframes a values disagreement as an asymmetry problem, which is fixable without a personality transplant.

- **Weaning a dependent staff (the two questions)**: Instead of telling people to stop taking your time, make disengagement their idea.
  - How: Meet each direct report and ask (1) "Looking at your responsibilities — are there areas where I need to be more involved and less involved?" and (2) "Looking at my job — do you ever see me doing things a person at my level shouldn't be doing?" They define legitimate face time and hand you back your calendar.

- **The four prejudices about free agents**: **1. I know what they want. 2. I know what they know. 3. I hate their selfishness. 4. I can always get someone else.**
  - When to use: When good people leave for less money, or you're resenting an employee who brings you an outside offer.
  - How: Ask what people want at every step rather than assuming; concede that knowledge workers know more than you in their domain; treat self-interest as normal (Jack Welch on an expensive broadcaster: "We *wanted* to give him the money"); assume top talent is a fungible asset who could be re-hired elsewhere within a week.

- **Stop Trying to Coach People Who Shouldn't Be Coached** — four categories to walk away from:
  1. People who **don't think they have a problem**.
  2. People **pursuing the wrong strategy for the organization** ("all you'll do is help them get there faster").
  3. People who **should not be in their job** — probe with: "What if we shut down today? Would you be surprised, sad, or relieved?" If they say relieved, "send them packing."
  4. People who **think everyone else is the problem** — "It's hard to help people who don't think they have a problem. It's impossible to fix people who think someone else is the problem."

## Key Concepts
- **Get-out-of-my-face time**: The counterpart to face time — the protected hours a boss must reclaim by redirecting, not rejecting, staff.
- **"Losing control because you're in control"**: Openness and availability generate the demand that destroys your schedule.
- **Checking the box**: Treating a message as delivered because you sent it — mistaking articulation for adoption.
- **The understanding/doing disconnect**: Leadership development's huge false assumption is that if people understand, they will do. "Most of us understand, we just don't do."
- **Free agent nation**: Employees operating as self-contained businesses who will drop the company when it stops meeting their needs.
- **Fungible asset vs. commodity**: A commodity is replaceable by anyone; a fungible asset can always get a better job elsewhere. Knowledge workers are the latter and know it.
- **Loss aversion (in compensation)**: The writer who ignored a $500 and then a $3,000 bonus but met deadlines once $3,000 was *deducted* — and quit within six months.
- **Prejudice**: Inflexible beliefs about a group that don't coincide with reality or how that group sees itself — applied here to employees, not demographics.
- **"Let it go"**: Goldsmith's father, corrected on his grammar for the ten-thousandth time by a first-grade teacher who treated the whole world as first graders: "Honey, I'm 70 years old. Let it go." The chapter's emblem for abandoning an uninterested target.

## Mental Models
- **Think of yourself as a product needing a warning label**: Don Imus tells his audience everything is jive except six words — "You have to stop that now." Every boss should be that legible.
- **Use the Stengel test on team dynamics**: a third love you, a third hate you, a third are undecided — "keep the third who hated you from getting together with the third who were undecided." One employee can't topple a good manager; a coalition can.
- **Think of a memo as the start of follow-up, not the end**: after sending, ask in sequence — did you hear it? did you understand it? did you do something about it?
- **Use "run, don't walk" when someone hires you to fix everyone but themselves**: this is an argument you will never win; skip the heroic measures.

## Worked Example
*Steve, CEO of a large service company, was told by his people that he stifled open communication — and, contradictorily, that he was always changing his mind.* The two flaws are usually mutually exclusive, and Steve laughed off the first: "I'm always talking things out with my people." A director supplied the key: Steve had been a star college debater and was "the world champion at arguing with himself." So every idea an employee brought got shot at in debate mode — open dialogue to Steve, an onslaught to them — and then Steve would re-debate approved decisions privately and reverse himself days later. Goldsmith: "you are the boss — and they aren't. You were the star debater — and they weren't. This isn't a fair fight!" Steve's counter — one staffer loved arguing as much as he did — was the proof: that one success wasn't replicating because that person was *just like him*. Over 18 months Steve apologized, invited opinions, and thought twice before challenging, without becoming a doormat. **Demonstrates the Golden Rule Fallacy: a style that feels like generosity from the top can be an unwinnable contest from below — you are not managing you.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Write a "How to Handle Me" memo — honest, believable, and about things that actually matter to the work.
2. Wean a dependent staff with the two questions rather than closing the door; let them define where you're needed.
3. Assume you are not managing you. Check whether your favorite mode of engagement is a fight only you can win.
4. Never confuse checking the box with landing the message; follow up in three passes — heard, understood, acted on.
5. Audit your four free-agent prejudices; the price of ignoring them is your best talent, and possibly your job.
6. Stop coaching people who don't want to change, are strategically misaligned, are in the wrong job, or blame everyone else. Let it go.
