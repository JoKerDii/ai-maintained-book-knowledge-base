# Chapter 3: The Success Delusion, or Why We Resist Change

## Core Idea
The four beliefs that make people successful — *I have succeeded, I can succeed, I will succeed, I choose to succeed* — are the same four beliefs that make them refuse to change. "That's the paradox of success: These beliefs that carried us *here* may be holding us back in our quest to go *there*." The only lever that moves them is natural law: self-interest.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Success Beliefs** (each an asset that becomes an obstacle the moment change is required):
  1. **"I have succeeded"** — belief in one's **skills and talent** (the *past*). We edit out failures and "run the highlight reel." 80–85% of 50,000+ professionals surveyed rate themselves in the top 20% of their peer group; 70% in the top 10%; among physicians, pilots, and investment bankers, 90% claim the top 10%.
  2. **"I can succeed"** — **confidence** (the *present*). The classic definition of **self-efficacy**, and "may be the most central belief driving individual success." Pairs with a high **internal locus of control**.
  3. **"I will succeed"** — **motivation** (the *future*). Unflappable optimism that mutates into **overcommitment**; executives are not "saving a sinking ship," they are "drowning in a sea of opportunity."
  4. **"I choose to succeed"** — **self-determination**. "When we do what we *choose* to do, we are *committed*. When we do what we *have* to do, we are *compliant*."
  - When to use: As a diagnostic when someone agrees change would be good and then doesn't do it. Identify which of the four beliefs is generating the resistance — each requires a different counter.
  - How: Belief 1 resists via delusional self-rating → confront with 360 data. Belief 2 resists via the superstition trap → separate "because of" from "in spite of." Belief 3 resists via *"I meant to, but I just didn't have time"* → treat overcommitment as the obstacle, not disagreement. Belief 4 resists via **cognitive dissonance** → get "I have chosen to succeed" to become "and I choose to change."
  - Why it works: It reframes resistance as a *side effect of the client's strengths*, not a character flaw — the only framing a high achiever will tolerate.

- **The Superstition Trap**: The core fallacy of the book. "One of the greatest mistakes of successful people is the assumption, **'I behave this way, and I achieve results. Therefore, I must be achieving results because I behave this way.'**" Stated earlier in Belief 2 as: **"I am successful. I behave this way. Therefore, I must be successful *because* I behave this way!"** The challenge is to make people see they are sometimes successful ***in spite of*** the behavior.
  - When to use: On any defended flaw ("my bluntness *is* my creativity," "my pushiness is why I close deals").
  - How: **"Superstition is merely the confusion of correlation and causality."** Tote up your "because of" and "in spite of" activities. Then ask constantly: *Is this behavior a legitimate reason for my success, or am I kidding myself?* Test with a counterexample question — have you ever met a nice person as creative as you? How do your nicer colleagues sell anything at all?
  - Why it works: It attacks the causal claim, not the person's success — leaving the success intact while removing its false justification.

- **Natural Law** (the only law that applies to change): **"People will do something — including changing their behavior — only if it can be demonstrated that doing so is in their own best interests as defined by their own values."**
  - When to use: Whenever you must motivate a change in someone else, or diagnose why your own change effort stalled.
  - How: Find the **hot button**. Self-interest usually boils down to four items — **money, power, status, and popularity**. Clients who already have all four shift to higher-level goals: leaving a legacy, being an inspiring role model, creating a great company. "Every choice, big or small, is a risk-reward decision where your bottom-line thinking is, 'What's in it for me?'"
  - Failure mode: Invoking an endgame that doesn't matter to the person. "People only change their ways when what they truly value is threatened."

- **The three-part denial response** to being told to change: (1) the other party is **confused** and has us mixed up with someone else; (2) **denial** — the criticism can't apply or we wouldn't be so successful; (3) **attack the messenger** — "Why is a smart guy like me listening to a loser like you?"

## Key Concepts
- **The success delusion**: We overestimate our contribution, take credit for others' successes, inflate our standing, ignore our costly failures and dead ends, and exaggerate our projects' profit impact — all "a direct result of success, not failure."
- **"Because of" vs. "in spite of"**: The distinction the whole book rests on. "Almost everyone I meet is successful *because of* doing a lot of things right, and almost everyone I meet is successful *in spite of* some behavior that defies common sense."
- **Self-efficacy**: The belief that you have the capability within yourself to make desirable things happen.
- **Internal locus of control**: Seeing outcomes as a function of motivation and ability rather than luck, random chance, or external factors — held "even when luck does play a critical role."
- **Cognitive dissonance**: "The more we are committed to believing that something is true, the less likely we are to believe that its opposite is true, even in the face of clear evidence that shows we are wrong." Destructive aimed at others (once Bill is a jerk, every act confirms it); protective aimed at oneself (it's why successful people don't buckle when times get tough).
- **Overcommitment**: The failure mode of "I will succeed" — and "as serious an obstacle to change as believing that you don't need fixing."
- **Hot button**: The specific self-interest that, when engaged, makes a person willing to change. "It's not the same thing in all people."
- **The two defensive reactions**: (1) insisting the bad behavior *causes* the success; (2) **fear of overcorrection** — that fixing it will destroy something valuable.

## Mental Models
- **Think of yourself as the salmon, not the bear**: The Unum ad — "YOU PROBABLY FEEL LIKE THE BEAR. WE'D LIKE TO SUGGEST YOU'RE THE SALMON." Your felt position and your real position differ.
- **Think of defended flaws as Skinner's pigeons**: Hungry pigeons repeated random twitches because grain happened to follow. "Hungry business people repeat certain behavior all the time... when they believe large pellets of money and recognition will come their way because of it."
- **Use "born on third base" when someone credits skill for luck**: "If you're born on third base, you shouldn't think you hit a triple."
- **Think of success beliefs as rose-tinted glasses that are usually a feature**: "If we had a complete grip on reality... we wouldn't get out of bed in the morning. After all, the most realistic people in our society are the chronically depressed." Don't try to remove the optimism — target only the false causal claim.
- **You can't mandate synergy** (Barry Diller: "The only thing that works is natural law"): You can't force people to work together, manufacture harmony, or order people to change their thinking. Given enough time, natural relationships develop.

## Anti-patterns
- **Defending a flaw as a success factor**: "I don't suffer fools gladly." Clinging to a causal link between success and specific behavior, "good or bad, responsible or risky, legitimate or inappropriate."
- **Fear of overcorrection**: Refusing to fix a lifelong bad habit because you might swing too far. A 55-year-old lifelong bad listener suddenly becoming excessively interested in others' opinions is "extremely remote" — "We were fixing one bad behavior, not manufacturing a religious conversion."
- **"I meant to, but I just didn't have time"**: The signature excuse of the do-nothings. Not disagreement — overcommitment. "'Later' never arrived."
- **The lottery mentality (the opposite failure)**: Believing success is purely luck — reliably correlates with *not* being a high achiever.
- **Pushing change on someone whose values it doesn't serve**: The antisocial software wizard. Changing him "wasn't worth it"; the right recommendation was to let him be.
- **Discrediting the messenger**: Assuming everyone else is confused, or that you could snap your fingers and get a better job elsewhere.

## Worked Example
*Harry, the world-class aggressive non-listener.* Harry consistently made his numbers, saw things no one else at the company could see, and drove groundbreaking new processes everyone credited him for. He genuinely cared about the company. He also didn't listen — direct reports felt that once Harry had made up his mind, expressing another view was useless. The 360 confirmed it up and down the company, and at home with his wife and kids. Goldsmith put the distinction to him plainly: you're successful **because of** your talent, hard work, and some good luck — and successful **in spite of** being an appalling listener. Harry produced **defensive reaction #1**: bad ideas were "brain pollution" he needed to filter; "I don't suffer fools gladly," said with more pride than patience. Asked whether he truly believed his coworkers and family — the people on whose backs his success rested — were fools, he conceded: "Upon further reflection, perhaps sometimes I am the fool." Then came **defensive reaction #2**, fear of overcorrection: listening more might dry him up creatively. That was dismissed as vanishingly unlikely. Harry concluded it was more productive to hear people out than to keep justifying dysfunctional behavior. **This demonstrates the superstition trap and its two-stage defense: the flaw is defended as a cause of success, and when that collapses, as too dangerous to remove — and both fall to the "because of / in spite of" question.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose resistance by naming which of the four beliefs is producing it — the beliefs are strengths misfiring, not weaknesses.
2. Ask of every defended habit: is this "because of" or "in spite of"? Superstition is confusing correlation with causality.
3. Expect the three-part denial response — confusion, denial, attack the messenger — and don't mistake it for the final answer.
4. Expect two defenses in sequence: "this flaw causes my success," then "fixing it will overcorrect." Answer the first with counterexamples, the second with proportion.
5. Treat overcommitment as a change-blocker in its own right; "I didn't have time" is the most common reason nothing happens.
6. Convert "I have chosen to succeed" into "and I choose to change" — you can't *make* anyone change, only help them change what they choose to.
7. Find the hot button: money, power, status, popularity — or, for those who have all four, legacy, role-modeling, and building something great.
