# Chapter 8: Telling the World, or Advertising

## Core Idea
After you apologize, you must advertise — declare exactly what you plan to change, then repeat it relentlessly. People's perception lags reality: "you have to get 100% better in order to get 10% credit for it from your coworkers," so changed behavior that nobody is told about registers as no change at all.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Advertising (telling the world)**: It's not enough to say you want to get better; you must declare precisely in what area you plan to change, and keep declaring it.
  - When to use: Immediately after the apology, and continuously for the full 12–18 month change effort.
  - How, in escalating order of odds:
    1. Tell people you are trying to change — your efforts land on their radar screen.
    2. Tell everyone **how hard you're trying**, and repeat it week after week.
    3. **Ask everyone for ideas** to help you get better — now coworkers are invested, and they watch to see whether you act on their suggestions.
  - Why it works / failure mode: The barrier is **cognitive dissonance** (Ch 3) — we see people consistently with our existing stereotype, so a saintly act from an "arrogant jerk" is filed as the exception that proves the rule. Advertising is what chips at the preconception. Failure mode: treating it as a "one day sale." "It's a little like the tree falling in the forest. If no one hears the thud, does it make a sound?" The apology and announcement point everyone toward the tree.

- **The "dumb phase"**: Named for the "dumb period" great wines pass through — somewhere between roughly 6 and 18 years the wine goes to sleep, tastes bland and characterless, then wakes up and improves dramatically. Personal-change campaigns and corporate initiatives do the same.
  - When to use: To hold your nerve when weeks of visible effort produce zero recognition.
  - How: Advertise *relentlessly*, as a long-term campaign, not a launch. Assume people did not hear you the first, second, or third time. "Pound the message into your colleagues' heads through repetition that's as steady as a metronome" — because they're not paying as close attention to your goals as you are.

- **"One, two, three, seven"** (the diagnosis for initiatives that die): Every successful project has **seven phases**: (1) assessing the situation, (2) isolating the problem, (3) formulating, (4) **woo up** — get superiors to *approve*, (5) **woo laterally** — get peers to *agree*, (6) **woo down** — get direct reports to *accept*, (7) implementation.
  - When to use: Any time a well-analyzed plan — or a well-executed personal change — produces no movement six months later.
  - How: Give phases 4–6 as much attention as 1, 2, 3, and 7. They are the political buy-in, "the sine qua non of getting things done," and each targets a different constituency. Skip them and "you may as well be working alone in a locked room where no one sees you, hears you, or knows you exist." You commit "one, two, three, seven" *on yourself* when you change privately without recruiting anyone.

- **Be your own press secretary** — five daily disciplines:
  - Treat every day as a **press conference** where colleagues are judging you and waiting for you to trip up; the mindset boosts self-awareness.
  - Treat every day as an opportunity to hit the message home — every day you don't, you lose a step and backslide on your promise.
  - Treat every day as a chance to take on all challengers — some people privately don't want you to succeed, so shed the naiveté and be a little paranoid.
  - Think of it as an **election campaign**: you don't elect yourself to "new improved you," your colleagues do. They are your constituency; without their votes you can never establish that you changed.
  - Think in weeks and months, not day to day — put out daily fires but hold the long-term agenda.

## Key Concepts
- **Advertising**: Proactively and repeatedly telling people what specific behavior you are working to change.
- **The 100%/10% ratio**: Goldsmith's calculation of the perception lag — total behavioral change earns a tenth of the credit.
- **Cognitive dissonance (applied)**: Existing stereotypes filter every new act, positive or negative, into "the exception to the rule."
- **The dumb phase**: The interval when a genuinely good change has been made but has not yet registered on anyone.
- **Woo up / woo laterally / woo down**: The three buy-in phases (approve / agree / accept) that separate formulation from implementation.
- **Staying on message**: Knowing what you want to say and repeating it "with extreme discipline and near-shamelessness" until it sinks in.
- **Doing something dramatic to announce it**: The apology itself is the most theatrical possible launch — especially in front of people who think you cannot change.

## Mental Models
- **Think of a personal change as a product launch**: a great new product with no advertising campaign doesn't get bought. You have to tell the world "Hey, I'm over here" and give them a reason to care.
- **Use the political campaign frame when repetition feels shameless**: candidates run the same ad over and over because it works — simple, un-nuanced messages break through the clutter.
- **Think of a stalled change as "one, two, three, seven"**: the analysis was fine; you skipped the wooing.
- **Use the dumb-phase idea when discouraged**: the wine hasn't died, it's asleep — you opened the bottle too early.

## Anti-patterns
- **Changing quietly and waiting to be noticed**: You cannot rely on other people to read your mind or take note of changed behavior. It takes far more than a few weeks for anyone to see the new you.
- **Announcing once**: "You're not running a 'one day sale' here." Assume they didn't hear you the first three times.
- **Skipping phases 4–6**: Study, diagnose, recommend, hand off — and six months later the trouble spot is unchanged. "You can't get to seven without counting from one to six. Anything less is bad arithmetic."
- **Actual press-secretary tricks**: Obfuscating, selective memory, dodging questions — valuable in the arsenal, explicitly *not* what's being recommended.
- **Naiveté about opposition**: Some colleagues, privately or overtly, don't want you to succeed.

## Worked Example
*The 12-year-old Bordeaux.* At a wine expert's dinner, a guest brought a 12-year-old bottle from a legendary French vineyard. The host politely suggested it wasn't the right time; everyone insisted it was ready. They decanted it, swirled it, inhaled the bouquet — and tasted nothing at all. Bland, characterless, as if it had died in the bottle. Tasted again: same verdict. The host then explained the "dumb period" — great wines go to sleep for a few years before waking up and improving dramatically. Their bottle was still asleep.

**Demonstrates why advertising has to be relentless rather than event-based: a genuinely improving thing can register as nothing at all for a long stretch, and the fault is in the timing of the tasting, not the wine. Your colleagues will "taste" your change during its dumb phase and conclude nothing has happened — unless you keep telling them, week after week, that the bottle is still improving.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Apologize, then advertise — and name the specific behavior, not a vague intent to improve.
2. Budget for the perception lag: 100% better buys about 10% credit, because cognitive dissonance filters everything through the old stereotype.
3. Escalate: announce it → repeat how hard you're trying → ask people for ideas, which makes them invested participants.
4. Expect a dumb phase and advertise through it with metronome-steady repetition.
5. Don't commit "one, two, three, seven" — woo up for approval, laterally for agreement, down for acceptance before expecting implementation.
6. Run it as an election campaign: your colleagues cast the votes that decide whether you changed.
7. Be your own press secretary — every day is a press conference, a message opportunity, and a long-term agenda.
