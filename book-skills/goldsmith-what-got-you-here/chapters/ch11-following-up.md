# Chapter 11: Following Up

## Core Idea
Follow-up is the difference-maker: "You do not get better without follow-up." Goldsmith's data across eight companies and 86,000 respondents shows that where leaders followed up consistently, perceived effectiveness jumped dramatically; where they didn't, there was little or no perceived change — regardless of how good the training was.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The monthly follow-up question** (the recurring "How'm I doing?" check with every stakeholder):
  - When to use: Continuously, after apologizing (Ch 7) and advertising (Ch 8). Cadence: go back to **all** your coworkers **"every month or so"**. Total duration of the follow-up phase: **12 to 18 months** — the most protracted part of the change process.
  - How (Goldsmith's client script, verbatim in structure): "Last month I told you that I would try to get better at being more inclusive. You gave me some ideas and I would like to know if you think I have effectively put them into practice."
  - Why it works: The question forces colleagues to stop, re-examine your effort, mentally gauge your progress, and keep you focused on continued improvement. Crucially it relocates the verdict: "When I tell you, 'I'm getting better,' I believe it. When I ask you, 'Am I getting better?' and you say I am, then *you* believe it."
  - Failure mode: Asking for input and then ignoring it — a leader who seeks input but doesn't follow up on it is logically perceived as not caring about getting better at all.

- **The Ed Koch "How'm I doing?" model** — four functions of a repeated public question:
  1. **Advertises** that you're trying and that you care.
  2. **Includes and involves** — phrasing it as a question, not the assertion "I'm doing great," tells people your fate rests in *their* hands.
  3. **Imprints** — repetition turns it into your slogan and signals that improvement is an ongoing process, not an overnight miracle.
  4. **Forces you to walk the talk** — if the answer is "not so great," you have to deal with it before the next asking.

- **The nightly coach routine** (Goldsmith's personal follow-up):
  - How: Recruit a "Jim Moore" — a person who calls you daily and asks the *same* prescribed list of ~a dozen small daily tasks tied to one issue you want to improve. You answer honestly; they do not judge.
  - Three criteria for picking a coach: (1) it must not be a chore for them to reach you; (2) they must be interested in your life and have your best interests at heart; (3) they may only ask the prescribed questions — they cannot judge your answers.
  - Why it works: Involving another person is the whole point. Logging answers alone "is not quite follow-up" — it's a diary, and diaries get abandoned. A human you don't want to disappoint plus who encourages you supplies the mirror.

## Key Concepts
- **Program of the month**: What every other step degrades into without relentless follow-up.
- **The 70/30 split**: ~100% of trainees intend to apply what they learned; a year later ~70% of their direct reports confirm they did, 30% say the boss did absolutely nothing. Consistent across all eight companies and across Americans, Europeans, and Asians — human nature, not culture.
- **The disconnect between understanding and doing** (lesson two): Leadership development rests on the false assumption "if people understand, then they will do." Most of us understand; we just don't do.
- **"Too busy"**: The mundane reason executives who promise to change don't — they return to message piles, reports, and clients, and get distracted by day-to-day demands.
- **The Hawthorne Effect** (Elton Mayo, Western Electric Hawthorne Works): productivity rises when workers believe bosses show greater interest and involvement in their work — the same dynamic that makes follow-up work.
- **Custom feedback**: Goldsmith's contribution to 360-degree feedback — surveys answering "What makes a great leader in *this particular organization*?"
- **Process, not event** (lesson four): Real development takes time; you can't "get it" in the form of a nitroglycerin tablet.
- **Peter Drucker's prediction**: "the leader of the future will be a person who knows how to ask."

## Mental Models
- **Think of follow-up as exercise, not a lecture on exercise**: Watching tapes on working out leaves you unfit a year later. Fitness comes from regular exercise; change comes from repeated follow-up, not from the seminar.
- **Use a scheduled weigh-in when you want a diet to stick**: If you know someone who matters will check your weight monthly, you stay on the diet.
- **Think of change as coming from others' lips, not yours**: Your self-report is worthless as evidence; their report is the measurement.
- **Use follow-up as an advertising campaign, not an audit**: Every ask reminds people you're trying and that they're helping.

## Anti-patterns
- **Training without follow-up**: The event feels great, reviews glow — but glowing reviews only prove attendees *thought* the class was valuable, not that it was.
- **Asking once**: A single check-in reads as a stunt; skepticism only erases through repetition.
- **Following up with yourself only**: A private log is a diary, "considerably less likely to breed ongoing success."
- **Picking a judgmental coach**: If the coach rates your answers, you start managing the answers instead of the behavior. (Goldsmith's warning: a spouse or parent may find suspending judgment hard.)
- **Asserting progress instead of asking about it**: "I'm doing great" excludes the people whose perception *is* the outcome.

## Worked Example
*The question that rebuilt a career practice.* Preparing training at a Fortune 100 company, an EVP asked Goldsmith, "Does anyone who goes to these leadership development programs ever *really* change?" He had trained thousands, had glowing reviews and letters from believers — and had to answer, "I don't know." He spent two years becoming a "follow-up grind," going back to client corporations until the data covered eight companies and 86,000 participants. He then traced five of the eight to measure follow-up itself, defined as interaction between would-be leaders and colleagues about whether they were improving, scored on five scales from "frequent interaction" to "little or none." Result: little or no follow-up → little or no perceived change; consistent follow-up → dramatic jump in perceived effectiveness.

**Demonstrates: follow-up is not a nicety appended to change — it is the variable that produces the change. Leaders who don't follow up aren't bad leaders; they're just not perceived as getting better, and perception is the whole game.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Go back to **all** your colleagues **every month or so** and ask whether you've put their ideas into practice. Budget **12–18 months**.
2. Ask, never assert — when *they* say you're better, they believe it; when you say it, only you do.
3. Expect the 70/30 split; the 30% fail not from unwillingness but from busyness.
4. Understanding ≠ doing. Close the gap with structure, not insight.
5. Recruit a daily coach: ~12 small tasks, same questions, no judgment, another human being in the loop.
6. Follow-up measures progress, advertises effort, imprints it, erases skepticism, and forces you to actually do it.
7. If you ask for input and then ignore it, you are worse off than if you never asked.
