## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**Central thesis**: The skills that got you to your current level are *not* the ones that get you to the next. At the top, the gap is never technical — **"the higher you go, the more your problems are behavioral."** These are **transactional flaws**: not deficits of skill, intelligence, or fixed personality, but interpersonal habits. The cure is almost always **subtraction**, not addition. Drucker: leaders don't need to learn what to do, they need to learn **what to stop**. Keep a **"To Stop" list**.

### The Twenty Habits (Ch 4) — the book's spine
Use this list when someone is technically excellent and still stalling.

| # | Habit | Tell |
|---|-------|------|
| 1 | Winning too much | Must win at all costs, in all situations, even when it's beside the point |
| 2 | Adding too much value | "Great idea, but…" — improves the idea 5%, cuts commitment 50% |
| 3 | Passing judgment | Rates others and imposes their standards |
| 4 | Making destructive comments | Sarcasm and cutting remarks mistaken for wit |
| 5 | Starting with "No," "But," or "However" | Negative qualifiers that secretly say "I'm right, you're wrong" |
| 6 | Telling the world how smart we are | Needs to prove they're smarter than you assume |
| 7 | Speaking when angry | Emotional volatility used as a management tool |
| 8 | Negativity, or "Let me explain why that won't work" | Shares negative thoughts unasked |
| 9 | Withholding information | Hoards to maintain advantage |
| 10 | Failing to give proper recognition | Cannot praise or reward |
| 11 | Claiming credit that we don't deserve | Overestimates own contribution to any success |
| 12 | Making excuses | Repositions annoying behavior as a permanent fixture |
| 13 | Clinging to the past | Deflects blame onto history and other people |
| 14 | Playing favorites | Can't see they're treating someone unfairly |
| 15 | Refusing to express regret | Won't take responsibility or admit being wrong |
| 16 | Not listening | The most passive-aggressive form of disrespect |
| 17 | Failing to express gratitude | The most basic form of bad manners |
| 18 | Punishing the messenger | Attacks the innocent who were trying to help |
| 19 | Passing the buck | Blames everyone but themselves |
| 20 | An excessive need to be "me" | Exalts faults as virtues because "that's who I am" |
| **21** | **Goal obsession** (Ch 5) | Hits the target and forgets what it was for — **not a flaw but a creator of flaws** |

**Two caveats**: these people aren't bad people, and **don't self-diagnose** — amateurs see cancer where a professional sees a muscle pull.

### The Success Delusion (Ch 3)
Use when someone agrees with the feedback and still doesn't change. Four beliefs make successful people resist change: **I have succeeded / I can succeed / I will succeed / I choose to succeed.** They produce the **superstition trap**: *"I behave this way, and I am successful, therefore I must be successful* because *I behave this way."* Break it with one question: **because of, or in spite of?** And apply **natural law**: people change only when it's demonstrably in their own interests, as defined by *their* values.

### The Change Sequence (Ch 6–12) — run in this order
1. **Feedback** — get the truth from 8–15 people who pass **the Four Commitments**: (1) let go of the past, (2) tell the truth, (3) be supportive, not a cynic/critic/judge, (4) pick one thing to improve in themselves. The only question that works: *"How can I do better?"*
2. **Apologizing** — "I'm sorry. I'll try to do better in the future." **Then say nothing.** Explanation dilutes it into a defense.
3. **Advertising** — announce the change and repeat it week after week. You must get 100% better to earn 10% credit; expect a **dumb phase** where you've improved and nobody's noticed.
4. **Listening** — think before you speak, listen with respect, ask **"Is it worth it?"** (not "is it true?"). Make the other person feel like the most important person in the room.
5. **Thanking** — the only response to feedback that can't be argued with or escalated.
6. **Following up** — ask everyone *"Last month I said I'd work on X — have I put your ideas into practice?"* **every month or so, for 12–18 months.** Across ~86,000 respondents: no follow-up, no perceived change, no matter the effort.
7. **Feedforward** — Use instead of criticism whenever you want ideas without defensiveness. Four steps: pick one behavior → describe it one-on-one to anyone → ask for **two suggestions for the future** (the past is off-limits) → listen, take notes, say only **"Thank you."** Repeat with others. *"If feedback is past tense, feedforward is future perfect."*

### Rules for Changing (Ch 13) & Leading (Ch 14)
**One gun, one bullet** — change one thing at a time. You may not have a disease behavioral change can cure; pick the right thing; don't hide from the truth; there is no ideal behavior; if you can measure it you can achieve it; **monetize** it ($10–20 a slip); the best time is now.
**Shift into neutral**: becoming nicer means filling a box with positive acts; **ceasing to be a jerk just means leaving the box empty**.
**Four gates before speaking**: will this help our customers? our company? the person I'm talking to? the person I'm talking about?
**Golden Rule Fallacy**: "you are not managing you." And **stop coaching people who shouldn't be coached** — those who deny the problem, are in the wrong job, or think everyone else is the problem. Sometimes the right move is: **"Let it go."**

## Scope & Limits
This skill covers the book content only. Goldsmith addresses **interpersonal, behavioral** flaws in already-successful people — it explicitly does not treat skill gaps, strategy, or clinical issues. Note: the "Wheel of Change" belongs to Goldsmith's later book *Triggers*, not this one, and this book contains no "mini-survey" or "Total Quality Leadership" method — the follow-up mechanism is the monthly stakeholder question in Ch 11. For hands-on application, combine with project-specific context. For topics beyond this book, check related skills or ask the agent directly.
