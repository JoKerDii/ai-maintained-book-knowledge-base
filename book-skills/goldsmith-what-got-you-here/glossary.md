# Glossary — What Got You Here Won't Get You There

## The Twenty Habits (Ch 4) — by name
**1. Winning too much** — The need to win at all costs and in all situations; the root habit behind most of the others (Ch 4).
**2. Adding too much value** — The overwhelming desire to add our two cents to every discussion (Ch 4).
**3. Passing judgment** — The need to rate others and impose our standards on them (Ch 4).
**4. Making destructive comments** — Needless sarcasm and cutting remarks we think make us sound sharp and witty (Ch 4).
**5. Starting with "No," "But," or "However"** — Overuse of negative qualifiers that secretly say "I'm right. You're wrong." (Ch 4).
**6. Telling the world how smart we are** — The need to show people we're smarter than they think we are (Ch 4).
**7. Speaking when angry** — Using emotional volatility as a management tool (Ch 4).
**8. Negativity, or "Let me explain why that won't work"** — The need to share our negative thoughts even when we weren't asked (Ch 4).
**9. Withholding information** — Refusing to share information in order to maintain an advantage over others (Ch 4).
**10. Failing to give proper recognition** — The inability to praise and reward (Ch 4).
**11. Claiming credit that we don't deserve** — The most annoying way to overestimate our contribution to any success (Ch 4).
**12. Making excuses** — Repositioning our annoying behavior as a permanent fixture so people excuse us for it (Ch 4).
**13. Clinging to the past** — Deflecting blame onto events and people from our past; a subset of blaming everyone else (Ch 4).
**14. Playing favorites** — Failing to see that we are treating someone unfairly (Ch 4).
**15. Refusing to express regret** — Inability to take responsibility, admit we're wrong, or recognize how our actions affect others (Ch 4).
**16. Not listening** — The most passive-aggressive form of disrespect for colleagues (Ch 4).
**17. Failing to express gratitude** — The most basic form of bad manners (Ch 4).
**18. Punishing the messenger** — The misguided need to attack the innocent who are usually only trying to help us (Ch 4).
**19. Passing the buck** — The need to blame everyone but ourselves (Ch 4).
**20. An excessive need to be "me"** — Exalting our faults as virtues simply because they're who we are (Ch 4).
**21. Goal obsession** — The twenty-first habit; not a flaw but *a creator of flaws* — pursuing a goal so hard you forget what it was for (Ch 5).

## Terms A–Z
**Advertising** — Deliberately telling colleagues what you are changing, repeatedly, because perception lags behavior (Ch 8).
**Apology formula** — "I'm sorry. I'll try to do better in the future." Then say nothing (Ch 7).
**Because of vs. in spite of** — The test that breaks the superstition trap: are you successful *because of* this behavior or *in spite of* it? (Ch 3).
**Blindside event** — Unsolicited feedback that reveals something everyone else already knew (Ch 6).
**Dumb phase** — The period (borrowed from wine) when improvement is real but invisible to others; keep advertising through it (Ch 8).
**Feedback** — Past-tense information about what happened; tells you *what* to change, not how (Ch 6).
**Feedforward** — Feedback going in the opposite direction: asking for two suggestions for the future, listening, saying thank you. "If feedback is past tense, feedforward is future perfect." (Ch 12).
**Four Commitments** — What you must ask of raters/helpers: (1) let go of the past, (2) tell the truth, (3) be supportive, not a cynic/critic/judge, (4) pick one thing to improve in themselves (Ch 6).
**Four Gate Questions** — Before speaking, ask: will this comment help our customers? our company? the person I'm talking to? the person I'm talking about? (Ch 4).
**Fallacy of added value** — Improving someone's idea by 5% while cutting their commitment to it by 50% by stealing ownership (Ch 4).
**Global Leadership Inventory** — The Appendix's 72-item, 15-dimension 360 instrument on a 5-point satisfaction scale (Appendix).
**Goal obsession** — See habit 21. Arises from misunderstanding what we want, or what others want us to do (Ch 5).
**Golden Rule Fallacy** — Managing people the way *you* want to be managed; "you are not managing you" (Ch 14).
**Great Western Disease** — "I will be happy when…" — deferring meaning to a future milestone (Coda).
**"How'm I doing?"** — Ed Koch's model for follow-up: ask constantly and publicly (Ch 11).
**"How to Handle Me" memo** — A leader's honest, accurate, and *relevant* self-description shared with the team (Ch 14).
**Is it worth it?** — The listening/speaking filter: not "is it true?" but "will saying this help?" (Ch 4, Ch 9).
**"Let it go"** — Knowing when to stop trying to change someone (or yourself); appears verbatim in Ch 14's "Stop Trying to Coach People Who Shouldn't Be Coached" (Ch 14).
**Mission neutral** — Behavior that is neither good nor bad; the target state for a bad habit is often simply *nothing* (Ch 4, Ch 13).
**Monetizing the habit** — Charging yourself a fixed sum ($10–$20) per offense to make an invisible habit visible and costly (Ch 4, Ch 13).
**Natural law** — People change only if it's demonstrably in their own best interests as defined by their own values (Ch 3).
**One gun, one bullet** — Pick exactly one behavior to change at a time (Ch 13).
**Program of the month** — The cynical read colleagues apply to any change effort that isn't followed up (Ch 11).
**Shifting into neutral / the empty box** — Becoming nicer means filling a box with positive acts; ceasing to be a jerk just means leaving it empty (Ch 4).
**Solicited feedback** — Asking "How can I do better?" — the only feedback question that reliably works (Ch 6).
**Stengel's thirds** — Any team splits into those who love you, hate you, and are undecided; manage the undecided (Ch 14).
**Success delusion** — The four beliefs (I have succeeded / I can succeed / I will succeed / I choose to succeed) that make successful people resist change (Ch 3).
**Superstition trap** — "I behave this way, and I am successful, therefore I must be successful *because* I behave this way." (Ch 3).
**Thanking** — The one response to feedback that cannot be argued with, escalated, or trumped (Ch 10).
**"There is never anyone in the other boat"** — Your anger is at a projection; the empty boat has no one to blame (Ch 4).
**To Stop list** — The counterpart to a To Do list; Drucker: leaders need to learn what to stop (Ch 4).
**Transactional flaws** — Interpersonal behaviors between people — not flaws of skill, intelligence, or fixed personality (Ch 4).
**95-Year-Old Exercise** — Imagine you're 95 and dying; advise your present self, write it down, then do it (Coda).
**70/30 split** — Of people who intend to change, ~70% act and ~30% do nothing, consistently across companies and cultures (Ch 11).
