# Patterns — Goldsmith's Change Techniques

The seven steps run in order. Skipping any one is the usual reason change fails.

## 1. Feedback — Get the Truth
**When to use**: First. Before you can change, you must know which behavior actually costs you, in others' eyes, not yours.
**How**:
1. Pick 8–15 people who see your interpersonal behavior daily — peers, reports, customers, your best friend at work.
2. Qualify each against **the Four Commitments**: will they (1) let go of the past, (2) tell the truth, (3) be supportive rather than a cynic, critic, or judge, (4) pick one thing to improve in themselves? If a name fails any of the four, drop it.
3. Collect confidentially and in aggregate so the data can't be attributed — or denied.
4. Ask the only question that works: **"How can I do better?"** Never argue with the answer.
5. Self-administered substitutes: list your remarks for a week; watch yourself with the sound off; complete "It would make my life easier if my colleagues stopped ___"; audit your self-aggrandizing remarks; ask at home.
**Trade-offs**: Feedback tells you *what* to change, never *how*. It also forces people to relive the past, which is why it stalls — hence feedforward later. Anonymity is what buys you honesty; sacrifice it and you get flattery.

## 2. Apologizing — Clear the Ledger
**When to use**: Immediately after feedback, before announcing any change. Nothing else works until the emotional debt is cleared.
**How**: Say **"I'm sorry."** Add **"I'll try to do better in the future."** Then **say nothing**. Get in and get out fast.
**Trade-offs**: The entire power comes from the silence after. Every word of explanation, context, or qualification dilutes it into a defense. Winking, joking, or "I'm sorry *if* you felt…" converts an apology into a fresh offense.

## 3. Advertising — Tell the World
**When to use**: After apologizing. Because you have to get 100% better before colleagues will grant you 10% credit — their perception is filtered through the old stereotype.
**How**:
1. Announce specifically what behavior you are changing.
2. Repeat it, week after week, metronome-steady — not a "one day sale."
3. Ask everyone for ideas, which makes them invested in your success.
4. Be your own press secretary: treat every day as a message opportunity and stay on message with near-shameless discipline.
**Trade-offs**: Feels immodest and repetitive to you long before it registers with them. Expect a **dumb phase** where you are genuinely better and nobody notices. Advertise anyway.

## 4. Listening — Earn the Right
**When to use**: Continuously; it is the habit most often cited and the cheapest to fix.
**How**: Three things all good listeners do — **think before you speak**, **listen with respect**, and **ask "Is it worth it?"** Tactically: don't interrupt, don't finish sentences, don't say "I already knew that," never open with "no," "but," or "however," say "thank you," and ask questions that make the other person talk. Drill: count silently to fifty before responding.
**Trade-offs**: Roughly half of what you were about to say may be correct — and still not worth saying. You trade being right for being effective. The paradox: the more you subsume your desire to shine, the more you shine.

## 5. Thanking — Close the Loop
**When to use**: As the response to every piece of feedback or suggestion, and as a standing practice.
**How**: Say "thank you" and stop. It is the one reply that cannot be argued with, escalated, or trumped — the only counter is "you're welcome." Drill: list the 25 people most responsible for your success, ask whether you ever actually told them, and write each a note.
**Trade-offs**: Some read gratitude as submissive; it isn't, but expect the flinch. Overuse as a conversational full-stop can shut down dialogue you actually wanted.

## 6. Following Up — Make It Stick
**When to use**: Every month or so, for **12 to 18 months**. This is the step that converts intention into perceived change.
**How**: Go back to **all** the coworkers who gave you feedback and ask: *"Last month I said I'd work on X. Do you think I've put your ideas into practice?"* Then thank them and repeat. Ed Koch's "How'm I doing?" is the model — it advertises, involves, imprints, and forces you to walk the talk.
**Trade-offs**: This is a grind, not an event — no nitroglycerin tablet. But the evidence is blunt: across ~86,000 respondents, leaders who did no follow-up showed no perceived improvement regardless of effort; consistent follow-up produced dramatic gains. Note: about 30% of people who fully intend to change do nothing, almost always pleading "too busy."

## 7. Feedforward — Replace Criticism with Ideas
**When to use**: Whenever you want improvement ideas without triggering defensiveness — yours or anyone else's. It never has to stop.
**How** (four steps):
1. Pick one behavior you want to change.
2. Describe it one-on-one to anyone — expertise is irrelevant; strangers can be ideal partners.
3. Ask for **two suggestions for the future**. No discussion of the past is permitted.
4. Listen, take notes, and say only **"Thank you."** No judging, rating, or critiquing — not even "that's a good idea." Then repeat with someone else.
**Trade-offs**: You give up the right to debate or defend, which is the point. It works because successful people accept future ideas while rejecting past criticism, because you can change the future but not the past, and because it costs the giver nothing — no need to be right, no career risk in "criticizing the boss." Weakness: it generates volume, not validation; you still need feedback and follow-up to know whether anything landed.

## Supporting Pattern: Monetizing the Habit
**When to use**: When a habit is invisible to you in the moment (starting with "no/but/however," destructive comments).
**How**: Appoint a witness and pay a fixed fine — $10 or $20 — per offense, on the spot, to charity.
**Trade-offs**: Crude and public, which is exactly why it works fast. It builds awareness, not judgment; retire it once the behavior is neutral.
