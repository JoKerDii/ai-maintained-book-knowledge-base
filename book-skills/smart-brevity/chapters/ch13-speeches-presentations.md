# Chapter 13: Speeches & Presentations

## Core Idea
A great speech or deck delivers ONE memorable Big Thought; everything else — slides, stats, stories — exists only to amplify it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Smart Brevity speech** (7 steps):
  1. Win before you begin — speak authentically; slides/notes/teleprompters are crutches; rehearse eye contact with 5–6 people.
  2. Remember the audience — they'll remember one point, if you're lucky. Open with a short real-life story ending in a chuckle (one joke/anecdote, "neighbor-length").
  3. Distill your ONE point/lesson; write it word for word (HBR: 15 words max — shorter is better).
  4. Hit them over the head: "The one thing I want you to remember today is…"
  5. Follow with a verbal "Why it matters" — "This matters to you because…"
  6. Unfurl a couple of numbered stats/stories to back it up ("Five points…").
  7. Reinforce the Big Thought at the end ("Remember, if there is one thing you take away…") and say thank you.
- **Duarte's secret structure** (Smart Brevity version): describe the status quo → contrast with your lofty idea → move back and forth between what is / what could be → call to action → end with a vivid portrait of utopia.
- **Presentation hacks**: (1) Write the precise outcome + 3–5 supporting points; sharpen the ask into ~6 words. (2) Simplify every slide — one message, absorbable in 3 seconds (billboard at 65 mph). (3) Pictures over word-clumps (images → 65% recall vs. 10% heard). (4) Keep it short — one big idea + 3–5 points; McKinsey rule: for every 20 slides you want, use 2. (5) Always be closing — "I created this deck so I can get ___ / teach you ___" is your final slide.

## Key Concepts
- **Big Thought**: The speech's one-sentence takeaway; if you don't know it, the audience never will.
- **Simplify to exaggerate**: Fewer words/slides/visuals; destroy anything that distracts.
- **Two-stimuli limit**: Brains process ~two stimuli (spoken word + picture); add bulleted text and you're lost.
- **18-minute rule (TED)**: Short enough to hold attention, long enough to matter (Chris Anderson).

## Mental Models
- Treat each slide as a **billboard** — would someone at 65 mph get the point?
- **You are not Steve Jobs** — most speakers just need to survive the stage with one point intact.

## Anti-patterns
- **Foggy, groggy generalities**: Biden's 28-minute Coast Guard address with no discernible Big Thought — "But all kidding aside…"
- **Death by PowerPoint** (Tufte): a drug making us stupid, boring, and wasteful — worthy of a worldwide recall.
- **Reading from slides**: Your audience has already checked out.

## Worked Example
**Pamela Meyer's TED opener** ("How to Spot a Liar"): Grab — "I don't want to alarm anybody in this room, but it's just come to my attention that the person to your right is a liar." Funny — the "we'll email you" bit. Map — "I'm going to clarify my goal for you." Big Thought — "Lying is a cooperative act… Its power emerges when someone else agrees to believe the lie." Nailed it.

**Biden fix**: "The world is changing. We're at a significant inflection point…" → "Nice work, graduates. You're living in an epic time in world history — and you can make a massive, lasting difference, fast. Here's how…"

## Key Takeaways
1. Know your Big Thought word-for-word before building anything.
2. Announce it explicitly, back it with numbered points, restate it at the end.
3. One message per slide; pictures beat text; cut the deck by ~90%.
4. Iconic speeches are short (Gettysburg: 272 words).
5. Always be closing — end by asking for the outcome.
