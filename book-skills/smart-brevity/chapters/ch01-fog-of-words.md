# Chapter 1: The Fog of Words (Introduction)

## Core Idea
We are drowning in words while attention collapses; the only fix is to adapt to how people actually consume content — scan, skim, bail — instead of how you wish they did.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Adapt, don't wish**: The book's founding premise.
  - When to use: Any time you sit down to write, speak, or present.
  - How: Accept that most people will scan or skip most of what you say, then make every word count. "Adapt to how people consume content — not how you wish they did."

## Key Concepts
- **The fog of words**: The modern glut of texts, emails, Slacks, memos that jams inboxes and buries what matters.
- **Two root causes**: (1) tech opened the floodgates to infinite content; (2) our stubborn habit of writing like it's 1980.
- **Length = depth fallacy**: We're taught that more words signal more intelligence/importance (papers by word count, thick books). Technology turned this glitch into a time-sapping bug.
- **Billions of wasted words**: ~1/3 of work emails that need attention go unread; most words of most stories are never seen; most chapters of most books go untouched.

## Mental Models
- Think of communication as a **war for attention** you are currently losing — every word is a battle for a few more seconds.
- Treat length as a **cost you impose on the reader**, not a gift you give them.

## Anti-patterns
- **Faking depth with volume**: Overindulging in words to show off smarts or hide that you don't know your point.
- **Writing generationally out of date**: Same word counts, same style, for an audience with less time and more distractions.

## Worked Example
The attention stats the authors marshal as their case:
- Eye-tracking: people spend ~26 seconds on a typical piece of content.
- Under 15 seconds on most web pages clicked; the brain decides in ~17 milliseconds whether it likes what it clicked.
- We check phones 344+ times/day — once every ~4 minutes.
- It takes 20+ minutes to refocus after a distraction; most of us are terrible multitaskers.
- Slack CEO Stewart Butterfield: in a 10,000-person company spending $1B on payroll, 50–60% of the average employee's time goes to communication — yet no one is trained to do it well.
Conclusion: "If you see everything, you remember nothing."

## Key Takeaways
1. Start from the data: most people scan or skip most of what you write.
2. Length signals fear; brevity signals confidence.
3. The problem is worst at work — remote/hybrid made it a critical weakness for every leader and rising star.
4. The upside is personal: punch through noise, be heard, win recognition for your ideas.
5. Short does NOT mean shallow — it means selective.
