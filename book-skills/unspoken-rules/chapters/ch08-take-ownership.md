# Chapter 8: Take Ownership

## Core Idea
Workplace assignments lack the explicit instructions, rubrics, and deadlines of school, so the secret to doing a good job is to understand exactly what's expected and stay one step ahead of your boss. Taking ownership means treating every assignment as if *your* reputation — not just your manager's — is on the line.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **What / How / By When**: The three questions you must answer with your manager for every task.
  - When to use: Immediately upon receiving any assignment.
  - How: Clarify *what* to do, *how* to do it, and *by when*. If unclear on what/how, research prior examples and bring options ("We could try A, B, or C; I suggest A but change X because Y — do you agree?"). If unclear on by-when, mirror coworkers' urgency or ask "When would you like to check in?"
  - Why it works: Missing details mean you do the wrong work, the wrong way, or too late — 30 seconds of clarifying up front saves 30 hours later.

- **RACI**: Every assignment has hidden roles — Responsible (does the work), Accountable (reputation on the line if it fails), Consulted (perspective needed), Informed (kept up to date).
  - When to use: Before starting, to map who must be involved.
  - How: Clarify each role by asking — "Who is responsible for what?", "Who needs to sign off?", "Whose perspective should I get?", "Who should I keep up to date?"
  - Failure mode: Skipping Consulted/Informed people makes you seem overbearing or threatening; this is acute when remote and you can't see who your manager talks to.

- **Work Backward from the Deadline**: Every task has a final (spoken) deadline and at least one interim (unspoken) check-in.
  - When to use: At the start of any deliverable with a due date.
  - How: Ask for an interim check-in shortly after the first meeting ("When should we check in for a status update?"); account for reviewers' and approvers' availability — a "7-weekday" task may really be 2 days if your manager and their manager are away.

- **Bundle, Escalate, and Show Your Homework**: How to ask non-urgent questions.
  - When to use: When you have questions you couldn't answer yourself.
  - How: Search email/folders, then online. Collect questions into a bundle. Escalate one rung at a time (peer → specialist → manager → manager's manager). Each time, give context and show what you already tried. Goal: ask five questions once, not one question five times.

- **Give Others Something to React To**: How to handle "what should I do next?" ambiguity.
  - When to use: When unsure of next steps and it isn't urgent.
  - How: Ask "What would I do if I owned this fully?" Find examples, brainstorm, weigh pros/cons, then frame: "I'm thinking of A or B; I suggest A because ___ — do you feel differently?" Prefer multiple-choice, yes/no, or default-with-deadline over open-ended "What do you think?"

## Key Concepts
- **Accountable**: A fancy word for "having your reputation on the line if something goes wrong."
- **Ownership**: Taking responsibility for an assignment as if it were your own reputation at stake — including knowing when to ask for help (not the same as being independent).
- **See the big picture**: Understanding the *goal* behind a task ("buy a cake" for a birthday vs. retirement vs. photo shoot) so you don't overlook what really matters.
- **Repeat it back**: Playing back what you heard ("Just to confirm, I will ___ — correct?") to catch misunderstandings; escalate to a written email when stakes are high (CYA).
- **Minimize others' workload**: Doing every step you can on a helper's behalf (attach the file, offer times in their zone, include the link) so they're more likely to actually help.
- **Be predictable**: Surprising coworkers forces them to scramble; the less you surprise them, the more "user-friendly" and compatible you are.
- **Call to action**: A statement specifying the response you need — emails without a question mark or "Let me know" are easy to ignore.
- **Promote publicly, correct privately**: Flag others' errors discreetly; bring options (not just problems) to your manager.

## Mental Models
- Use "**Mediocre performers wait to be managed; high performers manage their managers**" as the test for proactivity (e.g., "Remembering for your manager": "I know you'll be away Monday, so how about Friday?").
- Think of asking questions as **climbing a ladder** — go up one rung at a time, never skip to the top.
- Treat "We'll figure it out" from your manager as code for "*You'll* figure it out."
- Think of the school→work shift as moving from **"I'll wait for instructions" to "I'll try to figure it out."**

## Anti-patterns
- **Doing the work before clarifying**: Acting on a fraction of the picture produces wrong work and looks incompetent.
- **Asking the same question twice**: Erodes your competence signal; take notes, repeat it back, or switch to a different person.
- **Surprising people with changes**: Changing code, taking vacation, or presenting without consulting affected parties forces them to scramble.
- **Disappearing after someone helps you**: Roughly 100% chance of annoyance — always send a thank-you; flattery is free and universally appreciated.
- **Submitting and waiting**: Finishing the task is the second-to-last step; presenting it well (with disclaimers, next steps, anticipated answers) is the last.

## Worked Example
Veronica, a newly graduated intern in her second week at a hospital, reported to a resident and checked everything out of fear of mistakes — always saying "Let me check for you" and chasing down her resident. Then a patient of hers was about to enter surgery when the anesthesiologist requested a postponement, and a team of senior people Veronica had never met (surgeon, surgical resident, pharmacist, nurse) demanded an immediate meeting. Panicked, she ran to her resident, who simply said, "Then why don't you go down there? This is your patient, no?" It dawned on her: *This is my patient. I am a member of the team.* She joined the meeting, the surgeon advised postponing, and though she thought "You're asking *me* to decide?" she said, "Yes, this sounds like the best course of action." She then took ownership for the first time — she explained the delay to the patient, called the family, and emailed the surgical team to thank them and propose a regroup — all without her resident's help. Her lesson: "It's a progression: you go from 'I'm going to do this — is that OK?' to 'I took care of it.'" Ownership isn't independence; it's responsibility, which includes knowing when to ask for help.

## Key Takeaways
1. Clarify *what, how, and by when* immediately — ambiguity is your job to resolve, not avoid.
2. Map RACI before starting so the right people are consulted and informed.
3. Work backward from the deadline and ask for interim check-ins; account for reviewers' availability.
4. Bundle questions, escalate one rung at a time, and always show your homework.
5. Give others something to react to and a clear call to action — managers know what they don't like, not what they want.
6. Minimize helpers' workload and thank them; be predictable to be compatible.
7. Before review, run the self-check: followed directions, focused on details that matter, clear call to action, easy-to-review format, anticipated questions, and a stated next step.
