# Chapter 15: Show Your Potential

## Core Idea
Promotion requires demonstrating not just performance (can you do your current job?) but potential (can you do the next one?). You earn it by finding an unoccupied "swimlane" that matters to your team and claiming it—because a promotion is fundamentally an investment in you that others want assurance will pay off.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Performance vs. Potential (the Nine-Box Matrix)**: You're evaluated on two axes—performance (effectiveness in your current role) and potential (expected effectiveness in your next role). Aim for the top-right corner where both are high.
  - When to use: positioning yourself for any promotion, raise, or full-time conversion.
  - How: performance answers "Can you do the current job well? Are you excited to be here? Do you get along with us?"; potential answers "Can you do the next job well? Are you excited to grow here? Can you lead us?" In HR-driven firms this is the formal nine-box matrix (and "high-potential" lists); in less-structured firms it lives unspoken in management's minds—making manager compatibility decisive.
  - Why it works / failure mode: failure mode is delivering pure performance and assuming it auto-converts to promotion; without visible potential you stay an individual contributor.
- **The Five Swimlane Questions**: Five prompts to find an unoccupied lane that matters to your team and claim it.
  - When to use: actively building a case for promotion.
  - How: (1) **What can I do that hasn't been done?**—deliver more/better/faster/cheaper. (2) **What can I fix that hasn't been fixed?**—target massive pains and mission-critical problems, not minor itches. (3) **What can I bridge that hasn't been bridged?**—be a translator, facilitator, or combiner. (4) **What can I know that others don't?**—become a tool, person, or topic expert. (5) **What can I share that hasn't been shared?**—offer reusable templates and relevant information.
- **Noticed/Reported filter**: Rank potential contributions by how likely higher-ups are to notice and report them; prioritize changes that are both better (not just different) and urgent/important. (Ketty's social-media recruiting would be noticed; cleaning the communal fridge wouldn't.)
- **Ask-for-it (the raise pitch)**: When already dominating the top-right corner but unrecognized, seek recognition yourself.
  - How: gather comparison data (Galina texted peers for salaries), build a one-page table, calculate the median, anchor to your closest peer, rehearse, open with appreciation, present the gap, and ask for support with a specific number ("14 percent more"). If "no," negotiate alternatives like a development budget.

## Key Concepts
- **Individual contributor (IC)**: Someone who doesn't manage anyone; promotion means proving yourself again at a higher level.
- **Performance**: How effectively you do your current job.
- **Potential**: How effectively you can be expected to do your next job—your capacity to go from executing to leading.
- **Unoccupied swimlane**: A valuable area of need no one else has claimed, which you can own to demonstrate potential.
- **Massive pain / mission-critical problem**: High-leverage problems (vs. minor itches) whose resolution most signals promotability; spotted by what higher-ups complain about most.
- **Translator / Facilitator / Combiner**: Three bridge roles—translating between domains, mediating between people, or fusing two domains into something new.
- **Local expert (tool / person / topic)**: You need only know slightly more than your teammates to be valuable—a useful antidote to impostor syndrome.
- **More / Better / Faster / Cheaper**: The four things most organizations value; achieving any boosts perceived potential.
- **Up-or-out / vacancy / new need**: The three circumstances under which promotions typically happen.

## Mental Models
- Think of work as a wilderness expedition, not school's conveyor belt—where you go and how fast is up to you and the wilderness.
- A promotion is an investment in you; make the "business case" that investing in you helps the organization hit its goals.
- Use "better, not just different" as a test before proposing any change—your idea must beat the old way, not merely differ from it.
- When you think "I understand both X and Y—why is it hard for everyone else?" you've found a bridge opportunity.
- Beware over-competence at a tool: if you're the sole expert, you can get trapped doing that task forever—be an expert only in what you want to be known for.

## Anti-patterns
- **Performance-only complacency**: Doing your job well but never showing you can lead.
- **Different vs. better**: Proposing change that's just your preferred way—threatening to old-guard supporters without clear upside.
- **Overstepping when fixing**: If someone isn't doing their job, ambition-as-intent can become aggression-as-impact; consult someone close to the problem first.
- **Waiting to be recognized**: No one cares more about your career than you; talent isn't auto-rewarded (bias and lack of opportunity are real).
- **Asking for a raise prematurely**: Don't ask before you're already dominating the top-right corner.
- **Self-praise only (or others-praise only)**: Mirror your team's norms—on a "wins of the quarter" team, praise others while subtly showcasing your own contribution.

## Worked Example
Ketty was a temporary office assistant whose only job was processing nurses' paperwork at a company that matched nurses to hospital jobs. She kept hearing executives complain they couldn't find enough nurse candidates despite high demand—a massive, mission-critical pain. She realized the firm relied on emails and phone calls while her own nursing friends found jobs on social media. She searched social platforms for "nurse" + "jobs," found dozens of groups with tens of thousands of nurses, screenshotted them, compiled a link list, and emailed her supervisor a plan to post openings there. The supervisor forwarded it up; the manager loved it and had Ketty help execute. She ran the campaign and built the company's social-media page, drawing thousands of followers within weeks. Four months into her six-month contract: "I hope you aren't going anywhere. We want you here full time." She converted a dead-end temp role into a newly created full-time marketing coordinator job and later became one of the youngest managers in the company—by delivering recruiting that was faster and cheaper, claiming an unoccupied swimlane the higher-ups were forced to invest in keeping.

## Key Takeaways
1. Promotion needs both high performance AND high potential—aim for the top-right of the nine-box matrix.
2. Find an unoccupied swimlane that matters and claim it using the five questions (do / fix / bridge / know / share).
3. Target massive pains and mission-critical problems higher-ups complain about, and propose changes that are better, not just different.
4. You only need to know slightly more than your teammates to be a valuable local expert—don't let impostor syndrome silence you.
5. How you bring ideas forward matters as much as the idea; consult those close to the problem to avoid coming across as aggressive.
6. If you're already excelling but unrecognized, ask—with data, a one-pager, a specific number, and appreciation (Galina's 14% pitch).
7. You won't always win: bias (lack of objectivity) and circumstances (lack of opportunity) can block deserved promotions—then it may be time to move on.
