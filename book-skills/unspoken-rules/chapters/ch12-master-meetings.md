# Chapter 12: Master Meetings

## Core Idea
Meetings are opportunities to strategically demonstrate your Three Cs (competence, commitment, compatibility). People assume your visible meeting behavior fully reflects how you're doing overall — so mastering meetings means knowing when to be seen and heard, seen but not heard, or neither seen nor heard, and preparing accordingly.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Seven Questions to Prepare for Any Meeting**: Before — (1) What will it be about and who's in the room? (2) What's my role? (3) What questions might I be asked? (4) What's my one thoughtful comment and one thoughtful question? During — (5) When should I speak up? (6) How can I best deliver my point? After — (7) What do I need to do to follow up?
  - When to use: For essentially every meeting; scale the depth to the meeting's importance.
  - How: Walk the questions in order before/during/after, defaulting to over-preparing when higher-ups, clients, or your own work are involved.
  - Why it works: People can't read your mind; preparation lets your behavior signal the competence and commitment you actually have.

- **The Three Meeting Roles**: Be seen and heard / be seen but not heard / be neither seen nor heard.
  - When to use: To calibrate participation to the meeting.
  - How: Seen & heard = small, internal, or your-topic meetings → contribute. Seen but not heard = larger meetings with higher-ups/clients or topics outside your swimlane → sit, listen, take notes, speak only if asked. Neither seen nor heard = calls between higher-ups across orgs → stay on mute, often take notes to send afterward. When unsure, ask your manager or mirror peers at your level.
  - Failure mode: Peter misread a "seen and heard" meeting as "seen but not heard" and got demoted to phone-only invitations.

- **One Thoughtful Comment + One Thoughtful Question**: Show up with at least one of each.
  - When to use: When you have prep time, especially if anyone you'd like to impress is present, or if you struggle to think on your feet.
  - How: Mine prereads for anything missing, problematic, confusing, wrong, or unexpected; write it down. "Thoughtful" just means important to the discussion. Usually takes under 30 minutes (hence 30-min gaps between meetings).

## Key Concepts
- **Three Cs**: Competence, commitment, compatibility — what meetings let you demonstrate.
- **Learner mode vs. leader mode**: When you're expected to ask questions vs. expected to know and contribute (from Ch 4); Peter failed at both.
- **Update vs. discussion meeting**: Turn-taking status shares vs. free-flowing conversation — but every meeting has a hidden goal: make a decision, share information, or reach understanding.
- **Unspoken responsibilities**: Even with no speaking role, you're generally expected to learn (especially if new) and to represent your team (especially if you're its only attendee).
- **Preread / prework**: Materials to read before a meeting — the raw ingredients for your thoughtful comment.
- **External narrative**: Your crisp self-introduction — "Hi, my name is ___ and I am the ___ in charge of ___."
- **Meeting ally / amplifier**: A trusted, often-heard coworker who calls on you, credits your ideas ("To Ayane's point"), or reminds the group whose idea it was.
- **RACI list**: Used when organizing — to pick attendees (who's Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed).

## Mental Models
- Think of meeting hierarchy as the same chain of command from Ch 10 — a director can wander in late and ask "What's this about?", but you'd be prepared if your own boss were present.
- Time your comment like planting a seed: bring up the promo video after the group warms to a marketing campaign — too early confuses, too late misses the window.
- Frame risky questions as "Here's my question, and here's why I'm asking it" or "Here's what I know, and here's what I don't" — almost no one calls that a stupid question.
- Lower your standards on contributing: a comment only has to nudge the group forward, not win a Nobel Prize — say it before someone else says it and gets the credit.

## Anti-patterns
- **Being silent in a "seen and heard" meeting**: Reads as disengagement regardless of your actual work (Peter "taking up space").
- **Up-talk**: Turning statements into questions by tone ("The analysis is on page 16?") signals you're unsure.
- **Speaking too fast or fidgeting**: Undercuts confidence — speak half as quickly, hold steady eye contact, sit up straight.
- **Letting a man repeat your idea and get credit**: Combat with chat-window typing, an amplifier, or putting your name on and emailing the materials yourself.
- **Pretending to understand**: An executive recruiter's warning — pretending works short-term, but those who ask and learn overtake you. Be honest when you don't know.

## Worked Example
**Peter at the VC firm.** Peter spent days on a strong research memo, sent it to colleagues, and figured his work was done. On the video call with the startup CEO, the managing partner, VP, and senior associate each fired sharp questions ("Slide six shows a crowded market — what makes you different?"); Peter, the most junior, listened on mute. Prompted with "We haven't heard from you — any questions?", he answered "Nope!" In the debrief he stayed muted, then when finally asked offered only, "I think it looks interesting… they seem like they have a lot of competitors, but they seem… interesting." Afterward the managing partner IM'd: "We need to talk about Peter. Does he not want to be here?… we can't have him taking up space like that." Peter was downgraded from video meetings to phone calls only. The misread: this was a "seen and heard" meeting where, as memo author, he was the most knowledgeable person and the spotlight was shared — yet he failed both learner mode (no questions) and leader mode (no substantive contribution), and never asked for feedback to recover.

## Key Takeaways
1. Meetings are where you demonstrate the Three Cs — people assume your visible behavior reflects your whole performance.
2. Before any meeting, learn its topic, attendees, type, and hidden goal so you know how much to prepare.
3. Diagnose your role — seen and heard, seen but not heard, or neither — and act accordingly; when unsure, ask or mirror peers.
4. Prepare answers to "What are you working on / status of ___ / tell us more about ___," and arrive with one thoughtful comment and one thoughtful question mined from prereads.
5. During the meeting, time your point strategically, deliver it clearly and confidently, and use connector phrases ("Building on ___'s point…").
6. Even when fighting to be heard, keep contributing — lower your standards, use safe question framings, and recruit amplifiers.
7. Follow up to reinforce your Three Cs; when organizing, run the seven steps from objective/agenda through clarifying next steps.
