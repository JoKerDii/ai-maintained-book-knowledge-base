# Chapter 5: Know How to Tell Your Story

## Core Idea
Every question a newcomer gets ("What do you want to do here?") is a hidden test of competence, commitment, and compatibility—and a chance to steer toward the experience you want. The skill is telling a tailored external narrative rather than dumping your internal one.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Internal Narrative vs. External Narrative**: the two stories you hold about your work.
  - When to use: any time someone asks why you're here or what you want.
  - How: build your internal narrative first (the honest "me, me, me" reasons—loans, experience, exploring a field), then translate it into an external narrative aimed at your audience that says "This is what I want … and this is how I am competent in and committed to this work." Lead with the external one; the overlap between them grows the closer the role is to your dream job.
  - Why it works: pure internal narrative ("I'm only here for me" / "I don't know") makes others question your commitment and compatibility—and is a common reason people get ghosted after interviews.
- **The Five Steps to Build Your Story**: a sequence for always having talking points ready.
  - When to use: preparing before you start a role; refining after each intro.
  - How: (1) Build your internal narrative (work backward from your last day—what will you want to have done?); (2) Build your external narrative (turn it into tailored talking points emphasizing transferable skills); (3) Add structure (use Hero's Journey for backward-looking questions, research-backed detail for forward-looking ones); (4) Finesse your style (don't overshoot/undershoot the Three Cs); (5) Practice.
- **The "Mental Fridge" / Refrigerator of Talking Points**: store brainstormed talking points as "ingredients" and pull the one that best fits each question.
  - When to use: in-the-moment, when asked something unexpected.
  - How: pre-stock ingredients, then organize them by completing—Competence: "I have what it takes to succeed here because ___"; Commitment: "I am excited to be here because ___"; Compatibility: "I belong in this role and team because ___".
- **The Hero's / Heroine's Journey (Past, Present, Future)**: structure for backward-looking questions.
  - When to use: "What brought you to our company?" and similar origin questions.
  - How: frame where you come from (past), what you're up to now (present), and the goal you're pursuing (future). Start at the most relevant point, skip forward fast, keep it two minutes not twenty; read the culture for how personal to get.
- **Maintaining the Three Cs (don't overshoot or undershoot)**: a style calibration for each C.
  - When to use: every self-description.
  - How: Competence between clueless ("I have no clue") and overbearing ("I'm an expert")—aim for "I'd like to think my experience with ___ translates to ___." Commitment between apathetic and threatening—aim for "I'm curious about ___," not "I expect to get promoted." Compatibility between passive and poser—aim for "I'm excited to join the team!" not over-eager agreement.

## Key Concepts
- **Internal narrative**: the private story of why you do what you do—what gets you out of bed.
- **External narrative**: the audience-tailored story that proves you're competent, committed, and compatible.
- **Transferable skills**: capabilities from any past role (babysitter = responsibility, tutor = simplifying concepts, cashier = multitasking under pressure) that bridge to the new job.
- **Mental fridge**: a stocked set of talking-point "ingredients" ready to be pulled to fit any question.
- **Overshooting / undershooting**: saying too much or too little within a zone of the Three Cs, reading as arrogant/threatening/poser or clueless/apathetic/passive.
- **Uptalking**: ending statements with rising intonation as if asking a question—undermines confidence.
- **Three Cs**: competence, commitment, compatibility—the qualities each answer should signal.

## Mental Models
- Use "going shopping with a list" when entering a role—you're likelier to walk out with what you want if you decided in advance what you want.
- Think of yourself as a celebrity facing reporters: people bombard you with questions and analyze every response, so take them seriously.
- Use "do your homework, then show your homework": layer in researched specifics ("if I'm not mistaken, one of your drugs just entered clinical trials?") to convert an answer into a commitment signal.
- Think of each literal question as having a hidden meaning—"What's up?" from a manager mid-crisis means "What are you working on?" not the casual greeting.

## Anti-patterns
- **Leading with the internal narrative**: "me, me, me" makes others doubt your commitment and compatibility.
- **Answering "anything you want / I'm flexible"**: forces others to invent your work, usually producing work you don't want and results no one cares about (Meghan).
- **"Well, in my last job, I…"**: repeated reference to a past job reads as overbearing—coworkers think "if it was so great, why are you here?"
- **Naming the one job your manager holds as your goal**: reads as threatening ("you want my job?").
- **Refusing work that doesn't match your goals**: at a small firm everything is everyone's problem; reads as apathetic.
- **Sounding robotic / cliché**: reciting a memorized speech, or "synergy/innovative/disrupt" and vague "I'm passionate about your values" without elaboration.

## Worked Example
Meghan, a co-op student at a biotech company, is asked on day one, "So, what do you want to do here?" She freezes—she'd assumed they'd have a project ready—and defaults to "Anything you want! I'm flexible." Over six months she cleans spreadsheets, sends emails, and summarizes papers, and when updating her résumé she stares at a blinking cursor with nothing to write. Her own diagnosis: had she researched the organization and clarified what she wanted, she could have proposed her own ideas and earned the experience she wanted; instead she made them invent work, yielding work she didn't want and results they didn't care about. The fix runs through the chapter's machinery: build an internal narrative (what do I want from this co-op?), translate it into an external narrative emphasizing transferable skills and team-relevant interests, structure backward-looking answers as past→present→future, and calibrate style to stay inside the Three Cs. A contrasting positive external narrative: "The company's work in Alzheimer's is especially interesting to me because neuroscience was a favorite college class, and I volunteered at an eldercare facility and saw its effects on my grandfather"—a personal connection reframed as competence + commitment.

## Key Takeaways
1. Get clear on what you want first; you can't steer toward a fulfilling experience you haven't named.
2. Tell the external narrative—tailored to your audience to show competence, commitment, compatibility—not the raw internal one.
3. Every literal question hides a deeper one; answer the real question.
4. Stock a "mental fridge" of talking points so you're never caught without an answer.
5. Frame origin stories as a Hero's Journey: past, present, future—two minutes, not twenty.
6. Calibrate style to avoid overshooting (arrogant/threatening/poser) or undershooting (clueless/apathetic/passive) each of the Three Cs.
7. Delivery matters: confident not arrogant, proper not robotic, upbeat not immature—and watch listeners' body language to retune.
