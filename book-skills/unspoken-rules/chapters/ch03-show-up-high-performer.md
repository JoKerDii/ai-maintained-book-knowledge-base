# Chapter 3: Show Up Like a High Performer

## Core Idea
You can make a strong first impression before day one by researching your organization until you can "see the big picture," and then converting that knowledge into *good questions* — because people can't read your mind, so you must show your homework to get credit for it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Pre-Day-One Research (15 strategies, higher- and lower-priority)**: A checklist for showing up already knowing what the organization does, what it's been up to, who its competitors are, and who the key people are.
  - When to use: Preparing for a new role, networking call, professional meeting, or job interview — ideally turned into permanent habits.
  - How (higher-priority, ~45 min): skim the org's Wikipedia page; skim its website "About us / What we do / News" pages; Google recent articles; memorize names and faces of at least the head of the org, your department, and your team; find people CC'd in your emails and look them up on LinkedIn; observe how employees dress; confirm logistics (where, when, what to bring, arrive 15 min early); set a Google Alert for the org; subscribe to its blog/newsletter; accept any offer to chat. (Lower-priority): research competitors and set alerts for them; ask mutual LinkedIn connections for intros; cold-email people (guess their email) for 30-min calls; at each call's end ask "Is there anyone else you'd suggest I speak with?"; check Glassdoor, Reddit, and podcasts.
  - Why it works / failure mode: The point isn't trivia — it's filling in "the eight blanks" to see the big picture the way higher-ups do, signaling you're a thought partner, not just a workhorse.

- **The Three Circles of question quality (good vs. bad questions)**: A model for whether a question is worth asking.
  - When to use: Before asking anything when you're new.
  - How: Inner circle = what you already know. Middle circle = what you don't know but *can figure out yourself* (search, re-read emails) — questions here are **bad** ("I found the answer in ten seconds online"). Outer circle = what you *can't* learn alone, only by asking — questions here are **good**.
  - Why it works / failure mode: A stupid question is one whose answer is obvious, imminent, or easy to obtain. Bad questions make people wonder "what else will I need to do for you?"

- **"Do — and show — your homework"**: The structure for asking so you get credit.
  - When to use: Every time you ask a question as a newbie.
  - How: Use "Here's my question, and here's why I'm asking it," or "Here's what I know, and here's what I don't know." Explain where the question comes from *before* asking it.

## Key Concepts
- **Seeing the big picture / the eight blanks**: Stepping back to understand how the org and your role fit into the whole — the mindset of senior leaders.
- **Thought partner vs. workhorse**: The perception you earn by demonstrating big-picture understanding rather than just executing tasks.
- **Stupid question**: One whose answer is obvious (already given), imminent (answers itself soon), or easy to obtain (searchable).
- **Good question**: One you genuinely cannot answer on your own and can only learn by asking.
- **"'Do you have any questions?' is a 'yes' question"**: The unspoken rule that you should always have a question ready — and a good one.
- **Show your work for partial credit**: Like school — narrate the effort behind your question so people credit your competence and commitment.
- **Good-questions credit**: Asking good questions early earns leeway to ask bad ones later.

## Mental Models
- Picture three nested circles before asking anything: if your question lives in the middle ring (figure-out-able), answer it yourself; ask only from the outer ring.
- Think of preparation as building lifelong *habits*, not completing a one-time chore — searching news and looking people up should become automatic.
- When you blow a first impression, treat your *response* as the real test: self-deprecating ownership ("I guess I need to do my homework!") beats shriveling up.

## Anti-patterns
- **Showing up like "a kid in a suit"**: George the intern didn't recognize his own CEO in the elevator and became known all summer as "the intern who didn't know the CEO."
- **Asking bad questions**: Questions answerable obviously, imminently, or by a 10-second search make people doubt how much hand-holding you'll need.
- **Hoarding your research silently**: Doing the homework but not surfacing it — people can't read your mind, so you get no credit.
- **Telling people how to do their jobs**: Over-sharing knowledge as an overbearing newbie (overshoots competence).

## Worked Example
**Sana vs. George — two day-ones.** After Sana (an operations manager at a ride-hailing startup) got her offer, five people emailed her the rote "Let me know if you have any questions!" Instead of treating it as a chore, she reframed it as a relationship-building opportunity: she scheduled a call with each, and before every call researched the person's LinkedIn and blog posts and prepared tailored questions. She turned five emails into five conversations and five allies — so her first day "didn't feel like a first day at all," and she even joined a product launch led by someone she'd met by phone. George, an intern at a bank, rode an elevator with the CEO, didn't recognize her, asked "What about you?", and learned she was the CEO. A coworker spread the story; for the whole summer he was "the intern who didn't know the CEO." The author's verdict: Sana looked like a professional and flexed her Three Cs by thinking one step ahead and seeing the big picture; George missed the chance — but a blown first impression isn't fatal, and how you respond (owning it with humor) matters more than the slip itself.

## Key Takeaways
1. Make your first impression before day one — show up already knowing what the org does, its recent news, its competitors, and its key people.
2. Run the ~45-minute higher-priority research checklist for any new role, call, meeting, or interview, and turn it into a habit.
3. Memorize the faces/names of the head of the org, your department, and your team so you never become "the one who didn't know the CEO."
4. Always have a question, and make it a *good* one — never ask something obvious, imminent, or easily searchable.
5. Use the three-circles test: answer middle-ring questions yourself; ask only outer-ring questions.
6. Show your homework with "Here's my question and why I'm asking" or "Here's what I know and what I don't" — get partial credit for the work you did.
7. Asking good questions early buys you leeway to ask bad ones later.
