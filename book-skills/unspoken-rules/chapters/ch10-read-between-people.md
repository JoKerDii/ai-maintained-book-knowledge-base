# Chapter 10: Read between the People

## Core Idea
An organization is not just a collection of people; it is a web of hidden relationships and invisible boundaries. The most important people aren't always at the top, and decoding who has power, who is responsible for what, and what's "off limits" determines your compatibility and your ability to get things done.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Five Steps to Read between the People**: Identify (1) the chain of command, (2) the influencers, (3) the swimlanes, (4) the loyalties, and (5) the comfort zones.
  - When to use: Whenever you join a new team or organization, before taking initiative or sharing work.
  - How: Observe behavior and language patterns to map each layer in turn; when uncertain, default to asking for permission and respecting hierarchy.
  - Why it works: Most impact comes from navigating relationships, not from idea brilliance; mapping hidden structure prevents overshooting your "zone of commitment" and appearing threatening.

- **The Five Influencer Types**: Gatekeepers, Veterans, Experts, Socialites, Advisers — the people with informal power to sway decisions even without formal authority.
  - When to use: After mapping formal power, to find who really moves decisions.
  - How: Watch for patterns — decisions routed through one person (gatekeeper), someone always consulted (veteran/expert/socialite), a colleague your manager always cites (adviser). Introduce yourself and be especially gracious with them.
  - Failure mode: Influencers can be "door closers" — a rude greeting to an executive assistant (Christian) ended an interview before it began.

- **The Ask-for-Permission vs. Beg-for-Forgiveness Spectrum**: A culture-diagnostic for how rigid swimlanes are.
  - When to use: To decide whether to clear an action before acting or act and then inform.
  - How: "Ask for permission" cultures value specific responsibilities, titles, and the safe way → ask "Is there anyone I should speak with before moving forward?" "Beg for forgiveness" cultures value best ideas and quick/innovative ways → say "I wanted to let you know that I did ___." When in doubt, ask for permission.

## Key Concepts
- **Org chart**: A diagram of each person and their reporting lines; solid lines = direct manager, dotted lines = secondary manager.
- **Skip manager**: The boss of your boss.
- **Zone of commitment**: The scope of what you're authorized to take on; overshooting it makes you appear threatening (as Alison did).
- **Swimlane**: What a person is responsible for and when — not the same as their job title ("stay in their lane" = "stop doing my job").
- **Influencer**: Someone with leverage to influence decisions without formal authority to make them.
- **First loyalties**: People may be friendly or loyal to you, but their first loyalty may be to someone else — so anything you share may travel.
- **Comfort zones**: The behaviors, jokes, language, and topics a team finds appropriate vs. taboo.
- **Clique**: A tight group whose members share information among themselves; what you tell one likely reaches all.

## Mental Models
- Think of an organization as a web of invisible lines, not a list of names — read the relationships, not just the roster.
- Use behavior as an X-ray when no org chart exists: people stand straighter, defer attention, and start meetings only when the real boss arrives.
- Everyone has a boss, even the bosses — CEOs answer to boards, founders to investors, self-funded entrepreneurs to clients. Whoever pays the bills calls the shots.
- Mirror to find limits: recognize patterns of awkward pauses and topic-switches to learn the comfort zone, since few can articulate it explicitly.

## Anti-patterns
- **Overshooting the zone of commitment**: Three forms — going over someone's head before trying to resolve one-on-one, making someone look bad in front of their boss, or ignoring rank when people of different levels are together.
- **Assuming "flat" means non-hierarchical**: Flat orgs often just erased the lines while keeping the structure, making informal power even more decisive — be alert.
- **Mistaking friendliness for loyalty**: The teacher criticized her principal to a "nice" vice principal who was the principal's close friend — and it got back to him.
- **Acting before asking when remote**: You often can't see who's working on what until you've already overstepped.

## Worked Example
Alison used her break to clean a messy supply closet. Sue, the head of the department, snapped at her to stop, then confronted her again the next morning in front of her manager Michael, who stayed silent. Sue insisted, "That's my job. Not yours." Privately, Michael explained the hidden context: the closet had been a disaster for years, but Sue stored her personal things there, and once someone had thrown out her belongings without telling her — so she'd been paranoid about it ever since. Alison had seen only an empty, messy closet; she'd missed the invisible boundary surrounding it. By acting helpfully but blindly, she overshot her zone of commitment and became threatening to Sue. The fix: map the people and their boundaries before taking initiative, and when in doubt, ask first ("I'll make sure to ask first next time").

## Key Takeaways
1. The most important people aren't always at the top — map informal power, not just titles.
2. Learn the chain of command first; when in doubt, respect the invisible hierarchy.
3. Find and cultivate the five influencer types — they can open or close doors regardless of rank.
4. Identify swimlanes and read the ask-permission vs. beg-forgiveness culture; when unsure, ask for permission and keep your manager CC'd.
5. Uncover first loyalties and cliques — assume what you share may travel to allies you haven't seen.
6. Learn the comfort zones by recognizing patterns and mirroring; stay within the limits.
