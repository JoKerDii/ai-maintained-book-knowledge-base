# Chapter 1: The Three Cs: Competence, Commitment, Compatibility

## Core Idea
The minute you start a new role, managers, coworkers, and clients silently judge you on three questions — Can you do the job well? Are you excited to be here? Do you get along with us? — and you must get a "Yes" on all three, because perception of each C matters as much as the reality.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Cs (Competence, Commitment, Compatibility)**: The master framework for the whole book. People trust and invest in you only when you demonstrate all three Cs at once (visualized as the overlapping center of a Venn diagram).
  - When to use: As a diagnostic lens on every workplace interaction — when something feels "off" with how you're perceived, identify which C is failing.
  - How: (1) Competence = do the job fully, accurately, promptly, without micromanagement and without making others look bad. (2) Commitment = be fully present and eager to help the team's goals. (3) Compatibility = make others comfortable and eager to be around you. For each, avoid both undershooting and overshooting (see anti-patterns).
  - Why it works / failure mode: Showing only one or two Cs isn't enough — people won't trust you with important work (no competence), won't invest their time in you (no commitment), or won't want to spend time with you (no compatibility). Each C has a precise dual failure mode, so the goal is the center, not the maximum.

- **Perceived vs. Actual (for each C)**: Reality and perception of a C don't automatically align.
  - When to use: When you're working hard but not getting credit, or doing fine work but being judged poorly.
  - How: Recognize that managers often measure inputs (visible progress, confidence in meetings, self-promotion) rather than hard-to-measure outputs. Manage the perceived version deliberately, not just the actual one.

## Key Concepts
- **Competence**: Doing your job fully, accurately, and promptly without needing to be micromanaged — and without making others look bad.
- **Commitment**: Being fully present and eager to help your team achieve its goals.
- **Compatibility**: Making others comfortable and eager to be around you without seeming inauthentic.
- **Perceived competence/commitment**: How competent or committed others believe you are, which can matter as much as the reality.
- **Undershooting vs. overshooting**: Each C fails in two opposite directions — too little and too much.
- **"Cultural fit"**: A label often used to mask unconscious bias about identity (clothes, accent, name, background) rather than genuine teamwork ability.
- **The Venn diagram of the Three Cs**: A map where different people start in different positions and have different distances to travel to reach the trusted center.
- **Uniqueness as a superpower**: The author's reframe that identity differences are an asset, not a liability.

## Mental Models
- Think of every new colleague as silently asking three questions about you; your daily job is to keep answering "Yes" to all three.
- Use the undershoot/overshoot test before acting: for any move, ask "Could this read as too little (clueless/apathetic/passive) OR too much (overbearing/threatening/poser)?" Aim for the middle.
- Treat the workplace as not a level playing field — for some, competence is assumed; for others, it must be proven. Locate your own starting point honestly so you know which C needs the most work.

## Anti-patterns
- **Undershooting competence (clueless)**: Going silent or missing deadlines — like the intern fired for ignoring her manager's calls for a week.
- **Overshooting competence (overbearing know-it-all)**: Lecturing veterans on the "better" method — like the new teacher pushing 5E Lesson Planning.
- **Undershooting commitment (apathetic)**: Looking mellow, phone out, low energy — the camp counselor accused of laziness despite working hard.
- **Overshooting commitment (threatening)**: Doing teammates' work unasked and correcting your manager in front of higher-ups — the intern who lost his offer.
- **Undershooting compatibility (passive)**: Polite but no smiling or small talk — the cashier who failed probation.
- **Overshooting compatibility (poser)**: Breaking an unspoken norm to stand out — the MBA grad who spoke before the senior person and got eye-rolls.

## Worked Example
Six paired vignettes anchor the chapter, one for each failure direction. The clearest contrast is **commitment**: A summer camp counselor was branded "lazy" by the director even though he worked hard and took extra tasks. A fellow counselor pointed out the real problem — not effort, but *enthusiasm*: he looked mellow and kept his phone out while others acted "like they were in a musical about peppy camp counselors." He changed nothing about his actual output; he simply threw on a smile, walked faster, and added a spring to his step. Within weeks the director took him seriously. The lesson: perceived commitment, signaled through small visible behaviors, can override the reality of your effort. The author also shares his own story — as a first-gen, low-income student of Asian descent at an elite school, he struggled with all three Cs (mistakes hurt competence, slow email replies hurt commitment, no shared small-talk hurt compatibility) and stayed silent to avoid looking like an impostor.

## Key Takeaways
1. Win "Yes" on all three questions — Can you do the job? Are you excited? Do you get along? Two out of three is not enough.
2. Manage perception, not just reality; managers judge inputs when outputs are hard to measure.
3. For each C, avoid both extremes — undershooting and overshooting both break trust.
4. Small signals (replying fast, smiling, showing up early, speaking up) carry disproportionate weight in how your Cs are read.
5. Know your starting position in the Venn diagram — identity and bias mean different people travel different distances to the center.
6. Treat your uniqueness as a superpower to be unleashed, not a liability to hide.
