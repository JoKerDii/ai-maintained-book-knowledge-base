# Chapter 14: Resolve Conflicts

## Core Idea
When something feels wrong at work you have exactly three options—fix it, live with it, or leave it—and choosing well requires diagnosing the root cause first. Pursue the path of least regret, not the path of fastest relief or least effort.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Fix it / Live with it / Leave it**: The three responses to any difficult workplace situation.
  - When to use: whenever anxiety, frustration, or exhaustion crosses your mind at work.
  - How: diagnose the problem, evaluate options against several lenses, then fix tactfully or leave graciously. Default to "fix it" first if the issue is one a conversation with your manager could resolve.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Kathryn jumped straight to "leave it" without trying to fix it, chasing greener grass for two years only to return to her original firm at the same level—proving the path of fastest relief is often an unsustainable quick fix.
- **People / Position / Place (the three flavors of root cause)**: Every workplace problem traces to one of three sources.
  - When to use: Step 1, diagnosing the root cause.
  - How: **People problem** = an issue with a specific manager, coworker, or client. **Position problem** = you dislike the day-to-day work, career path, pay, or benefits. **Place problem** = you're worried about the organization's direction, leadership, or values. Then ask the matching escape question: people→switch teams? position→switch professions? place→switch organizations?
- **The "Why?" exercise**: Repeatedly ask "Why is this happening?" to drill from symptom to root cause.
  - How: Kathryn's chain—no motivation → don't enjoy it → people aren't great → working with a new manager with a different style → Aha! The single root cause (the manager) underlay boring work, travel, and poor mentorship.
- **The Puddle (locality)**: Picture your problem as a puddle—small (a set of people), medium (your employer), or large (your whole role/profession)—to judge how far you must step to escape and how feasible that is.
- **10-10-10**: Suzy Welch's time-travel test—which option feels best 10 minutes, 10 months, and 10 years from now? Surfaces the path of least regret and strips emotion from the decision.
- **The Leave-it Ten-Question Test**: Ten yes/no questions (tried living with it? asked allies? identified what you want? engaged the individual? escalated? tried switching teams? exhausted options? reached patience limits? secured an alternative job? convinced it solves the problem?). The more "yes," the more leaving makes sense.

## Key Concepts
- **Root cause**: The hidden single problem generating the racing thoughts and lost motivation—often not "everything," just one thing.
- **Path of least regret**: The choice you'll feel best about long-term, as opposed to the path of fastest relief or least effort.
- **Trade-off triangle**: No role offers good people + good position + good place all at once; you choose what to value and what to sacrifice.
- **Boomerang employee**: Someone who leaves, works elsewhere, and returns—often at the same level/pay they left.
- **Locality of a problem (puddle size)**: How broadly the problem extends, determining whether switching teams, professions, or organizations would solve it.
- **Intent vs. awareness**: Many conflicts persist only because the other person doesn't realize their negative impact—"What? I had no idea!"—making a single conversation the fix.
- **Discreet job hunt**: Searching for a new role without signaling it (personal email/device, off-calendar, LinkedIn discretion) until you're ready to announce.
- **Last impression**: The lasting impression made on departure, which can matter as much as the first impression—a low bar most people fail because they check out.

## Mental Models
- Use journaling (what you did/learned/felt/enjoyed/disliked, daily for a month) when the root cause won't reveal itself quickly.
- Think "fix it before leave it" when the change is reasonable, needs few/low-ranking people to approve, and the other party may be unaware of their impact.
- When proposing a fix, give people "something to react to": "This is the problem and this is my proposal" beats "I don't like what I've been given."
- Think of leaving as deliberate as entering—wind down, document, and depart on the least-disruptive date.

## Anti-patterns
- **Leaving before diagnosing or fixing**: Kathryn's two-year detour for grass that was greenest where she started.
- **Demanding instead of collaborating**: "I need ___" or "___ is dumb" reads as entitled; reframe to "I'd love to find a way for us to better ___" and "I'd love your advice on ___."
- **Assuming negative intent**: Pre-empt defensiveness with "I know you [positive intent], so I suspect it wasn't your intent, but [negative impact]."
- **Proposing permanent changes**: People resist commitment; offer low-stakes experiments ("Could we try…") instead.
- **Checking out once you announce departure**: Burns the last impression and future references.

## Worked Example
Kathryn, a consultant, loved her job until her favorite manager left and she was reassigned to a senior director with the opposite style: kept her off client work, sent her on pointless weeklong travel, never mentored her. Within weeks she lost all motivation and—without trying to fix anything—jumped to an asset-management firm. It lacked role models; she lasted nine months, tried a smaller firm, quit within a year, and boomeranged back to her original consulting firm at her old level and pay while her cohort had been promoted to manager. Two years lost. The book reconstructs the fix she never attempted: a low-pressure "pick your brain" meeting opening with appreciation ("I really appreciate you being so generous"), framed entirely as asking for advice ("how you went about finding your niche"), and surfacing the real issue indirectly—"the travel expectation has made it hard to attend the Friday lunch-and-learns; any advice on how to navigate this?" The manager responds: "We can definitely work something out. Let me talk to the client." A single tactful conversation could have spared her the two-year detour, because people can't read your mind.

## Key Takeaways
1. Three options always exist: fix it, live with it, leave it—don't collapse straight to leaving.
2. Diagnose first: classify the root cause as People, Position, or Place, then ask the matching escape question.
3. Use the "Why?" chain and journaling to find the single hidden root cause behind many symptoms.
4. Weigh locality (puddle size), temporariness, and 10-10-10 to choose the path of least regret.
5. Fix tactfully: show appreciation, assume positive intent, ask for advice, offer experiments, and give people a concrete proposal.
6. Many conflicts persist only because others are unaware of their impact—try a conversation first.
7. If you leave, leave graciously: hunt discreetly, give ample notice, wind down, say thoughtful goodbyes, and stay in touch—the last impression lasts.
