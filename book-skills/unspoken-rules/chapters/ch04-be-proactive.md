# Chapter 4: Be Proactive

## Core Idea
No one will care more about your success—or know more about what you need—than you, so the workplace favors the proactive. When no one structures your onboarding or assigns you work, think like an owner and structure it yourself.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Twelve "Yes!" Onboarding Questions ("Learn Your Job")**: a checklist of twelve questions you should be able to answer "Yes!" to when starting a role.
  - When to use: any first day / ramp-up period, structured or chaotic, in person or remote.
  - How: confirm you have (1) submitted paperwork, (2) met supervisor(s), (3) clarified reporting lines, (4) clarified role expectations, (5) found a regular interaction schedule with your manager, (6) introduced yourself to coworkers, (7) learned the team's priorities, (8) defined your day-to-day work schedule, (9) set up workspace/tools/access, (10) gotten access to files and calendar invites, (11) navigated your work environment, (12) sorted out your daily routine. If no one helps you, ask coworkers at your level or HR with scripted questions.
  - Why it works: it converts an unstructured ramp-up into an explicit, ownable to-do list so you set yourself up for success rather than waiting on busy managers.
- **Find Your Job (three strategies to generate your own work)**: when given tasks but no real role, manufacture usefulness.
  - When to use: after onboarding, when teammates reach out but have no plan for you (especially remote).
  - How: (1) Observe others and take notes ("Would it be helpful for me to join this meeting?" / "Mind if I observe how you do this?"); (2) Ask questions and learn from the answers (keep a running question list; do—then show—your homework); (3) Embrace and volunteer for work (after meetings, when people complain, or via "How can I be helpful?").
- **Learner Mode vs. Leader Mode**: two modes you occupy in any role that govern how much you're expected to ask.
  - When to use: calibrating whether to speak up. New = always learner mode.
  - How: in learner mode people expect questions, so always have questions; in leader mode people expect you to know what's going on and ask thoughtful questions/comments.

## Key Concepts
- **Think like an owner**: treat the gaps in your own onboarding and role as your problem to solve, not someone else's.
- **"First day of school" vs. "first day in a new country"**: two onboarding extremes—fully guided vs. nobody knows who you are; most first days land in between.
- **Learner mode**: the period when others expect you not to know much and therefore to ask questions.
- **Leader mode**: the later period when others expect you to know what's going on.
- **Voluntelling**: when a higher-up assigns you a task while framing it as optional; treat "Can you do this?" as a "Yes!" question, not yes/no.
- **Mini tests of competence and commitment**: tedious early tasks that are actually evaluations in disguise.
- **Transferable usefulness**: hunting for ways to help by listening for what teammates are stressed about or complaining about.

## Mental Models
- Use "school is about keeping up; work is about stepping up" when deciding whether to wait for instructions—school has a syllabus, work does not.
- Think of every new-job task request as a "Yes!" question: "Yes! And would it be helpful if I also did ___?"
- Think of complaints in meetings as hidden opportunities—a complaint is an unstated request for help.
- Use the rule "volunteer only if you have time": commitment without competence (failing to deliver) costs more than not volunteering.

## Anti-patterns
- **Sitting around waiting for opportunities**: managers are busy and may not even notice you arrived; passivity reads as low commitment.
- **Staying quiet to avoid looking clueless**: silence is the opposite of what people expect from a newcomer—asking questions signals commitment.
- **Asking questions only for show**: questions are also for your learning; if you don't take notes and remember, you waste them and erode trust.
- **Over-volunteering**: saying yes to more than you can deliver sacrifices competence to look committed.

## Worked Example
Two contrasting first days. Jabril, in a brewing company's rotational trainee program, lives the "first day of school": he picks up his name tag, backpack, laptop, branded water bottle, and training schedule, and finds a welcome message plus a mass introduction email already waiting. Valeria, arriving at an oil field, lives the "first day in a new country": the site supervisor greets her with "Who are you?"—he either wasn't told or forgot she was coming. Ng's point is that most readers land between these poles, and the response is identical regardless. Where structure exists (Jabril), take it. Where it's absent (Valeria), don't wait—work the twelve questions yourself: ask "Who will I be reporting to?", request a 30–60 minute intro with your manager, walk around introducing yourself ("Hi, I don't think we've met. I'm ___. I'm the new ___"), ask coworkers their top priorities, set up tools before you need them, and request file/calendar access. The lesson distilled in "Try This": if no one introduces themselves, you introduce yourself; if no one shares information, you ask for it; if no one finds you work, you find the work.

## Key Takeaways
1. The workplace favors the proactive—your success is everyone's interest, so asking for what you need is demonstrating commitment, not being annoying.
2. Aim to answer "Yes!" to all twelve onboarding questions; return to the list whenever the ramp-up is chaotic.
3. When you have no assigned role, generate one: observe and take notes, ask and learn, then volunteer.
4. You're always in learner mode when new—so always have questions, and always take notes on the answers.
5. Treat "Can you do this?" as a "Yes!" question, ideally "Yes! And would it be helpful if I also did ___?"
6. Volunteer only if you have time; delivering on what you promise is what converts commitment into credit.
