# Chapter 2: Think, "Let's Give This a Shot!"

## Core Idea
Opportunities constantly whiz past like road signs on a highway, but they're worthless unless you seize them — and the mindset that seizes them is "Who knows? Let's give this a shot," driven by the insight that the worst-case answer is usually just "no."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"Let's give this a shot" (the seize-the-opportunity mindset)**: The core mental posture for spotting and acting on opportunities others let pass.
  - When to use: Whenever you face a crazy idea that probably won't work but might; something you'll probably fail at but might succeed at; something you probably won't like but might.
  - How: (1) Notice the opportunity (the road sign). (2) Ask "What's the worst that could happen?" — usually just a "no." (3) Reframe the fear as a fear of *judgment*, not danger. (4) Act anyway, genuinely and without entitlement. (5) Let other people be the ones to tell you "no."
  - Why it works / failure mode: Expectations of a new hire are at their lowest, so you can set them on your terms ("Know the rules. Then bend them."). Failure mode: tipping from curiosity into entitlement — demanding rather than asking damages your compatibility.

- **Curiosity vs. Entitlement test**: A guardrail for how far to push.
  - When to use: Before asking for something beyond the "usual process."
  - How: Imagine getting a "no." If your honest reaction would be "No worries — just thought I'd ask," proceed. If it would be "How come?! That's ridiculous!", don't ask yet. Ask genuinely, without expecting the answer.

- **"Some people need to be less entitled; some need to be more"**: Calibrate based on your context.
  - When to use: Deciding whether to push for more or hold back.
  - How: If you already received an unearned advantage (e.g., a connection-based opportunity), dial entitlement *down* and over-show willingness to learn. If you tend to wait for permission and want something, dial it *up* — if you want it, strive for it; if unsure you can have it, ask.

## Key Concepts
- **Opportunities as road signs**: Chances that are valuable only if the "driver" recognizes and acts on them.
- **"Do the bare minimum, get the bare minimum"**: Outcomes scale with the initiative you put in.
- **Fear of judgment vs. fear of danger**: Most workplace risks threaten only your ego, not your safety.
- **"Think like an owner"**: If you don't look after your career, no one else will.
- **Hero of your own journey**: Everyone is too busy thinking about themselves to be thinking about you, so self-advocacy isn't as conspicuous as it feels.
- **"Grow a lot by growing a little"**: Improvement compounds; the important thing is just to start trying.
- **Context/bias caveat**: Women, people defying stereotypes, and those without insider connections may need to maneuver harder to get noticed.

## Mental Models
- Think of yourself as the *driver* on a highway of road-sign opportunities — seeing the sign is nothing; pulling over is everything.
- Use "Where you start off does not have to dictate where you end up" when discouraged by your starting position.
- Before backing down, run the worst-case test: if the worst outcome is hearing "no," the real risk is small — leave the "no" to other people.
- Use "if you want more than the bare minimum, do more than the bare minimum" when tempted to wait for instructions.

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting around for others to decide your future**: Opportunities not on the official menu still exist; passivity hands your future to someone else.
- **Limiting yourself before you've given yourself a chance**: Pre-rejecting your own request because of less experience, not being known, or "that's not the process."
- **Tipping into entitlement**: Demanding, or being overly disappointed by a "no," which threatens your compatibility.

## Worked Example
**Annie's rotation.** A new grad at an oil and gas company was placed on her third-choice team and grew restless; the team she actually wanted wasn't even on HR's preference survey. Her assigned "buddy" pushed back on her assumption: "The survey doesn't make the decision — people do." Annie emailed the SVP, Chiderah, with a tight pitch — a specific reason for interest, a relevant credential (grant-proposal experience), and three concrete availability windows. Two weeks of silence, then a follow-up email. Another week of silence. Seeing Chiderah online, Annie fought the fear ("Do the thing you fear most and the death of fear is certain"), drafted a low-key chat message in a separate document so she couldn't send it by accident, then sent it. Chiderah called: no role available — but invited Annie to a women's book club with six senior executives. When Chiderah's group email ("What do you all think?") got no replies for a week, Annie replied-all with an actual drafted plan. Chiderah: "Love it. Let's do it." They launched the company's first women's book club; Annie went from knowing no senior leaders to knowing a dozen, and Chiderah eventually pulled her onto the team via HR — making Annie the first person ever placed on that team for a rotation. Annie's own framing: she was "insecure the entire time" but gave herself credit for past wins, and learned from getting a C on her first college essay (which she fixed by asking the professor "Tell me everything I'm doing wrong" and ending with the top grade) that "you can grow a lot by growing a little."

## Key Takeaways
1. Adopt "Let's give this a shot" — opportunities only count if you seize them.
2. Run the worst-case test: a "no" is a fear of judgment, not danger; let others say it.
3. Do more than the bare minimum if you want more than the bare minimum; create opportunities that aren't on the menu.
4. Keep curiosity from becoming entitlement — ask genuinely, accept "no" gracefully.
5. Calibrate to context: be less entitled if handed an unearned advantage, more entitled if you habitually wait for permission.
6. Where you start does not have to dictate where you end up — look forward, and give yourself credit for how far you've come.
