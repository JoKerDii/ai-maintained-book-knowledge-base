# Chapter 9: Manage Your Workload

## Core Idea
You'll never have enough hours to do everything, so staying competent while staying sane means correctly judging what's urgent and important, juggling priorities by avoiding surprises, and recognizing patterns so they work for you instead of against you.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four Factors of Urgency**: Urgency at work is more than the nearest deadline.
  - When to use: When deciding how soon a task must be addressed.
  - How: Weigh **Proximity** (how close the deadline / how long others have waited), **Scrutiny** (how much leverage the requester has over you), **Anxiety** (how eager others are to move on — mirror their urgency), and **Time sensitivity** (whether options shrink or the task blocks other work as time passes).

- **Three Factors of Importance**: The "points" a task is worth are unspoken at work.
  - When to use: When deciding how much a task matters and how well to do it.
  - How: Weigh **Centrality** (how core the task is to your role — have-to-do vs. nice-to-do), **Scrutiny** (how many people, and how powerful, are affected or watching), and **Criticality** (how much it matters to a project the team/company cares about — clue: higher-ups asking for regular updates).

- **Eisenhower Matrix (author's adaptation)**: Cross urgency with importance to decide action.
  - When to use: When triaging a list of competing tasks.
  - How: Important + urgent → **do immediately**. Important, not urgent → **schedule for later**. Urgent, not important → **timebox** it (or delegate if you're a manager). Neither → **remove it from your life**. If many tasks are both, force-rank them.
  - Failure mode: It only captures *others'* sense of importance (mainly your manager's), which isn't sustainable long-term — what you, your manager, and your company find important can diverge.

- **Identify Problems at Their Root Cause**: Don't patch symptoms; ask "Why?" repeatedly until you find the underlying cause.
  - When to use: When a negative pattern appears (repeating error, recurring complaint, equipment malfunction) — if it happens twice, don't let it happen a third time.
  - How: Keep asking "Why is this happening?" until the root surfaces; report a hypothesis to your manager, then return with the cause plus a few solutions to choose from.

- **Preempt Patterns**: Others' habits are patterns you can get ahead of.
  - When to use: Whenever you can predict a recurring behavior.
  - How: Clear your schedule after holidays if a boss creates post-holiday fire drills; send a progress update before the Friday your manager always asks; template repeated emails/workplans for copy-paste reuse.

## Key Concepts
- **Promotable tasks**: Work the higher-ups care about (building features, making money) that helps you get ahead.
- **Nonpromotable tasks / "office housework"**: Note-taking, ordering lunch, organizing events — contribute to the greater good but don't advance your career unless they're in your job description.
- **Fire drill**: A sudden urgent-and-important request demanding undivided attention, often dropped by higher-ups.
- **Avoid surprises**: Warning others early and proposing a plan *before* a constraint impacts them.
- **Under-promise and over-deliver**: Beat expectations, but back the signal with convincing, consistent behavior.
- **Input vs. output**: What matters is what you accomplish (output), not how hard you appear to work (input).
- **Pattern of behavior**: Once people can say "Whenever ___, [you] always ___," you've set a reputation — make it a positive one.
- **Protect what's sacred**: Defend the routines (sleep, exercise, family time — your "emotional immune system") that keep you delivering.

## Mental Models
- Think of any deliverable as a **cheap / fast / high-quality triangle** — you can usually hit two, rarely all three; articulate the trade-off instead of silently failing one.
- Use **expectations as the scoreboard**: perception depends on actual vs. *expected* performance, so a surprise turns a manageable constraint into a "problem."
- When communicating, **assume the worst**: people don't know what you know, haven't read what you sent, aren't fully paying attention, won't remember, and lack your time — so lead with context or your main point and limit to three supporting points.
- Treat office housework that "boomerangs" back to you as a negotiation: redirect it with an alternative ("This could be a great opportunity for Caleb...") rather than a flat "no."

## Anti-patterns
- **Letting a constraint become a surprise**: Failing to warn early and propose options forces a last-minute scramble.
- **Monitoring or patching instead of fixing the root cause**: The pattern returns and you waste time chasing symptoms.
- **Always volunteering for / absorbing office housework**: Especially costly for women and people of color, who are asked more, credited less, and penalized more for declining.
- **Vague communication**: "Thoughts?" invites useless feedback; assuming people read and remember leads to dropped balls.
- **Making the same mistake twice**: Recovery is fine; repetition signals you didn't learn — own up, explain, and change.

## Worked Example
Crystel, a field organizer on a political campaign, was responsible for nearly everything — volunteers, voter data, even policy advice — all of it urgent and important. She had spent the week preparing door-knocking maps and had ordered tablets to replace printed copies. While picking up takeout, she got a call: her boss couldn't find the tablets, and 50 volunteers were arriving to canvass. Checking her email, she found the shipping had been delayed until the next evening — she'd been too buried in voter data to monitor it. She rushed back as volunteers piled up; because everyone had assumed tablets, no maps were printed. For the next half hour she and her boss scrambled to export maps, find clipboards, and run to a print shop — keeping 50 volunteers waiting. The author's diagnosis: the real failure wasn't the late delivery; it was that Crystel *didn't warn her boss as soon as she knew and propose a plan*. Had she messaged early — "The delivery's delayed; three options: (1) reorder elsewhere for on-time delivery and return the late shipment, (2) order locally and drive to pick up, (3) revert to pen and paper; option 2 costs ~$300 more, I think we try option 1 first — what do you think?" — her manager would have picked an option and both moved on. By letting her manager be surprised, she let a constraint become a problem.

## Key Takeaways
1. Urgency = proximity + scrutiny + anxiety + time sensitivity; importance = centrality + scrutiny + criticality.
2. Triage with the Eisenhower Matrix: do, schedule, timebox/delegate, or delete.
3. The matrix reflects *others'* priorities — watch the tension with your own, and guard against an unfair load of nonpromotable "office housework."
4. Avoid surprises: surface constraints early with options before they impact others.
5. Fix root causes, not symptoms; preempt the recurring patterns of the people around you.
6. Assume people didn't read, hear, or remember you — communicate deliberately and pick the right medium.
7. What matters with mistakes is the *kind* — own up, recover gracefully, and never repeat the same one.
