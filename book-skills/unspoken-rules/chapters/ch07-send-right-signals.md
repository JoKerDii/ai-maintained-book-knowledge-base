# Chapter 7: Send the Right Signals

## Core Idea
Everything you do and don't do shapes others' perceptions of your competence, commitment, and compatibility (the Three Cs). Since you control only your intent but not your impact, managing misunderstandings means deliberately sending signals that show the right amount of urgency and seriousness for your audience.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Intent vs. Impact**: Intent is what you *mean*; impact is how you *come across* to others. You know your intent; others only infer it from your signals.
  - When to use: Any time you communicate or are observed, especially remotely where signals are the only impression people get.
  - How: Before acting, ask how a behavior will be *read* by others, not just what you mean by it; close gaps proactively by clarifying intent.
  - Why it works: Misunderstandings arise because others can't see your reasons (e.g., your manager's last-minute changes); controlling impact prevents unfair doubt about all Three Cs.

- **Email/IM Formality Spectrum**: Writing at work is like dressing for work, ranging across business formal → business casual → smart casual → casual.
  - When to use: When choosing tone for any written message; match your team's "email-first" vs "IM-first" culture.
  - How: Email-first workplaces default to proper emails (dress down occasionally); IM-first default to casual IMs (dress up occasionally). Tone down by changing greeting ("Dear" → "Hi" → "Hey") and sign-off ("Best" → "Thanks" → "Cheers"), then adding exclamation marks, emojis, GIFs.

- **Five Questions Before Send**: Pre-send checklist for emails/IMs.
  - When to use: Before clicking Send on any written message.
  - How: (1) Is it free of typos/formatting inconsistencies? (2) Am I fully caught up on the thread? (3) Am I sending at an appropriate time? (4) Does the tone align with my intent? (5) Would I mind if this were forwarded to the entire team?

- **Monochronic vs. Polychronic Analysis**: A lens for diagnosing how a culture treats time so you can calibrate urgency.
  - When to use: When deciding which signals to prioritize, especially across cultures or teams.
  - How: Monochrons do one thing at a time, treat deadlines as orders, prize getting the job done, are always prompt. Polychrons multitask, treat deadlines as suggestions, prize relationships, change plans freely. As a polychron among monochrons, be more prompt/structured; as a monochron among polychrons, tone down intensity and mirror their urgency.

## Key Concepts
- **Three Cs**: Competence, commitment, and compatibility — the qualities every signal is judged against.
- **Email-first workplace**: Traditional setting where proper emails are the default and IM is for quick casual chats.
- **IM-first workplace**: Startup/service/trades setting where casual IMing is the default and formal email is for outside or official communication.
- **CYA (cover your ass)**: A real workplace term — for sensitive content, use a phone call or written record to protect yourself, since emails/IMs are permanent and forwardable.
- **Call to action / clarity**: Get to the point, enunciate, eliminate background noise; in voice mails follow a standard script.
- **Digital signals**: Social posts, IM status, public calendar, file version numbers/timestamps, read receipts, forwarded threads — all of which others use to judge you.
- **Monochron / Polychron**: A person who sees time as blocks to organize vs. a person who sees time as fluid.

## Mental Models
- Think of a phone call as *dancing with a partner* — graceful when smooth, painful when both step on each other.
- Use "What signal is this sending?" as a scan for everything visible/audible on a video call — background, upper-body appearance, actions, shared screen, prep.
- Treat earned authority as the right to *strategically break* rules: know the rules first, then bend them when stakes are low.
- Your online activity is *an extension of you* — before posting, ask "Will I regret this signal days, weeks, or years from now?"

## Anti-patterns
- **Visibly losing your cool over small issues**: Reads as a lack of awareness and raises doubt about how you'd behave under real stress.
- **Sending at odd times without explanation**: A midnight email or week-late reply invites the inference that your work is out of control or you don't care.
- **Letting tone leak emotion**: Anger or passive-aggressiveness slips into writing under pressure — hit Save, not Send, and revisit later.
- **Assuming a universal standard**: There is no objective "urgent" or "serious"; judging by your own definition instead of the audience's creates mismatched signals.

## Worked Example
Neel, a management consultant, had a polished analysis to present to his manager, a director, and the client's CEO at 2:30 p.m. His manager's last-minute slide comments left him scrambling; he then couldn't find the meeting link, a software update forced a restart, and he finally joined at 2:42. When asked to present, his slideshow crashed — he grunted, banged the keyboard, and (forgetting he was unmuted) was heard by the partner. The clients were impressed by his analysis. Yet six months later his evaluation read: "Neel was easily and visibly frustrated by small issues... raises the concern of the behavior he would show if placed in more stressful situations." His *intent* was positive (deliver great work), but his *impact* was negative: late arrival (doubted commitment), losing his cool (doubted competence), unprofessional in front of clients (doubted compatibility). The root cause — his manager's last-minute changes — was invisible to everyone else. Lesson: positive intent does not protect you; you must manage impact.

## Key Takeaways
1. You control intent, not impact — others infer everything, so manage how you come across.
2. Every signal feeds the Three Cs; remote work makes digital signals your *only* impression.
3. Match formality to your team's email-first vs. IM-first culture, like dressing for work.
4. Run the Five Questions before sending; assume anything could be forwarded.
5. On calls and video, scan every audible/visible detail for the signal it sends.
6. There's no universal standard of "urgent" or "serious" — calibrate to your audience's monochronic/polychronic style and personal circumstances.
7. When you violate a rule, acknowledge it and offer a reasonable explanation rather than leaving minds to wander.
