# Glossary

**Act Confident / Act as if** — William James's principle that regulating action (standing tall, speaking boldly) regulates feeling; act brave and courage replaces fear (Ch 2, Ch 9).

**Act in Earnest** — behaving energetically and enthusiastically so that acting earnest makes you feel earnest (Ch 8).

**Attention step** — the arresting opening phase of a talk that "tunes in" the audience from the first words (Ch 5).

**Audience-centered** — orienting a talk around the listeners' minds and hearts rather than the speaker's self (Ch 3, Ch 10).

**Audience participation** — techniques (show of hands, votes, demonstrations, using a volunteer) that make listeners "a partner in the enterprise" (Ch 5, Ch 10).

**Audience-fright / stage fright** — the nervousness beginners feel; normal, partly useful, and reducible through practice (Ch 1, Ch 2).

**Blue pencil of inattentiveness** — the audience's silent editing of an over-detailed talk by withdrawing attention (Ch 7).

**Burning your ships** — eliminating every mental escape route so the only option is to advance (Caesar at Dover) (Ch 1).

**Certainty of reward** — the principle that persistent, faithful practice reliably produces competence (Rockefeller; William James) (Ch 6).

**Common ground of agreement** — starting an argument from what speaker and audience both believe (Lincoln) (Ch 13).

**Contagious enthusiasm** — feeling communicated so forcefully it thrusts aside opposing ideas; stirs emotion over cold thought (Ch 13).

**Earn the right to talk** — qualifying to speak on a subject through personal experience or genuine study (Ch 3, Ch 7).

**Enlarged conversation** — the modern conception of public speaking as direct talk *with* an audience, not oratory *at* them (Ch 1, Ch 9).

**Enlarged/intensified naturalness** — conversational delivery scaled up in energy for a larger room ("your natural tones of eloquence, enlarged a bit") (Ch 9).

**Enumerate** — explicitly signposting points ("my first point is...", "second...") for clarity (Ch 12).

**Example–Point–Reason** — see Magic Formula.

**Five-W formula** — the reporter's checklist (When? Where? Who? What? Why?) for packing an example with color (Ch 7, Ch 11).

**Four Contacts with the World** — we are judged by what we do, how we look, what we say, and how we say it (Ch 9).

**Four Guideposts** — Take Heart from Others' Experience; Keep Sight of Your Goal; Predetermine Your Mind to Success; Seize Every Opportunity to Practice (Ch 1).

**Four Purposes of a Talk** — to persuade/get action, to inform, to impress and convince, to entertain (Ch 11).

**Habit of success** — confidence built by accumulating a record of successful speaking experiences (Ch 1, Ch 2).

**Here and Now (Principle of)** — drawing impromptu material from the audience, the occasion, and previous speakers (Ch 14).

**Humanize, Personalize, Specify, Dramatize, Visualize** — the five ways to use illustrative material (Ch 7).

**Incubation period** — choosing a topic early so the subconscious works on it during idle moments (Ch 7).

**Keystone words** — foundation terms used repeatedly; must be defined before use (Ch 12).

**Law of Exercise / Law of Effect** — we change behavior through repeated incidents (Exercise) or a single startling event (Effect) (Ch 11).

**Linkage technique** — impromptu training where each speaker continues a story where the last stopped (Ch 14).

**Magic Formula** — the sure-fire short-talk structure: Example (incident), Point (what to do), Reason (benefit); reverses the newspaper technique (Ch 11).

**Memorization trap** — writing out and memorizing a talk, "mental dope addiction" that courts a blank mind and mechanical delivery (Ch 2).

**Pause–Part–Punch** — how to deliver a speaker's name at the end of an introduction: silence, separation of names, vigor (Ch 4).

**Picture-making words** — concrete, specific terms that set images before the eyes ("a white bantam rooster with a broken leg") (Ch 7).

**Plateaus in the curve of learning** — periods of stagnation or apparent regression that precede sudden breakthroughs (Ch 6).

**Predetermine your mind to success** — deliberately adopting an optimistic, resolute attitude before you begin (Ch 1, Ch 2).

**Race horses vs. draft horses** — sensitive speakers always feel some fear; placid "cool as a cucumber" types are uninspiring (Ch 2).

**Rapport** — the live interrelationship between speaker, speech, and audience; the key to successful speaking (Ch 10, Ch 14).

**Reserve power** — gathering far more material (10–100x) than you use, giving authority and readiness for emergencies (Ch 7).

**Reliving the experience** — recreating original emotion rather than reporting coolly in the third person (Ch 8, Ch 11).

**Shock technique** — an unexpected, arresting fact that "jars the mind" into focus (Ch 5).

**T-I-S Formula** — introduction structure: Topic, Importance, Speaker (Ch 4).

**Talks with general preparation** — "impromptu" speaking that actually rests on countless mental talks never given (Ch 14).

**Transfer value** — the spread of speaking confidence to selling, socializing, and life generally (Ch 1, Ch 2).

**Vitality/aliveness/enthusiasm** — the first essential qualities of a speaker (Ch 8).

**Yes-Response** — inducing affirmative reactions at the outset so the mind resists deflection to "no" (Overstreet) (Ch 13).
