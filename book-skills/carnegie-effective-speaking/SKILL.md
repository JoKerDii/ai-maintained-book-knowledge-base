## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The whole philosophy in one line**: public speaking is *enlarged conversation* — talk *with* people, not *at* them. There is no "born public speaker"; skill comes from the right attitude plus relentless practice.

### Building Confidence — the Four Rules (Ch 2)
Use when stage fright threatens. (1) **Get the facts about fear** — 80–100% of beginners feel it; a limited amount helps you think and speak faster. (2) **Prepare in the proper way** — assemble and arrange ideas; **never memorize word-for-word** (the mind goes "not just blank but black"). (3) **Predetermine your mind to success** — lose yourself in the subject, keep attention off negatives, give yourself a pep talk. (4) **Act confident** — regulate action to regulate feeling (William James); breathe deeply, stand tall, meet eyes. Rule of thumb: **"Only the prepared speaker deserves to be confident."**

### The Three Cardinal Rules — quick & easy speaking (Ch 3)
The foundation of everything. (1) **Speak about something you have earned the right to talk about** — through experience or study, not book-lifted generalizations. (2) **Be excited about your subject** — test: if someone opposed you and you'd leap to defend it, it's the right topic. (3) **Be eager to share it with your listeners** — be audience-centered; success is decided "in the minds and hearts of your hearers."

### Earning the Right to Talk — attention-compelling material (Ch 7)
Prefer concrete detail over abstract logic. **Limit your subject** (short talk = 1–2 points; 30-min talk = 4–5 max). Build **reserve power** (gather 10–100x more than you use). Fill with illustration via **Humanize, Personalize, Specify (5-W: When/Where/Who/What/Why), Dramatize (dialogue), Visualize (demonstrate)**. Use **picture-making words** ("a white bantam rooster with a broken leg," not "fowl").

### Vitalizing the Talk — the Three Ways (Ch 8)
Vitality/enthusiasm is the first quality of a speaker. (1) **Choose subjects you are earnest about.** (2) **Relive the feelings** you have about your topic (don't report coolly). (3) **Act in earnest** — acting earnest makes you feel earnest.

### Delivering the Talk — Five Keys + intensified naturalness (Ch 9)
We're judged by four contacts: what we do, how we look, what we say, **how we say it**. (1) **Crash through self-consciousness.** (2) **Be yourself — don't imitate.** (3) **Converse with the audience** (pick one person). (4) **Put your heart into it.** (5) **Practice voice/pace only away from the audience.** Deliver with "your natural tones of eloquence, enlarged a bit."

### Sharing the Talk — Five Ways to build rapport (Ch 10)
(1) **Talk in terms of listeners' interests** ("nothing so interesting to ourselves as ourselves"). (2) **Give sincere, specific appreciation** (never hollow flattery). (3) **Identify with the audience** (names, "you"/"we"). (4) **Make them partners** (participation). (5) **Play yourself down** (humility wins; the show-off is resented).

### The Four Purposes of a Talk (Ch 11)
Analyze audience and occasion, then pick ONE: **get action · inform · impress/convince · entertain.** Mismatching purpose to occasion is fatal (the congressman hooted off stage for informing at a rally).

### The Magic Formula — short talk to get action (Ch 11)
**Example → Point → Reason** (Incident–Point–Benefit). Lead with a vivid personal **incident** (~75% of time); state the **point** — specific, overt, easy action (~20 sec); give **one relevant benefit**. Reverses the newspaper technique to build suspense. Never open with the demand.

### The Talk to Inform — five rules (Ch 12)
**Restrict** the subject · **sequence** ideas (time/space/topical) · **enumerate** points · **compare strange with familiar** (turn facts into pictures) · **use visual aids** (eye beats ear ~25:1); define keystone words before using them.

### The Talk to Convince — five rules (Ch 13)
Plant your idea while keeping opposing ideas from arising. **Win confidence by deserving it** (character) · **get yes-responses** from common ground · **contagious enthusiasm** (stir emotion over cold ideas) · **respect/affection** for the audience · **begin friendly** — liken your proposal to what they already believe (Lincoln, Paul on Mars Hill). Frontal attack "always ends in a draw."

### Impromptu Talks — six rules (Ch 14)
**Practice** (slip game, linkage story) · **be mentally ready** ("what would I say now?") · **get into an example immediately** · **animation and force** · **here-and-now** (audience/occasion/prior speakers) · **give a real talk** grouped around one central point. Don't apologize; don't ramble.

### Ways to Open & Close (Ch 5)
**Open** with: incident · suspense · arresting fact · show-of-hands · self-interest promise · exhibit. Avoid apology, "funny" story, and stunt openings. **Close** by summarizing, then asking for specific, easy, achievable action — planned in advance, never trailing off.
