# Chapter 2: Develop Confidence

## Core Idea
Stage fright is normal, useful, and controllable; confidence comes not from eliminating fear but from proper preparation, a determined mind, and physically acting as if you were already brave.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Rules for Developing Confidence**: Carnegie's method for conquering stage fright in weeks.
  - When to use: any time nerves threaten to undermine a talk.
  - How: (1) **Get the Facts About Fear** — 80–100% of beginners feel it; a limited amount actually helps you think and speak faster. (2) **Prepare in the Proper Way** — assemble and arrange your ideas; never memorize. (3) **Predetermine Your Mind to Success** — lose yourself in the subject, keep attention off negative stimuli, give yourself a pep talk. (4) **Act Confident** — regulate action to regulate feeling; behave brave until you become brave.
- **The Proper Preparation Method**: (a) search your background for significant experiences; (b) brood over the topic until it becomes "mellow and expansive"; (c) jot ideas on scraps of paper; (d) arrange them; (e) rehearse by "talking it out" in ordinary conversation with friends.

## Key Concepts
- **Only the prepared speaker deserves to be confident**: the one lesson standing "like Mount Everest" above all others.
- **Race horses vs. draft horses**: sensitive speakers always feel some fear; the placid "cool as a cucumber" type is about as inspiring as a cucumber.
- **Memorization trap**: writing out and memorizing a talk is "mental dope addiction" that courts a blank mind and mechanical delivery.
- **Talking it out**: rehearsing by using your ideas casually in real conversations, watching listeners' reactions.
- **Auto-suggestion / pep talk**: telling yourself, in clear terms, that this is the right talk and you are the most qualified person present to give it.
- **Act as if**: William James's principle — feeling follows action; act brave and courage replaces fear.

## Mental Models
- Think of stage fright as nature arming you: quicker thinking, more fluent speech, greater intensity — if kept within limits.
- Prepare by thinking in *ideas*, not *words* — "if our ideas are clear, the words come as naturally as the air we breathe."
- Use James's loop: you can't will a feeling, but you can will an action; regulate the action to indirectly regulate the feeling.
- Before speaking: breathe deeply for 30 seconds, stand to full height, look the audience in the eye "as if every one of them owed you money."

## Anti-patterns
- **Memorizing word for word**: Churchill, Kaltenborn, and Vance Bushnell all blanked mid-talk after memorizing — the mind goes not just blank but "black."
- **Total lack of preparation**: the Rotary official who fumbled unordered notes "began without knowing what he was going to say, and finished without knowing what he had said."
- **Dwelling on negative projections**: imagining grammar errors or a mid-talk blank cancels confidence before you start.

## Worked Example
Vance Bushnell, a rising Equitable Life vice-president, was given a 20-minute talk to 2,000 representatives. He wrote it out, memorized it, and rehearsed 40 times before a mirror — every phrase and gesture "flawless." On stage he said "My part in this program is..." and his mind went blank. He stepped back to restart, blanked again, stepped back again — and on the fourth step toppled off the four-foot platform into the gap behind it. The audience roared, thinking it was an act. Humiliated, he wrote a resignation. His superiors tore it up; he went on to become one of the firm's finest speakers — but never memorized a talk again.

## Key Takeaways
1. Expect fear; a controlled amount improves performance — don't fight to eliminate it.
2. Build "a record of successful experiences" — the habit of success dissolves fear (Albert Wiggam).
3. Prepare by brooding over your own experience, jotting notes, and talking it out — never memorizing.
4. Lose yourself in your subject; sell yourself on its importance to the audience.
5. Just before speaking, put attention on other speakers, not on yourself.
6. Breathe deeply, stand tall, meet eyes, and act confident — action manufactures the feeling (Teddy Roosevelt trained himself to fearlessness this way).
