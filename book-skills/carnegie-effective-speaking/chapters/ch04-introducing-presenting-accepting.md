# Chapter 4: Introducing Speakers, Presenting and Accepting Awards

## Core Idea
Short ceremonial talks — introductions, presentations, and acceptances — are far more important than people assume; each demands preparation, sincerity, brevity, and a simple time-tested formula.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The T-I-S Formula (speech of introduction)**: organizes the facts you've gathered about a speaker.
  - When to use: introducing any speaker to an audience.
  - How: **T** = Topic (give the exact title of the talk); **I** = Importance (bridge the topic to this group's interests); **S** = Speaker (list qualifications relevant to the topic, then give the name distinctly). Keep it under ~1 minute.
- **The Presentation Formula (giving an award)**: (1) Tell why the award is made; (2) Tell the group's interest in the recipient's life/activities; (3) Tell how deserved it is and how warmly the group feels; (4) Congratulate and convey good wishes.
- **The Acceptance Formula (receiving an award)**: (1) Warmly sincere thank-you to the group; (2) Give credit to others who helped; (3) Tell what the gift/award means to you (open and display it if wrapped); (4) End with another sincere expression of gratitude. Even shorter than the presentation.

## Key Concepts
- **Introduction = "lead to the inside"** (Latin *intro* + *ducere*): lead the audience inside the topic and inside the speaker's fitness to discuss it.
- **Sell the topic and sell the speaker**: the two jobs of an introduction, done in the briefest time.
- **Be specific**: only specificity heightens attention and makes the audience receptive.
- **Pause–Part–Punch**: how to deliver the speaker's name at the end.
- **Yearning for recognition**: "the deepest yearning of the human heart is for recognition — for honor."

## Mental Models
- When naming the speaker: **Pause** (silence sharpens anticipation), **Part** (separate first and last name so it lands clearly), **Punch** (say the name with vigor).
- Look out over the audience until the last syllable of the name — then turn to the speaker (never announce the name to the speaker alone).
- Build the introduction to a climax at the name, so applause carries the speaker onto the platform.
- In presentation/acceptance, stress friendly sentiment over the gift's intrinsic value.

## Anti-patterns
- **"Inspiration" over preparation**: the introducer who "waited for inspiration" listed the three better speakers who couldn't come, then named John Mason Brown to dead silence.
- **Vague, canned introductions**: "recognized everywhere as an authority... let's see now, oh, here it is, Mr. Blank."
- **Omitting the speaker's greatest achievement**: introducing W. B. Yeats without mentioning his Nobel Prize.
- **Over-praise / under-praise**: promising the audience they'll be "rolling in the aisles" ruins a humorist; labeling a speaker "cheaper talent" humiliates him.
- **Memorizing the introduction / mangling the name**: leads to blanks and gaffes ("John Brown Mason," "John Smith Mason").
- **Exaggeration in acceptance**: "greatest day of my life," "most wonderful thing that ever happened" — takes in too much territory.

## Worked Example
Editor Homer Thorne introduced telephone executive George Wellbaum with the topic "The Telephone Serves You." He opened with the *Importance* step by teasing the audience's curiosity — "Why do you get a wrong number? Why can you sometimes call New York to Chicago quicker than the next town over?" — then established the *Speaker's* fitness: "For twenty years it has been his job to digest all sorts of details about the telephone business and make it clear to other people... a telephone company executive who has earned his title by work." Finally he named him with vigor. The introduction followed T-I-S perfectly yet sounded spontaneous and conversational, never like a formula.

## Key Takeaways
1. Never treat the introduction as trivial — it "sells" both topic and speaker in under a minute.
2. Gather three facts: the talk's title, the speaker's relevant qualifications, and the correct name/pronunciation.
3. Follow T-I-S but keep it conversational — never memorize, never predict what the speaker will say.
4. Be specific, enthusiastic, and warmly sincere; avoid clichés, canned jokes, and snide humor.
5. Deliver the name with Pause–Part–Punch, facing the audience until the final syllable.
6. For presentations and acceptances, be brief, sincere, credit others, and avoid all exaggeration.
