# Chapter 6: Apply What You Have Learned

## Core Idea
Start applying these techniques immediately in everyday speech, persist through the inevitable plateaus, and reward is ultimately certain — the ordinary person with grit ends up among the competent speakers of the community.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Five Application Rules**: how to turn book knowledge into permanent skill.
  - When to use: from the very next conversation onward.
  - How: (1) **Use Specific Detail in Everyday Conversation** — colorful, dramatic details make you a compelling conversationalist. (2) **Use Effective Speaking Techniques in Your Job** — clear, concise instruction is the yardstick superiors use to judge competence. (3) **Seek Opportunities to Speak in Public** — join clubs, do committee work, become program chairman, develop a 20–30 minute talk. (4) **You Must Persist** — push through the plateaus. (5) **Keep the Certainty of Reward Before You** — believe firmly in success and act to bring it about.

## Key Concepts
- **Plateaus in the curve of learning**: periods of stagnation or apparent regression that all learners hit; the weak give up, those with grit break through "almost overnight."
- **Learning by waves**: progress comes in abrupt starts and sudden stops, not steady improvement.
- **Initial fear**: the fleeting nervousness before speaking (Paderewski fidgeted with his cuffs) that vanishes "like a mist in August sunshine" after the first sentences.
- **Certainty of reward**: John D. Rockefeller Sr.'s "first essential for success" — patience plus knowledge that reward is ultimately certain.
- **Belief as prerequisite**: Farragut to Dupont — the unspoken reason for failure was "You did not believe you could do it."
- **Expressiveness as leadership**: clear, forceful expression is one of the marks of leadership in society.

## Mental Models
- Use William James: keep "faithfully busy each hour," and you can "with perfect certainty" count on waking one morning among the competent ones of your generation.
- When discouraged, use Roosevelt's plan: picture Lincoln in your place and ask what he would do.
- Treat everyday speech as practice — "everything I did became grist for the conversational mill" (Mrs. Hart).
- "Never undertake anything thinking defeat" — the proper psychology for speaking or scaling Everest.

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting to apply the techniques**: the answer to "when do I start?" is "Immediately" — the very next speaking situation.
- **Giving up on a plateau**: mistaking a normal stagnation phase for a ceiling; "the weak ones give up in despair."
- **Being a passive club member**: joining but staying "a mere looker-on" forfeits the practice.

## Worked Example
Carnegie illustrates the certainty of reward with Abraham Lincoln. Lincoln had less than one year's total schooling, borrowed every book within fifty miles, and read by firelight, keeping a book wedged in the cabin's log cracks to grab at first light. He walked twenty to thirty miles to hear speakers, then practiced everywhere — in fields, woods, before crowds at Jones' grocery — and joined debating societies in New Salem and Springfield. Though shy and tongue-tied around women, by faithful practice and home study he became the man who debated Douglas and reached the heights of Gettysburg and his second inaugural. His advice: "Always bear in mind that your own resolution to succeed is more important than any other one thing."

## Key Takeaways
1. Apply the principles immediately, starting with everyday conversation and your job.
2. Fill your speech with specific, picturesque detail — it's what makes conversation interesting.
3. Actively seek public-speaking opportunities: committees, program chair, speaker's bureaus.
4. Expect plateaus and push through them; grit beats brilliance.
5. Believe firmly in success — belief drives the actions that produce it.
6. Reward is ultimately certain for the persistent; expressiveness becomes leadership.
