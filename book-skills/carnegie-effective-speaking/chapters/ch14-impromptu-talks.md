# Chapter 14: Impromptu Talks

## Core Idea
The ability to speak "off the cuff" is even more important than prepared speaking in modern life; it is learnable through practice, mental readiness, leading with an example, animation, and drawing on the here-and-now.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Six Rules for Impromptu Speaking**: making an acceptable, even brilliant, talk on the spur of the moment.
  - When to use: whenever you may be called on without warning — meetings, conferences, banquets.
  - How: (1) **Practice Impromptu Speaking** — rehearse thinking on your feet. (2) **Be Mentally Ready to Speak Impromptu** — condition yourself in advance. (3) **Get into an Example Immediately** — start with an incident. (4) **Speak with Animation and Force** — physical energy fires the mind. (5) **Use the Principle of the Here and Now** — draw on audience, occasion, and prior speakers. (6) **Don't Talk Impromptu — Give an Impromptu Talk** — keep ideas grouped around a central point.
- **The Three Here-and-Now Sources**: (1) the audience — who they are and the good they do; (2) the occasion — the circumstances that brought the meeting about; (3) prior speakers — amplify something one of them said.

## Key Concepts
- **Talks with general preparation**: "impromptu" speaking really rests on countless talks made mentally but never given (like a pilot rehearsing emergencies).
- **The linkage technique**: a training game where each person continues a fantastic story where the last left off — builds fluency without preparation.
- **The sixty-second slip game**: draw a random topic and speak on it for a minute (Fairbanks, Chaplin, Pickford) — practice in "thinking on one's feet."
- **Body charges the mind** (William James): "grasp an idea," "clutch at a thought" — physical animation soon gets the mind functioning fast.
- **Genuinely impromptu = tailor-made**: the best off-the-cuff talks flower out of the moment, fitting the situation "like hand in glove."

## Mental Models
- Condition yourself at every meeting by asking, "What would I say if I were called on right now?"
- Lead with an example: it frees you from thinking hard about the next sentence, dissolves first-moment jitters, and captures attention instantly.
- Don't apologize for being unprepared — it's expected; launch into the topic at once.
- Say what you have to say briefly, then sit down; keep it grouped around one central thought.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rejecting the whole skill**: the brilliant research director who "can talk to my feet, not to an audience" — his defeatist attitude was unnecessary.
- **Apologizing for being unprepared**: wastes the audience's goodwill and the moment.
- **Making off "in all directions"** (Leacock's befuddled horseman): panicking and rambling instead of staying calm and close to the meeting.
- **Stringing together "disconnected nothings"**: talking impromptu without a central point is not giving an impromptu talk.

## Worked Example
Douglas Fairbanks described a nightly game he, Charlie Chaplin, and Mary Pickford played for two years. Each wrote a subject on a slip of paper; the slips were folded, shaken, and drawn; whoever drew had to stand and talk for sixty seconds on that subject — never repeating a topic. One night Fairbanks drew "lampshades" ("Just try it if you think it is easy. I got through somehow."). The point wasn't the individual talks but the cumulative effect: all three "sharpened up," learned to "assemble our knowledge and thoughts on any topic at a moment's notice" — learning to think on their feet. Carnegie uses the same principle in class: members get surprise topics (an accountant on advertising, a banker on schoolteaching) and discover they can do far better than they believed.

## Key Takeaways
1. Impromptu skill is learnable — practice with games like the sixty-second slip and the linkage story.
2. Prepare mentally: at meetings, constantly rehearse what you'd say if called on.
3. Start with an example — it kills jitters, buys thinking time, and grabs attention.
4. Speak with animation and force; physical energy accelerates the mind.
5. Draw on the here-and-now: audience, occasion, and previous speakers.
6. Give a real talk, not rambling — group ideas around one central point, then sit down.
