# Chapter 11: The Short Talk to Get Action

## Core Idea
Every talk serves one of four purposes; for the short talk to get action, the Magic Formula — Example, Point, Reason (Incident–Point–Benefit) — is a psychologically-designed structure that reliably moves an audience to act.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Purposes of a Talk**: (1) to persuade / get action; (2) to inform; (3) to impress and convince; (4) to entertain. Match your purpose to the audience and occasion.
  - When to use: at the outset of any talk — analyze audience and occasion, then choose one purpose.
- **The Magic Formula (Example — Point — Reason)**: the sure-fire structure for a two-to-three-minute action talk.
  - When to use: any short talk (or letter, or request) aimed at getting someone to do something.
  - How: (1) **Example** — open directly with a vivid incident from your life that taught the lesson (takes ~75% of the time). (2) **Point** — state, briefly and specifically, exactly what you want the audience to do (~20 seconds). (3) **Reason** — highlight the one benefit they gain by acting.

## Key Concepts
- **Magic Formula reverses the newspaper technique**: give the news story first, then "headline" it with your point — this builds suspense so the audience isn't hit with a demand up front.
- **Law of Exercise vs. Law of Effect**: we change behavior either through repeated similar incidents (Exercise) or through a single startling event (Effect) — the action talk exploits the latter.
- **Single personal experience**: the most powerful example is one dramatic event that changed you; "if it happened to you, it can happen to them."
- **Relevant detail only**: select details that emphasize the point; irrelevant detail (scenery, lodging) dissipates attention.
- **Reliving the experience**: dramatize with the "fervor of re-creation" so the talk sticks in memory.
- **Overt vs. mental action**: ask for something visible ("visit your grandparents this weekend"), not vague ("think of your grandparents").

## Mental Models
- Open right inside the incident: answer Who/When/Where/What/How/Why in the first sentence ("Last July I was driving at a fast clip down Highway 42...") — "Once upon a time" opens the floodgates.
- Write the Point like a telegram: fewest words, clear, explicit, and easy to do ("Repeat the next stranger's name five times within five minutes," not "improve your memory").
- Stress ONE reason only, and make it relevant to the example — like a magazine ad, which never sells two ideas or gives two reasons at once.
- State the Point with force and conviction — it's your last impression; no diffidence in asking for the order.

## Anti-patterns
- **Mismatching purpose to occasion**: the congressman who gave an informative talk at a political rally was hooted off the New York Hippodrome stage.
- **Vapid, apologetic openings**: "I didn't have time to prepare," "Unaccustomed as I am to public speaking" — audiences want action from the opening word.
- **Vague or general points**: "Help the patients at the orphanage" instead of "Sign up tonight to take twenty-five children on a picnic next Sunday."
- **Cluttering the example with irrelevant detail**: too many unrelated details make a talk "a boring test of endurance."
- **Giving multiple reasons**: dilutes the appeal; choose one outstanding benefit and rest your case on it.

## Worked Example
Leland Stowe predisposed his audience to support the UN Appeal for Children not by asking for money up front (which would trigger "a scramble for the exits") but by leading with a searing Example: in bomb-scarred Athens, with only a half-pound can of peanuts, he was mobbed by dozens of ragged, starving children — "begging hands, clutching hands, despairing hands; all of them pitifully little hands" — dropping peanuts one at a time as emaciated bodies scrambled at his feet, until he stood helpless with an empty blue can. Only after that vivid incident does the appeal for contributions land. Carnegie contrasts this with the failed opening "Ladies and gentlemen, I'm here to collect five dollars from each of you." The Example prepares the way for the desired action.

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify your talk's purpose and fit it to the audience and occasion before anything else.
2. For action talks, use the Magic Formula: Example (75%), Point, Reason.
3. Build the Example on one vivid personal experience and open right inside it.
4. Include only relevant detail; relive the experience with dramatic fervor.
5. State the Point briefly, specifically, as an easy overt action, with force.
6. Give one relevant benefit and stop — like an ad selling a single idea.
