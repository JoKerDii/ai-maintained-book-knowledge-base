# Chapter 5: Enemies, Allies & Whom to Trust

**Covers Laws 2, 10, 18, 19, 20, 42.** The relationship-risk laws: who you associate with, trust, commit to, and cut off determines your exposure. Greene's counterintuitive claim runs through the chapter — friends are a bigger liability than enemies, and independence beats loyalty.

## Core Idea
Choose your associations by strategic risk, not sentiment. Distrust friends (they breed envy), make use of enemies (they must prove themselves), never fully isolate, read each person before acting, refuse to commit to any side, and neutralize troublemakers at the source.

## The Laws (essence · key tactic · reversal)

- **Law 2 — Never put too much trust in friends; learn how to use enemies.** Friends betray fastest because they're easily roused to envy and grow entitled; a former enemy hired into your service has more to prove and often proves more loyal. *Tactic:* keep friendship and business separate; convert able enemies into allies who owe you. *Reversal:* a true, tested friend is invaluable — the warning is against *reflexive* trust and mixing friendship with power.
- **Law 10 — Infection: avoid the unhappy and unlucky.** Emotional states and misfortune are contagious; chronic losers and malcontents drag you into their disasters. *Tactic:* associate with the happy and fortunate; cut off the perpetually miserable before their gravity pulls you down. *Reversal:* none endorsed — though genuine, temporary hardship in a strong person is distinct from chronic self-made misfortune.
- **Law 18 — Do not build fortresses to protect yourself; isolation is dangerous.** Cutting yourself off feels safe but blinds you to information and makes you a conspicuous target. *Tactic:* circulate, mingle, gather allies — the crowd is your best shield. *Reversal:* brief, chosen withdrawal (Law 16) differs from defensive isolation; retreat tactically, never wall yourself off permanently.
- **Law 19 — Know who you're dealing with; do not offend the wrong person.** People react differently; some, deceived, will pursue vengeance for life. *Tactic:* profile your target before you move — spot the thin-skinned "wolf in lamb's clothing" and never provoke them. *Reversal:* none — misreading a mark is presented as pure downside.
- **Law 20 — Do not commit to anyone.** Rushing to take sides makes you a tool; independence makes you the courted prize. *Tactic:* stay above the fray, keep others guessing and competing for you, play sides against each other. *Reversal:* you may temporarily commit to *one* side to defeat a common enemy — but keep your ultimate independence.
- **Law 42 — Strike the shepherd and the sheep will scatter.** Trouble usually traces to one strong instigator; groups collapse when the ringleader is removed. *Tactic:* identify the single stirrer/poisoner, then isolate or banish them — don't negotiate, don't wait for the trouble to spread. *Reversal:* be sure you've found the *true* source; striking the wrong "shepherd" creates a martyr and leaves the real one operating.

## Mental Models
- **Friends-as-liability / enemies-as-asset:** invert the sentimental default — envy makes intimates dangerous, while an enemy-turned-ally is motivated by the debt of the second chance (Law 2).
- **Emotional contagion:** moods and luck spread like disease; curate your circle for it (Law 10).
- **Independence as leverage:** the uncommitted player is pursued by all sides and owned by none (Law 20 ↔ Law 18's caution against *isolation*, not against *mingling*).
- **Single point of failure (offense):** organizations have a keystone person; remove them and the structure loses cohesion (Law 42).

## Worked Example
*Never trust friends; use enemies (Law 2).* Greene's recurring pattern: a ruler promotes a loyal friend into a position of power; the friend, now a peer, grows envious of what he doesn't have and resentful of the debt, and becomes the first to betray. Meanwhile a former rival, spared and elevated, works twice as hard to justify the mercy and prove his worth. **Reproduce it as a hiring/alliance heuristic:** don't hand power to someone merely because you like them — friendship curdles into entitlement when mixed with power. A capable former opponent, given a stake and something to prove, is often the safer bet. Keep affection and leverage in separate accounts.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reflexively trusting friends with power** — breeds the envy that produces betrayal (violates Law 2).
- **Keeping company with chronic malcontents** — their misfortune infects you (violates Law 10).
- **Walling yourself off** — isolation cuts your information and paints a target (violates Law 18).
- **Provoking a vengeful personality** — offending the wrong person creates a lifelong enemy (violates Law 19).
- **Committing early to a side** — turns you from courted prize into disposable tool (violates Law 20).
- **Negotiating with the ringleader** — the instigator is irredeemable; remove, don't bargain (violates Law 42).

## Key Takeaways
1. Distrust reflexive friendship; convert able enemies into motivated allies (Law 2).
2. Curate your circle — misery and bad luck are contagious (Law 10).
3. Circulate and gather allies; never wall yourself off (Law 18).
4. Read each person before you act; never offend the vengeful (Law 19).
5. Stay uncommitted so all sides court you (Law 20).
6. To break a hostile group, remove its single instigator (Law 42).
