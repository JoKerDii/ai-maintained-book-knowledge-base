# Chapter 16: Why Training Is the Boss's Job

## Core Idea
Training is **one of the highest-leverage activities a manager can perform** — twelve hours of your preparation can return two hundred hours of work — and precisely because it must be tied to how your organization actually operates and delivered by a credible role model, **you** must teach it, not a specialist.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Leverage Arithmetic of Training** — Grove's calculation, worth memorizing:
  > Four lectures × 3 hours of preparation per hour of course time = **12 hours of your work.**
  > 10 students × ~2,000 hours each next year = **20,000 hours** worked for your organization.
  > A **1% improvement** in their performance = **200 hours** gained.
  > **200 hours returned for 12 hours spent.**

- **Why the Manager Must Do It — Three Arguments**:
  1. **It's the other half of your only job.** "A manager's output is the output of his organization — no more, no less… A manager generally has two ways to raise the level of individual performance: by increasing **motivation** and by increasing **individual capability, which is where training comes in.** It is generally accepted that motivating employees is a key task of all managers, **one that can't be delegated to someone else. Why shouldn't the same be true for the other principal means** at a manager's disposal for increasing output?"
  2. **Effectiveness requires organizational fit.** "For training to be effective, it has to be **closely tied to how things are actually done in your organization**" — which rules out most "canned courses" taught by outsiders.
  3. **The teacher must be a role model.** "Training must be done by a person who represents a **suitable role model. Proxies, no matter how well versed they might be in the subject matter, cannot assume that role.** The person standing in front of the class should be seen as a **believable, practicing authority** on the subject taught."

- **Training Is a Process, Not an Event**: "training has to maintain a **reliable, consistent presence.** Employees should be able to count on something **systematic and scheduled, not a rescue effort summoned to solve the problem of the moment.**"

- **Who Teaches Whom**: "**You yourself should instruct your direct subordinates and perhaps the next few ranks below them. Your subordinates should do the same thing, and the supervisors at every level below them as well.**"
  - At Intel: **2–4% of employees' time is spent in classroom learning**, much of it taught by the company's own managerial staff, and "conducting training is a worthwhile activity for everyone from the first-line supervisor to the chief executive officer."
  - The catalogue lists **over fifty classes**, ranging "from proper telephone manners to quite complicated production courses — like one on how to operate the ion implanter, which requires nearly **two hundred hours of on-the-job training** to learn to use correctly, **almost five times the hours of training needed to get a private pilot's license.**" Managers are trained in strategic planning and in **constructive confrontation**, "a problem-solving approach we favor at Intel."
  - **Grove's own teaching repertoire**: preparing and delivering performance reviews; conducting productive meetings; and a three-hour introduction to Intel covering history, objectives, organization, and management practices — given "to a sizable proportion of our professional employees." He also pinch-hits in other management courses. **"(To my regret, I have become far too obsolete to teach technical material.)"**

- **The Two Training Tasks, and Why the Distinction Matters**:
  | | **New-employee training** | **New-skill training** |
  |---|---|---|
  | Teaches | New members the skills needed to perform their jobs | New ideas, principles, or skills to **present** members |
  | Sizing | Set by the number of new people. A department with **10% annual turnover and 10% growth must teach 20% of its staff** the basics each year | **Five times larger** if you want to train all staff within a year |
  | Grove's cost check | — | A new **one-day** course for Intel's middle-management staff: **"the cost of the students' time alone was over one million dollars."** |
  - **"Obviously such a task should not be entered into lightly."**

- **The Seven-Step Startup Procedure** — how to actually begin:
  1. **List what your people should be trained in.** "Don't limit the scope of your list. Items should range from what seems simple (**training the person who takes calls at the restaurant**) to loftier and more general things like **the objectives and value systems of your department, your plant, and your company.**"
  2. **Ask the people working for you what they feel they need.** "They are likely to surprise you by telling you of needs you never knew existed."
  3. **Inventory the manager-teachers and instructional materials available.**
  4. **Assign priorities among the items.**
  5. **Start very unambitiously** — "like developing one short course (three to four lectures) on the most urgent subject."
  6. **Set a schedule with deadlines and commit to it.** "Create an outline for the whole course, develop just the **first** lecture, and **go.** Develop the second lecture after you have given the first."
  7. **Treat the first delivery as a throwaway.** "It won't be great, because no matter how hard you try, you'll have to go through one version that won't be. **Rather than agonize over it, accept the inevitability of the first time being unsatisfactory and consider it the path to a more satisfactory second round.**"
     - **How to make the throwaway harmless**: "teach this course to the **more knowledgeable of your subordinates**, who won't be confused by it but who will **help you perfect the course through interaction and critique.**"

- **The Scaling Question**: before the second attempt, ask — "Will you be able to teach all members of your organization yourself? Will you be able to cover everybody in one or two courses, or will it require ten or twenty? **If your organization is large enough to require many repetitions… then set yourself up to train a few instructors with your first set of lectures.**"

- **Getting Feedback on Your Teaching**: "ask for **anonymous** critiques from the employees in your class. Prompt them with a form that asks for **numerical ratings** but that also poses some **open-ended questions.**"
  - **Calibrate your expectations**: "understand that **you will never be able to please all members of your class: typical feedback will be that the course was too detailed, too superficial, and just right, in about equal balance.** Your ultimate aim should be to **satisfy yourself that you are accomplishing what you set out to do.**"

- **The Three Discoveries of a First-Time Teacher**:
  1. **Training is hard work.** "Even if you have been doing your job for a long time and even if you have done your subordinates' jobs in great detail before, **you'll be amazed at how much you don't know.** Don't be discouraged — this is typical. **Much deeper knowledge of a task is required to teach that task than simply to do it.** If you don't believe me, **try explaining to someone over the phone how to drive a stick-shift car.**"
  2. **You will learn the most.** "**Guess who will have learned most from the course? You.** The crispness that developing it gave to your understanding of your own work is likely in itself to have made the effort extremely worthwhile."
  3. **It is exhilarating.** "When the training process goes well, it is nothing short of exhilarating. And even this exhilaration is **dwarfed by the warm feeling you'll get when you see a subordinate practice something you have taught him.** Relish the exhilaration and warmth — it'll help you to arm yourself for tackling the second course."

## Key Concepts
- **Training as capability, motivation as desire** — the two halves of eliciting performance (Ch 11's diagram).
- **Canned course** — outside-taught material misaligned with how your organization works; Grove's named failure mode.
- **Process, not event** — training scheduled and systematic rather than summoned in a crisis.
- **The throwaway first delivery** — a deliberate design choice, not an accident.
- **20% baseline** — turnover plus growth sets the irreducible annual new-employee training load.

## Mental Models
- **You cannot delegate motivation; treat training the same way.** The symmetry argument is the whole case.
- **Teaching demands deeper knowledge than doing.** The stick-shift-over-the-phone test.
- **Explaining reveals your own gaps.** The manager is the primary beneficiary of the course.
- **A course that contradicts your practice does damage, not nothing.** Misalignment demoralizes.
- **Start small and ship the first lecture before writing the second.** Course preparation expands to fill any schedule you don't set.
- **Expect thirds**: too detailed, too superficial, just right. Judge by your own objective instead.

## Anti-patterns
- **Delegating training to specialists** while accepting that motivation can't be delegated.
- **Canned outside courses that contradict how you actually operate** — see the career-development example below.
- **Training as a rescue effort** for the problem of the moment rather than a standing process.
- **Bogging down in course preparation**: "you'll be tempted to go into **more and more background until this begins to obscure the original objective of your course.**"
- **Perfecting the first delivery** instead of accepting it as the throwaway.
- **Teaching the first version to your least knowledgeable people**, who will be confused rather than helpful.
- **Launching a company-wide one-day course casually** without pricing the students' time.
- **Trying to please every student.**

## Worked Example — The Restaurant Without Wine
Grove and his wife booked dinner by phone. "The woman who took reservations seemed **flustered and then volunteered that she was new and didn't know all the ropes.** No matter, we were booked."

On arrival they "quickly learned that the restaurant had **lost its liquor license** and that its patrons were expected to bring their own wine if they wanted any. As the maitre d' rubbed his hands, he asked, **'Weren't you told this on the phone when you made your reservations?'**"

"As we went through our dinner without wine, **I listened to him go through the same routine with every party he seated.** I don't know for sure, but it's probably fair to assume that **nobody instructed the woman taking calls to tell potential guests what the situation was.** Instead, the maitre d' had to go through an **inept apology time and time again**, and nobody had wine — **all because one employee was not properly trained.**"

**Why the example works**: the cost is invisible on any ledger (no scrap, no incident report), recurs with every single customer, and is absorbed entirely by the *maitre d's* repeated humiliation and the guests' disappointment. The training gap is one sentence long.

## Worked Example — The Ion Implanter and the Million-Dollar Scrap
At an Intel silicon fabrication plant, "one of our sophisticated pieces of production machinery — a machine called an **ion implanter** — **drifted slightly out of tune.**"

"The machine operator, like the woman at the restaurant, was relatively new. **While she was trained in the basic skills needed to operate the machine, she hadn't been taught to recognize the signs of an out-of-tune condition.** So she continued to operate the machine, subjecting **nearly a day's worth of almost completely processed silicon wafers** to the wrong machine conditions."

**The cost, compounding**: "By the time the situation was discovered, **material worth more than one million dollars had passed through the machine — and had to be scrapped.** Because it takes **over two weeks** to make up such a loss with fresh material, **deliveries to our customers slipped, compounding the problem.**"

**What to extract**: (1) she *was* trained — on operation, but not on **fault recognition**, which is the gap most training plans have; (2) the wafers were **almost completely processed**, so the loss struck at the highest-value stage (Ch 1's lowest-value-stage rule violated by omission); (3) the damage didn't stop at scrap — it propagated to schedule and then to customers. **"Insufficiently trained employees, in spite of their best intentions, produce inefficiencies, excess costs, unhappy customers, and sometimes even dangerous situations."**

## Worked Example — The Career-Development Course That Backfired
"Recently, some **outside consultants** taught a course on career development at Intel. Their approach was **highly structured and academic — and very different from anything practiced at the company.**"

**The mismatch, precisely**: "While they advocated **career plans that looked ahead several years, together with carefully coordinated job rotations** based on them, **our tradition has been more like a free market: our employees are informed of job opportunities within the company and are expected to apply for desirable openings on their own initiative.**"

**The result**: "**Troubled by the disparity between what was taught in the course and what was practiced, the participants got a bit demoralized.**"

**The lesson**: a course out of step with your actual practice is worse than no course. It doesn't fail to teach — it teaches employees that either the training or the company is wrong, and both readings cost you.

## Key Takeaways
1. Training is among the highest-leverage things a manager does: ~12 hours in, ~200 hours out at just 1% improvement.
2. Motivation and capability are the only two levers on individual performance; neither can be delegated.
3. Effective training must match how your organization actually works — canned outside courses risk demoralizing people.
4. Only you can be the credible role model in front of the room; proxies cannot fill that role.
5. Make training a scheduled process, not a crisis response, and cascade the obligation down every level.
6. Distinguish new-employee training (sized by turnover + growth, often ~20%/year) from new-skill training (roughly 5× larger) and price the students' time before committing.
7. Build the list from the trivial to the value-system level, and ask your people what they need.
8. Start with one three-to-four-lecture course, set deadlines, ship lecture one before writing lecture two.
9. Plan the first delivery as a throwaway and give it to your most knowledgeable subordinates for critique.
10. Train instructors from your first set of lectures if the organization needs many repetitions.
11. Collect anonymous ratings plus open questions, expect thirds, and judge against your own objective.
12. Expect to be the biggest learner — teaching a task requires much deeper knowledge than doing it.
