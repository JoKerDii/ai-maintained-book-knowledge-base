# Chapter 15: Compensation as Task-Relevant Feedback

## Core Idea
Pay is a **feedback channel**, not just an expense. Merit-based compensation only works if you accept that **someone has to finish last**, and promotion should continue until incompetence is reached — with **recycling**, not firing, as the remedy when it is.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Money's Dual Role Across the Hierarchy**: money serves physiological and safety needs (food, housing, insurance), but higher up it becomes **"a measure of one's worth in a competitive environment."**
  - **The test (from Ch 11)**: if the **absolute** amount of a raise matters, the person is motivated by physiological/safety needs. If the **relative** amount — what he got compared to others — is the issue, he is motivated by self-actualization, "because money here is a **measure, not a necessity.**"
  - **The middle-manager band**: "middle managers are usually paid **well enough that money does not have crucial material significance to them, but not well enough that it is without any material significance.**"
  - **The supervisor's obligation**: "one middle manager's needs can differ greatly from another's, depending on individual circumstances — number of children, a working spouse or not… **You must be especially careful not to project your own circumstances onto others.**"

- **Why Middle-Manager Pay Is Hard to Tie to Performance**: "a middle manager **cannot be paid by the piece**, his job can never be defined by simple output. And because **his performance is woven into the performance of a team**, it is hard to design a compensation scheme tied directly to the individual performance of a middle manager." So you build compromises.

- **The Performance Bonus, Scaled by Level**:
  | Level | Bonus as % of total compensation | Rationale |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Highly paid senior manager** | **as high as 50%** | "the absolute dollars make relatively little difference" |
  | **Middle manager** | **10–25%** | Pay is "at a level where substantial fluctuations could cause personal hardship" — but you can "at least give a taste of task-relevant feedback" |
  - **The principle**: the bonus percentage **should rise with total compensation.**

- **Five Design Questions for a Bonus Scheme**:
  1. **Team or individual?** Is the performance linked to a team or mostly individual work?
  2. **Which team?** A project team, a division, or the entire corporation?
  3. **What period?** "Cause and effect tend to be offset from each other, often by a long time, but **a bonus needs to be paid close enough to the time the work was done that the subordinate can remember why it was awarded.**"
  4. **What basis?** Strictly countable items (financial performance), measurable objectives, or subjective elements — the last risking being "drawn into a **beauty contest.**"
  5. **What's the floor?** "We don't want to devise something that **pays out lavishly even as the company is going bankrupt.**"

- **The Three-Factor Bonus Structure** (Grove's worked design): base the bonus on three components —
  1. **Individual performance only**, as judged by his supervisor.
  2. **His immediate team's objective performance** — his department, perhaps.
  3. **The overall financial performance of the corporation.**
  - **Why splitting works**: "When you take, let's say, 20 percent of a manager's compensation and split it into three parts, **any one will have only a small impact on total compensation, yet attention will still be called to its significance.**"
  - **The honest caveat**: "**No matter what way you choose to determine bonuses, none gives you exactly what you want**, but most of them will spotlight performance and deliver task-relevant feedback."

- **The Two Pure Forms of Salary Administration** (and the compromise everyone actually uses):
  | | **Experience-only** | **Merit-only** |
  |---|---|---|
  | Rule | Salary increases with time spent in the position | "I don't care if you are one year out of college or have spent twenty years in the work force. **I only care to see how you perform in this job.**" |
  | Shared constraint | **Any job has a maximum value** — salary ultimately has to level off | Same: a given job still has a maximum value |
  | Ease of administration | **Easiest.** "If your subordinate does not like the raise, all you have to do is show him the book where it says that for X amount of time on the job he deserves and gets Y amount of pay." | Hardest — requires allocating a finite resource, which "requires thought and effort" |
  | Practiced by | Large Japanese companies (no performance distinction in the first ~10 years, "**which are probably the most productive years of a professional's life**"); unions; most government jobs | Impractical in pure form — "**it is very hard to ignore a person's experience as you try to pay a fair salary**" |
  - **The compromise**: "a family of curves… The shapes of all of them approximate the curve representing the experience-only approach, but… **while people start at the same salary level, they move up at different speeds and arrive at different places, depending upon individual performance.**"
  - **The message experience-only sends**: "Apart from whether this is fair or not, **the message from management is that performance doesn't matter much.**" Grove's example: teachers — "A good one gets paid the same salary as a bad one if they both have been around for the same length of time. How a teacher is evaluated is not usually tied even symbolically to compensation, **which makes me wonder if the pass/fail system of grading did not have its origin in the way the typical teacher is paid.**"
  - **The social-norm trap**: "even though we say that every job has a finite value where compensation should level off, **we often let an individual become too highly paid because we, management, keep giving routine raises.**"

- **The Hard Requirement of Merit Pay**: "any merit-based system requires a **competitive, comparative evaluation of individuals.**"
  - **"Merit-based compensation simply cannot work unless we understand that if someone is going to be first, somebody else has to be last."**
  - **The asymmetry Grove points at**: "As Americans, we have no problem accepting a competitive ranking in a sports event. **Even the person who comes in last in a race feels comfortable about the system that says someone has to finish last.** But at work, unfortunately, competitive ranking frequently becomes a highly charged issue, difficult to accept and to administer — **yet it is a must if we want to use salary as a way to encourage performance.**"

- **Promotions**: defined as **a substantial change in a person's job** — "very important to the health of any organization and should be considered with great care." They produce a big raise, and (from Ch 13) they are "readily seen by other members of the organization, and so take on a vitally important role in communicating a value system." **"Promotions must be based on performance, because that is the only way to keep the idea of performance highlighted, maintained, and perpetuated."**

- **Grove's Restatement of the Peter Principle** — the ratings-oscillation model:
  - The classic form: "when someone is good at his job, he is promoted; he keeps getting promoted until he reaches his **level of incompetence** and then stays there."
  - **Grove's version, tracked over a career**:
    1. **Point A**: the demands of Job 1 so tax him that he performs in an average fashion — he **"meets the requirements."**
    2. With more training and motivation he improves to above average — he **"exceeds the requirements."**
    3. He is now promotable, and is promoted to Job 2, where he **at first performs only at "meets requirements."**
    4. With more experience he again "exceeds the requirements," and the cycle repeats.
  - **"Thus, an achiever will alternate between the 'meets requirements' and the 'exceeds requirements' ratings throughout his career, until he eventually settles at a 'meets requirements' level, at which time he will no longer be promoted. This, perhaps, is a better description of how the Peter Principle works."**

- **Why There Is No Alternative**: "If we take a person at point B and **don't offer him more work and greater challenges** even though he 'exceeds the requirements' of Job 1, **we are not fully utilizing a human resource of the company. In time, he will atrophy, and his performance will return to a 'meets requirements' level and stay there.**"
  - **The two types of "meets" performer**:
    | Type | Description |
    |---|---|
    | **Noncompetitor** | "Has no motivation to do more or faces no challenge to do more… settled and satisfied in his job." |
    | **Competitor** | "Each time he reaches a level of 'exceeds requirements,' he becomes a candidate for promotion. Upon being promoted, he very likely becomes a 'meets' performer again. **This is the person Dr. Peter wrote about.**" |
  - **The conclusion**: "we really have no choice but to promote until a level of 'incompetence' is reached. **At least this way we drive our subordinates toward higher performance, and while they may perform at a 'meets' level half the time, they will do that at an increasingly more challenging and difficult job level.**"

- **Recycling — the Remedy for a Promotion That Went Too Far**: when someone is "promoted into a position so much over his head that he performs in a below-average fashion for too long a time," the solution is to **recycle** him — "to put him back into the job he did well before he was promoted."
  - **Why it rarely happens**: "this is a very difficult thing to do in our society. **People tend to view it as a personal failure. In fact, management was at fault for misjudging the employee's readiness for more responsibility.** Usually the person who was promoted beyond his capability is **forced to leave the company** rather than encouraged to take a step back. This is often rationalized by the notion that '**It is better that we let him go, for his own sake.**'"
  - **Grove's verdict**: "**I think it is dead wrong to force someone in such circumstances out of the company.** Instead, I think management ought to **face up to its own error in judgment** and take forthright and deliberate steps to place the person into a job he can do. Management should also **support the employee in the face of the embarrassment** that he is likely to feel."
  - **The payoff**: "**If recycling is done openly, all will be pleasantly surprised how short-lived that embarrassment will be.** And the result will be a person doing work we **know** from past experience he can perform well. In my experience, **such people, once they regain their confidence, will be excellent candidates for another promotion at a later time — and the second time they are likely to succeed.**"

## Key Concepts
- **Compensation as task-relevant feedback** — pay is a message about performance, read as such whether or not you intend it.
- **Performance bonus** — the portion of pay that varies, scaled up with seniority.
- **Beauty contest** — the failure mode of a bonus based on subjective elements.
- **Family of curves** — the merit/experience compromise: same start, different slopes and endpoints.
- **Recycling** — returning an over-promoted person to a job they did well, openly and with support.
- **Competitor vs. noncompetitor "meets" performer** — identical ratings, opposite meanings.

## Mental Models
- **Read the raise, not the rating.** A mismatch between review words and dollars is one of the seven complaints in Ch 13 — the money is the message people believe.
- **Every job has a ceiling.** Routine raises past it are a management failure, not generosity.
- **Merit pay is a ranking system whether you say so or not.** If you can't name who finished last, you aren't paying on merit.
- **Sports rank people comfortably; workplaces don't.** The discomfort is cultural, and Grove's answer is to accept it rather than dilute the system.
- **Oscillating between "meets" and "exceeds" is what a healthy career looks like** — not a sign of an underperformer.
- **A failed promotion is management's misjudgment.** Own it, and place the person; don't rationalize an exit as being for their own good.

## Anti-patterns
- **Projecting your own financial circumstances onto subordinates.**
- **Pure experience-only pay**: broadcasts that performance doesn't matter — Grove's teacher example and his suspicion about pass/fail grading.
- **A bonus that pays out lavishly while the company goes bankrupt.**
- **A bonus paid so long after the work that no one remembers why.**
- **Subjective bonus criteria** that turn the scheme into a beauty contest.
- **Routine raises past a job's finite value.**
- **Refusing to rank** while claiming to pay on merit.
- **Withholding challenge from a high performer**: they atrophy back to "meets" and stay there.
- **Firing the over-promoted instead of recycling them**, rationalized as being for their own sake.

## Worked Example — Designing a 20% Bonus
Grove's illustrative scheme for a manager whose bonus is 20% of total compensation, split three ways:

| Factor | What it measures | Who judges | Why include it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Individual performance only** | His supervisor | Direct task-relevant feedback on his own work |
| 2 | **His immediate team's objective performance** (his department) | Objective measures | Ties his reward to the output he is actually accountable for (Ch 3's output equation) |
| 3 | **Overall corporate financial performance** | Company results | Prevents optimizing locally while the company fails; also the bankruptcy guard |

**The deliberate consequence**: each third is only ~6–7% of total pay — small enough that no single factor can cause hardship, large enough that "attention will still be called to its significance."

**What to check against the five design questions**: it explicitly answers *team or individual* (both), *which team* (department and corporation), and *what basis* (supervisor judgment for factor 1, objective measures for 2 and 3, avoiding the beauty contest). It leaves *what period* to you — with the constraint that the subordinate must still remember what the bonus was for.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat compensation as task-relevant feedback; it will be read that way regardless.
2. Diagnose whether money is a necessity or a measure for each person, and don't generalize from your own situation.
3. Scale performance bonus percentage with total compensation — up to ~50% for senior managers, 10–25% for middle managers.
4. Split the bonus across individual, team, and corporate factors so each signals without any one causing hardship.
5. Pay bonuses close enough in time that the recipient remembers the work, and cap the scheme so it can't pay out while the company fails.
6. Use the merit/experience compromise — same start, different slopes — and recognize that pure experience-only pay announces that performance doesn't matter.
7. Accept comparative ranking as the unavoidable cost of merit pay: someone must finish last.
8. Base promotions on performance; they are the loudest values statement you make.
9. Expect achievers to oscillate between "meets" and "exceeds"; promote until incompetence, because withholding challenge causes atrophy.
10. Recycle over-promoted people openly, name management's misjudgment, support them through the embarrassment — and expect them to succeed on a later promotion.
