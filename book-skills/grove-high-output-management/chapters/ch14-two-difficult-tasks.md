# Chapter 14: Two Difficult Tasks

## Core Idea
Two emotionally charged tasks resist all technique: **interviewing** (where you must judge potential in an hour, and "careful interviewing… merely increases your odds of getting lucky") and **saving a valued employee who quits** (where your reaction in the first thirty seconds decides the outcome).

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Four Purposes of an Interview**:
  1. **Select a good performer.**
  2. **Educate him** as to who you and the company are.
  3. **Determine if a mutual match exists.**
  4. **Sell him on the job.**
  - **The honest framing**: "We know how hard it is to assess the actual past performance of our own subordinates even though we spent much time working closely with them. Here we sit somebody down and try to find out in an hour how well he is likely to perform in an entirely new environment. **If performance appraisal is difficult, interviewing is just about impossible.** The fact is, we managers have no choice but to perform the interview, no matter how hard it is. **But we must realize that the risks of failure are high.**"

- **The 80% Rule and Interview Control**: "**The applicant should do 80 percent of the talking** during the interview, and **what** he talks about should be your main concern. But you have a great deal of control here by being an **active listener**."
  - **Interrupt.** "When you ask a question, a garrulous or nervous person might go on and on with his answer long after you've lost interest. Most of us will sit and listen until the end **out of courtesy.** Instead, you should interrupt and stop him, because if you don't, **you are wasting your only asset — the interview time.**" Apologize if you like, and say: *"I would like to change the subject to X, Y, or Z."*
  - **"The interview is yours to control, and if you don't, you have only yourself to blame."**

- **The Shared-Vocabulary Rule**: "An interview produces the most insight if you steer the discussion toward **subjects familiar to both you and the candidate**… but this should be done **in terms familiar to you**, so that you can evaluate its significance. In short, **make sure the words used mean the same thing to both of you.**"

- **The Four Information Categories** — what you are actually mining for:
  1. **Technical knowledge / skills** — "not engineering or scientific knowledge, but what he knows about performing the job he wants — his skill level." For an accountant, accounting; for a tax lawyer, tax laws; for an actuary, statistics and actuarial tables.
  2. **What he *did* with what he knows** — how he performed in an earlier job *using* those skills. "Not just what the candidate knows, but also what he **did** with what he knows."
  3. **Discrepancies** — "the reasons why there may be any discrepancy between what he **knew** and what he **did**, between his capabilities and his performance."
  4. **Operational values** — "those that would guide him on the job."

- **The Question Bank, Mapped to Categories** (compiled from a group of managers):
  | Category | Questions |
  |---|---|
  | **Technical / Skills** | Describe some projects that were highly regarded by your management, especially by the levels above your immediate supervisor. • What are your weaknesses? How are you working to eliminate them? |
  | **What He Did With Knowledge** | What do you consider your most significant achievements? Why were they important to you? • What do you consider your most significant failures? |
  | **Discrepancies** | What did you learn from those failures? • What are some of the problems you are encountering in your current position? How are you going about solving them? What could you have done to prevent them from cropping up? |
  | **Operational Values** | Why do you think you're ready for this new job? • Convince me why my company should hire you. • Why do you think an engineer should be chosen for a marketing position? *(vary according to the situation)* • What was the most important course or project you completed in your college career? Why was it so important? |

- **The Deliberate Exception to the Potential Trap**: "The ultimate purpose of interviewing is to make a judgment about how the candidate would perform in **your** company's environment. **This is at odds with a principle we stressed about performance reviews: the need to avoid the 'potential' trap. But when you're hiring, you must judge potential contribution.**" Within an hour you must "move between the world of the past employer and your own, and **project the candidate's future performance in a new environment based on his own description of past performance.** This managerial task is clearly tricky and high-risk, but unfortunately unavoidable."

- **Direct Questions Get Direct Answers**: "You can't get away from relying on a candidate's **self-assessment.** But that's not a bad way to get direct answers to direct questions." Ask *"How good are you technically?"* — "the interviewee might be taken back momentarily but then clear his throat and say timidly, 'Well, I think I'm pretty good…' As you listen, you'll probably get a decent fix on how capable he really is. **Don't worry about being blunt; direct questions tend to bring direct answers, and when they don't, they produce other forms of insight.**"

- **Let the Candidate Interview You**: "Ask the candidate what he would like to know about you, the company, or the job. **The questions he asks will tell you what he already knows about the company, what he would like to know more about, and how well prepared he is** for the interview."
  - **Grove's counter-example, told against himself**: a prospective manager arrived with a copy of the annual report "which he had read very carefully and marked up with penetrating questions. **In fact, I couldn't answer many of them. I was very impressed. We hired him and he failed badly on the job.** As I said, interviewing is a high-risk proposition…"

- **Reference Checking**: "you'll often be talking to a total stranger, so even if he comments freely, **what he says won't have much meaning to you without some knowledge of how his company does business and what values it works by.** Moreover, **while few references will out-and-out lie, they tend not to volunteer specific critical remarks.**"
  - **You're after the same four categories** you sought from the candidate directly.
  - **Build a bond to get real information**: "If you know the reference personally, you have a much better chance of getting 'real' information. If you don't, **try to keep him on the phone long enough to let some sort of personal bond develop.** If you can uncover some common experience or association, the reference will probably become more open with you. **In my experience, the last ten minutes of a half-hour conversation are much more valuable than the first ten minutes**, thanks to that bond."
  - **Re-interview afterward**: "you should talk with the applicant again after you have checked his references, because you may have gotten some new perspectives. Such a follow-up interview can be quite a focused affair."

- **No Tricks**: Admiral Rickover, interviewing every candidate for the Navy's nuclear submarine program, reportedly used techniques like **seating the candidate on a three-legged chair** — "when it tipped over, the poor man would be left sprawling on the floor. Rickover evidently thought the trick tested strength of character in the face of embarrassment."
  - **Grove's rejection**: "I think the interview should be **completely straightforward.** Remember, a candidate is a **potential employee.** He will go away from having talked to you with **a strong set of first impressions. If those are wrong and you hire the person, it will take a long time before they change.** So show yourself and your environment as they really are."

- **The "I Quit!" Protocol** — for the employee Grove specifically cares about: "not… someone whose motives are more money and better perks at another company, but… **an employee who is dedicated and loyal yet feels his work is not appreciated.** You and the company don't want to lose him, and his decision to leave reflects on you. **If he feels his efforts have gone unrecognized, you have not done your job and have failed as his manager.**"
  1. **Recognize the setup.** "The opening shot usually occurs when you are **on the run.** On your way to what you consider an important meeting, your subordinate timidly stops you and mutters under his breath, *'Do you have a minute?'*"
  2. **Understand the stakes of the first thirty seconds.** **"Your initial reaction to his announcement is absolutely crucial.** If you're human, you'll probably want to escape to your meeting… But **in almost all such cases, the employee is quitting because he feels he is not important to you. If you do not deal with the situation right at the first mention, you'll confirm his feelings and the outcome is inevitable.**"
  3. **Drop what you are doing. Sit him down. Ask why.** "Let him talk — **don't argue about anything with him.** Believe me, **he's rehearsed his speech countless times during more than one sleepless night.**"
  4. **Then ask more questions.** "After he's finished going through all his reasons for wanting to leave (**they won't be good ones**), ask him more questions. Make **him** talk, because **after the prepared points are delivered, the real issues may come out.**"
  5. **Don't argue, don't lecture, don't panic.** "Remember, this is only the opening skirmish, not the war. **And you cannot win the war here — but you can lose it!**"
  6. **Convey importance by action, and buy time.** "You have to convey to him **by what you do** that he is important to you… **Don't try to change his mind at this point, but buy time.** After he's said all he has to say, ask for whatever time you feel is necessary to prepare yourself for the next round. **But know that you must follow through on whatever you've committed yourself to do.**"
  7. **Make it your supervisor's problem too.** He'll also be on his way to an important meeting and will also try to put things off — "most probably **not because he doesn't care, but because the situation affects you more than your supervisor.** … **It is up to you to make it your supervisor's problem and make him participate in the solution to your problem.**"
  8. **Pursue every avenue — including giving him away.** "Your subordinate is a valued employee **of the company.** You now must vigorously pursue every avenue available to you to keep him with the firm, **even if it means transferring him to another department. If it seems that is the likely solution, you must become the project manager of that solution until the whole thing is settled.**"

- **The Golden Rule of Corporate Citizenship**: why put yourself out for an employee you're going to lose anyway? **"There is a basic principle involved: you owe it to your employer to save an employee for the company."** Beyond that: **"Today you save a valued contributor for the company by virtually giving him to a fellow manager. Tomorrow it will be his turn to do you the same favor. In the long run, if all managers take this position, they will all win."**

- **The Two Objections and Their Answers**:
  | Objection | Grove's response |
  |---|---|
  | *"You should have offered me this long ago — you're only doing it because I forced you. If I stay, you'll think of me as the blackmailer forever."* | **"You did not blackmail us into doing anything we shouldn't have done anyway. When you almost quit, you shook us up and made us aware of the error of our ways. We are just doing what we should have done without any of this happening."** |
  | *"I've accepted a job somewhere else and can't back out."* | **You have to make him quit again.** He has made **two** commitments: "first to a potential employer he only vaguely knows, and second to you, his present employer. And **commitments he has made to the people he has been working with daily are far stronger than one made to a casual new acquaintance.**" |

- **Why This Matters Beyond One Person**: "the good of the company is involved and **the issue is even more important than keeping one valued employee.** This subordinate is valuable and important because he has attributes that make him so. **Other employees respect him; and if they are like him, they identify with him. So other superior performers like him will track what happens to him, and their morale and commitment to the company will hinge on the outcome of this person's fate.**"

## Key Concepts
- **Active listening as control** — steering an interview by interrupting, not by talking.
- **Discrepancy** — the gap between what a candidate knows and what he did with it; often the most diagnostic of the four categories.
- **Operational values** — the same term as Ch 12; what will guide the person's judgment when unsupervised.
- **Corporate citizenship** — treating employees as the company's asset rather than your headcount.
- **The opening skirmish** — the resignation conversation, which can only be lost, never won.

## Mental Models
- **Interviewing is unavoidable and unreliable; act accordingly.** Increase your odds, don't expect certainty.
- **Hiring is the one place you must judge potential** — the exact opposite of the review discipline.
- **Time is your only asset in an interview.** Courtesy that wastes it is a failure of nerve.
- **First impressions are sticky, so show the environment as it is.** Tricks buy you a distorted candidate and a distorted employer.
- **A resignation is a verdict on you.** For the loyal, dedicated employee, it means their effort went unrecognized — which is your job undone.
- **Save the person for the company, not for your org chart.**

## Anti-patterns
- **Letting a nervous or garrulous candidate consume the hour** out of politeness.
- **Talking past each other in unshared vocabulary** so you can't evaluate what you hear.
- **Relying on reference checks to exempt you from the interview**: references rarely lie but rarely volunteer criticism.
- **Interview tricks** (Rickover's three-legged chair): the candidate is a potential employee forming durable first impressions.
- **"Let's talk later"** when someone says they're quitting — it confirms exactly the feeling that produced the resignation.
- **Arguing or lecturing during the resignation conversation** — the speech has been rehearsed over sleepless nights, and there is nothing to win.
- **Trying to change his mind on the spot** instead of buying time and following through.
- **Letting your supervisor defer it** because it's your subordinate, not his.
- **Treating a rehearsed reason as the real reason** — "they won't be good ones"; the real issues surface only after.

## Worked Example — The Cost Accountant Who Derived Wafer Costs
Grove interviewed a candidate for cost accountant at Intel: **a Harvard MBA from the food service industry.** "He knew nothing about the semiconductor business and I knew nothing about finance, **so we really couldn't talk in much detail about his technical ability to do the job.**"

**The improvised method**: Grove "decided to take him through the **semiconductor manufacturing process step by step.** After saying I would answer any specific questions he had, **I asked him what the finished cost of a wafer would be.**"

**What happened**: "He asked some questions and pondered matters for a while. He then **started to think his way through the basic semiconductor cost accounting principles, discovering some of them as he went along, and ultimately came up with the correct answer.**"

**The decision**: "He was hired, because this exercise demonstrated (**as it turns out, correctly**) that his **problem-solving capacity was first-rate.**"

**Why the design works**: neither party had domain overlap, so no shared-vocabulary conversation was possible in category 1 (technical knowledge). Grove **manufactured a shared context** by supplying the domain himself, converting the interview into a live test of categories 2 and 3 — what he does with what he knows, and how he closes a gap between knowledge and situation. The signal was the *derivation*, not the answer.

## Worked Example — The Hire Grove Still Can't Explain
"Several years ago I interviewed a person for a **high-level position** at Intel. I did the work **as carefully and thoroughly as I could.** I had a very good feel, I thought, of the whys and wherefores of the person's skills, past performance, and values, and we hired him."

**"From day one he was a disaster."**

"Much humbled, I've since gone over my notes from the interviews and the conversations with references. **To this day I haven't a clue about why I didn't spot the candidate's considerable flaws.**"

**The conclusion Grove draws**: "So in the end **careful interviewing doesn't guarantee you anything, it merely increases your odds of getting lucky.**"

**Why this belongs in a management text**: it sets the correct expectation. A manager who believes a good process guarantees a good hire will either blame themselves wrongly or, worse, stop doing the process when it fails. Grove is explicitly licensing the disappointment while insisting on the rigor.

## Key Takeaways
1. An interview has four purposes, and only one of them is selection — you are also educating, matching, and selling.
2. Accept that interviewing is nearly impossible and high-risk; do it rigorously anyway to improve the odds.
3. Let the candidate talk 80% of the time, but control the hour by interrupting and redirecting without apology.
4. Mine four categories: technical skill, what he did with it, discrepancies between the two, and operational values.
5. Keep the vocabulary shared, ask blunt direct questions, and use hypotheticals when domains don't overlap.
6. Judge potential when hiring — the one deliberate exception to the review's potential trap.
7. Skip tricks; the candidate is forming lasting first impressions of a place he may join.
8. Build a personal bond with references — the last ten minutes are worth more than the first ten — and re-interview afterward.
9. When someone valuable quits, drop everything immediately; the delay itself confirms the reason they're leaving.
10. Let them talk out the rehearsed speech, then ask more questions to reach the real issue; buy time rather than arguing.
11. Escalate to your supervisor and treat retention as a company-wide obligation, even if it means handing the person to a peer.
12. Remember the audience: other high performers are watching how this person is treated.
