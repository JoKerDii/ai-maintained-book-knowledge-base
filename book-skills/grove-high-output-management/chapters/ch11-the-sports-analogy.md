# Chapter 11: The Sports Analogy

## Core Idea
The single most important task of a manager is to **elicit peak performance** — and since underperformance has only two causes (**can't** or **won't**), the only two levers are **training and motivation**. Motivation cannot be injected; you can only build an environment where **self-actualization** takes over, which is why work should be endowed with the characteristics of competitive sports.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Can't/Won't Diagnostic**: "When a person is not doing his job, there can only be two reasons for it. The person either **can't** do it or **won't** do it; he is either **not capable** or **not motivated**."
  - **The test**: *if the person's life depended on doing the work, could he do it?* Yes → not motivated. No → not capable.
  - Grove on himself: "If my life depended on playing the violin on command, I could not do it. But if I had to run a mile in six minutes, I probably could. Not that I would want to, but if my life depended on it, I probably could."
  - **The two remedies map directly**: can't → **training**; won't → **motivation**.

- **Motivation Comes From Within**: "For most of us, the word implies doing something to another person. But I don't think that can happen, because motivation has to come from within somebody. **All a manager can do is create an environment in which motivated people can flourish.**"

- **Performance, Not Feeling, Is the Output**: "a subordinate's saying 'I feel motivated' means nothing. What matters is if he **performs** better or worse because his environment changed. An attitude may constitute an indicator, a 'window into the black box' of human motivation, but **it is not the desired result or output. Better performance at a given skill level is.**"

- **Why Fear Stopped Working**: for most of Western history motivation rested on fear of punishment — in Dickens's time, no work meant no pay meant no food, and stealing food meant hanging. The shift traces to the decline of manual labor and the rise of knowledge workers: **a manual laborer's output is readily measurable and departures spotted immediately, but for a knowledge worker departures take longer to detect "because even the expectations themselves are very difficult to state precisely."** Hence: **"fear won't work as well with computer architects as with galley slaves."**

- **Maslow's Hierarchy, Grove's Reading**: needs cause drives, which produce motivation. **"A need once satisfied stops being a need and therefore stops being a source of motivation. Simply put, if we are to create and maintain a high degree of motivation, we must keep some needs unsatisfied at all times."** People have concurrent needs, but one is always strongest and largely determines performance.
  | Level | What it is | Grove's note |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Physiological** | Things money buys — food, clothing, basic necessities | Fear is hitched here: fear of deprivation |
  | **Security/Safety** | Protection from slipping back into deprivation | Medical insurance protects against bankruptcy from hospital fees. **"The existence of benefits is rarely a dominant source of employee motivation, but if benefits were absent and employees had to worry, performance would no doubt be badly affected."** |
  | **Social/Affiliation** | Belonging — but not to *any* group; to one whose members share something with you | "When people are excited, confident, or happy, they want to be around people who are also excited, confident, or happy. Conversely, **misery loves not just any company, but the company of other miserable people.**" |
  | **Esteem/Recognition** | "Keeping up with the Joneses" — powerful when the Jones is last year's gold medalist or Laurence Olivier | **"Esteem exists in the eyes of the beholder."** A top player saying hello in the hall makes an aspiring high-school athlete feel terrific; family and friends meet the story with blank stares. |
  | **Self-Actualization** | *"What I can be, I must be."* The need to achieve one's utter **personal best** | **The only non-self-limiting need.** All others extinguish themselves once satisfied; self-actualization "continues to motivate people to ever higher levels of performance." |
  - **The dividing line**: "The physiological, safety/security, and social needs all can motivate us to **show up for work**, but other needs — esteem and self-actualization — **make us perform once we are there.**"

- **Two Roads to Self-Actualization**:
  - **Competence-driven** — concerned with job or task mastery. "A virtuoso violinist who continues to practice day after day is obviously moved by something other than a need for esteem and recognition… just as a teenager on a skateboard practices the same trick over and over again. **The same teenager may not sit still for ten minutes to do homework, but on a skateboard he is relentless.**"
  - **Achievement-driven** — a minority (**"*not* the majority"**) moved by an abstract need to achieve in all they do.

- **The Ring-Toss Experiment** (how to spot an achiever): volunteers were put in a room with pegs set at various distances on the floor and given rings, **with no instruction on what to do with them.** Three behaviors emerged:
  | Type | Behavior | Interpretation |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Gamblers** | Casually tossed rings at faraway pegs | High risk, **but no influence on the outcome** |
  | **Conservatives** | Stood over the pegs and dropped rings down | Very little risk |
  | **Achievers** | Walked **just far enough away that tossing a ring on was a challenge** | Working at the boundary of their capability |
  - **The finding**: "some people simply **must** test themselves. By challenging themselves, these people were likely to miss a peg several times, but when they began to ring the peg consistently, they gained satisfaction and a sense of achievement." Both competence- and achievement-oriented people **spontaneously** try to test the outer limits of their abilities.

- **The 50/50 Objective-Setting Rule** — for when the need to stretch is *not* spontaneous and management must create it: **"In an MBO system, objectives should be set at a point high enough so that even if the individual (or organization) pushes himself hard, he will still only have a fifty-fifty chance of making them. Output will tend to be greater when everybody strives for a level of achievement beyond his immediate grasp, even though trying means failure half the time."**

- **An Output-Valuing Environment**: to cultivate achievement-driven motivation you must value output, not knowledge. Grove's first job was at an R&D laboratory where people were highly motivated but **knowledge-centered** — "driven to know more, but not necessarily to know more in order to produce concrete results. Consequently, relatively little was actually *achieved*." **"The value system at Intel is completely the reverse. The Ph.D. in computer science who knows an answer in the abstract, yet does not apply it to create some tangible output, gets little recognition, but a junior engineer who produces results is highly valued and esteemed. And that is how it should be."**

- **Money: Necessity vs. Measure** — the pivot of the whole chapter.
  - **At lower levels**, money buys necessities and motivates **only until the need is satisfied.** Grove's Caribbean assembly plant: employees enjoyed a standard of living substantially above most of the population, yet in the early years many "worked just long enough to accumulate some small sum of money and then quit. Having reached a predetermined notion of how much money they wanted, more money and a steady job provided no more motivation."
  - **At the self-actualized level**, money becomes **a measure of achievement** and therefore motivates **without limit.** The venture capitalist who made ten million and works hard for another ten: physiological, safety, and social needs don't apply, and since VCs usually don't publicize successes, esteem doesn't either. "**The second ten million can be just as important as the first, since it is not the utilitarian need for the money that drives him but the achievement that it implies, and the need for achievement is boundless.**"
  - **The diagnostic test**: **"If the *absolute sum* of a raise an individual receives is important to him, he is working mostly within the physiological or safety modes. If, however, what matters to him is *how his raise stacks up against what other people got*, he is motivated by esteem/recognition or self-actualization, because in this case money is clearly a measure."**

- **Task-Relevant Feedback**: once in the self-actualization mode, a person needs measures to gauge progress — and **the most important measure is feedback on performance.**
  - For the competence-driven, the feedback mechanism is internal: "Our virtuoso violinist knows how the music should sound, knows when it is not right, and will strive tirelessly to get it right. **Accordingly, if the possibility for improvement does not exist, the desire to keep practicing vanishes.**"
  - **The Olympic fencer**: a Hungarian Olympic champion who immigrated to the U.S. quit fencing shortly after arriving. "He said that the level of competition here was not high enough to produce someone who could give him a contest, and that **he couldn't bear to fence any longer because every time he did, he felt his skill was diminishing.**"
  - **In the workplace**: the best measures **tie an employee's performance to the workings of the organization** — MBO indicators and milestones linked to individual performance. **"An obvious and very important responsibility of a manager is to steer his people away from irrelevant and meaningless rewards, such as office size or decor, and toward relevant and significant ones."** The most important form of task-relevant feedback is **the performance review** (Ch 13).

- **Fear of Failure — Positive or Negative**: at upper levels fear becomes fear of failure, and it cuts both ways. "Given a specific task, fear of failure can spur a person on, **but if it becomes a preoccupation, a person driven by a need to achieve will simply become conservative.**"
  - **The electric-shock variant of the ring toss**: "If a person got an electrical shock each time he threw a ring and missed, soon enough he would walk over to the peg and drop the ring from directly over it to eliminate the pain associated with failure." — i.e. **punishing failure converts achievers into conservatives.**
  - "In the upper levels of motivation, fear is not something coming from the outside. It is instead fear of not satisfying yourself… **You cannot stay in the self-actualized mode if you're always worried about failure.**"

- **The Sports Analogy Itself**: "Why does a person who is not terribly interested in his work at the office stretch himself to the limit running a marathon? What makes him run? **He is trying to beat other people or the stopwatch.**" People exert themselves to undreamed heights "not for money, but just to beat the distance, the clock, or other people."
  - **The cultural obstacle**: "Our society respects someone's throwing himself into sports, but anybody who works very long hours is regarded as sick, a **workaholic**. So the prejudices of the majority say that sports are good and fun, but work is drudgery, a necessary evil, and in no way a source of pleasure."
  - **The prescription**: "if you can't beat them, join them — **endow work with the characteristics of competitive sports.** And the best way to get that spirit into the workplace is to establish some **rules of the game** and **ways for employees to measure themselves.** Eliciting peak performance means going up against something or somebody."
  - **The manager's obligation**: "he has to **see the work as it is seen by the people who do that work every day** and then create indicators so that his subordinates can watch their 'racetrack' take shape."
  - **Coping with failure**: "in any competitive sport, at least 50 percent of all matches are lost. All participants know that from the outset, and yet **rarely do they give up at any stage of a contest.**"

- **The Manager as Coach — Three Attributes**:
  1. **Takes no personal credit for the success of his team** — "and because of that his players trust him."
  2. **Is tough on his team** — "by being critical, he tries to get the best performance his team members can provide."
  3. **Was likely a good player himself** — "and having played the game well, he also understands it well."

## Key Concepts
- **Self-limiting motivation** — every need except self-actualization stops motivating once satisfied.
- **Personal best** — Grove's shorthand for self-actualization, borrowed from the film title.
- **Racetrack** — an arena of measured competition created deliberately by a manager.
- **Task-relevant feedback** — performance measures tied to the actual work and the organization's workings.
- **The workaholic prejudice** — the cultural asymmetry that makes sports admirable and long hours pathological.

## Mental Models
- **Diagnose before you intervene.** Training and motivation are different medicines; the life-depends-on-it test tells you which.
- **You can't motivate anyone. You can only build the racetrack.**
- **Keep some needs unsatisfied.** A fully satisfied person is an unmotivated one — which is why only self-actualization scales.
- **Read the raise reaction.** Absolute sum → lower needs. Relative comparison → money as a measure.
- **Removing the competition removes the motivation.** The newspaper columnist who "thrived on beating the competition" and whose pleasure waned after the papers merged: *"I walked out to get the train, and I just thought: **There isn't anyone else to beat.**"*
- **Punishing failure manufactures conservatives.** The shocked ring-tosser walks to the peg and drops the ring.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating "I feel motivated" as the result**: attitude is at best an indicator; performance is the output.
- **Relying on fear with knowledge workers**: expectations are too hard to state precisely for fear to bite usefully.
- **Rewarding knowledge over output**: Grove's R&D lab, where highly motivated knowledge-centered people achieved relatively little.
- **Setting objectives you're confident of hitting**: the 50/50 rule exists because certainty suppresses output.
- **Irrelevant rewards** — office size, decor — which the manager is specifically responsible for steering people away from.
- **Making failure painful**: converts achievers into conservatives, permanently.
- **Removing the arena**: merge away the competitor, or become the only good player in the country, and the motivation dies with it.
- **A coach who takes credit for the team's success**: destroys the trust the role depends on.

## Worked Example — The Building Czars
"For years the performance of the Intel facilities maintenance group, which is responsible for keeping our buildings clean and neat, was **mediocre, and no amount of pressure or inducement seemed to do any good.**"

**The intervention**: a program in which each building's upkeep was **periodically scored by a resident senior manager, dubbed a "building czar."** Each score was then **compared with those given the other buildings.**

**The result**: "The condition of **all of them** dramatically improved almost immediately."

**What was *not* done**: **"Nothing else was done; people did not get more money or other rewards."**

**What they got instead**: **"a racetrack, an arena of competition. If your work is facilities maintenance, having your building receive the top score is a powerful source of motivation."**

**The design principles visible here**: (1) a **rule of the game** (periodic scoring on defined criteria); (2) a **way to measure yourself** against peers doing the same work; (3) scoring by a **resident senior manager** — someone whose esteem is worth having to that group; (4) **relative** comparison, which converts an ordinary metric into a contest. This is Ch 2's observation that comparing indicators across groups doing the same function "frequently has an electrifying effect."

## Worked Example — Moving Up the Hierarchy at Harvard Business School
Chuck, later an Intel manager, in his first year:
1. **Physiological/survival**: "engulfed by a fear of the class material, of his professors, of failure, of flunking out."
2. **Security/safety**: fear gave way to the realization that everyone else was in the same boat. **Students formed study groups "whose ostensible purpose was to consider class material together, but whose real purpose was to strengthen confidence."**
3. **Social/affiliation**: the study groups dissolved; students associated with the whole class. "The entire class, or 'section,' developed a definite and recognizable set of characteristics; **it became, in short, a team.** Members enjoyed belonging, associating, and identifying with it, and worked to sustain the section's image among the professors and other students."

**And the reverse is equally real**: a highly motivated, smoothly working team of manufacturing employees in a California plant was **"suddenly jolted — all too literally"** from a very high level of needs down to the most fundamental. **An earthquake shook the factory**; people feared for their lives, "dropped everything" — abandoning an inventory of silicon wafers, expensive equipment, even friends — and ran for the nearest exit, "totally consumed by the most fundamental of all physiological needs — survival." **Regression down the hierarchy can happen in seconds.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Underperformance is either can't or won't; use the life-depends-on-it test, then apply training or motivation accordingly.
2. You cannot motivate anyone — you can only build the environment. Judge it by performance change, never by expressed feeling.
3. Only self-actualization motivates without limit; every other need switches off once satisfied.
4. Lower needs get people to show up; esteem and self-actualization make them perform.
5. Set MBO objectives at roughly 50/50 odds, and build a value system that rewards output over knowledge.
6. Read whether money is a necessity or a measure by whether the absolute raise or the relative raise matters.
7. Supply task-relevant feedback tied to the organization's workings, and steer people away from office-size rewards.
8. Never punish failure into a preoccupation — it turns achievers into conservatives.
9. Create racetracks: rules of the game plus self-measurement, designed by seeing the work as the people doing it see it.
10. Coach by taking no credit, being genuinely tough, and knowing the game from having played it.
