# Chapter 13: Performance Appraisal: Manager as Judge and Jury

## Core Idea
The performance review is **the single most important form of task-relevant feedback** a supervisor can give, and its one fundamental purpose is **to improve the subordinate's performance** — not to justify a rating, not to unload every observation you've collected.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Purpose Hierarchy**: managers offer many reasons for reviews — assess work, motivate, provide feedback, justify raises, reward performance, provide discipline, give work direction, reinforce culture. "Though all of the responses given to my questions are correct, there is one that is more important than any of the others: **it is to improve the subordinate's performance.**"
  - The review works on two variables: **skill level** (what skills are missing, and how to remedy that lack) and **motivation** (getting him to a higher performance curve *at the same skill level*) — exactly the two levers of Ch 11.
  - "The review process also represents **the most formal type of institutionalized leadership.** It is the only time a manager is mandated to act as **judge and jury**: we managers are required by the organization that employs us to make a judgment regarding a fellow worker and then to deliver that judgment to him, face to face."

- **What's Wrong With Most Reviews** (Grove's middle managers, recalling reviews they had received): comments too general; **mixed messages** (inconsistent with the rating or the dollar raise); no indication of how to improve; negatives avoided; supervisor didn't know my work; only recent performance considered; **surprises**.
  - And what supervisors *feel* giving them: pride, anger, anxiety, discomfort, guilt, empathy/concern, embarrassment, frustration.

- **Output Measures vs. Internal Measures** — the black box applied to a person:
  - **Output measures**: the box's output — completing designs, meeting sales quotas, increasing production yield. "Things we can and should plot on charts."
  - **Internal measures**: what goes on *inside* the box — "whatever is being done to create output for the period under review **and also that which sets the stage for the output of future periods.**" Are we hitting production goals in a way that produces disgruntled employees two months out? Are we positioning and developing people so the business can handle future tasks? Are we doing the things that add up to a well-run department?
  - **"There is no strict formula by which we can compare the relative significance of output measures and internal measures. In a given situation, the proper weighting could be 50/50, 90/10, or 10/90 and could even shift from one month to the next. But at least we should know which two variables are being traded off against each other."**

- **Long-Term vs. Short-Term Trade-off**: an engineer must complete a design on schedule to generate revenue, but may also be building a **design method** that makes future similar products easier. Both need evaluating. **"A way to help weigh questions like this is the idea of 'present value' used in finance: how much will the future-oriented activity pay back over time? And how much is that worth today?"**

- **The Time Offset Between Activity and Output**: "the subordinate's output during the review period may have all, some, or nothing to do with his activities during the same period." The supervisor must explicitly look at the lag — in both directions:
  - **Output lagging behind bad work** — the "light from distant stars" case below.
  - **Output not yet existing** — in Intel's early years Grove had to review a subordinate setting up a production facility from scratch that had manufactured nothing yet. "The review could not wait for tangible output… **I gave my subordinate credit for doing well, even though output remained uncertain.**"
  - **The principle**: **"As managers, we are really called upon to *judge* performance, not just to see and record it when it's in plain sight."**

- **Reviewing a Manager — Person or Group?** **Both.** "Ultimately what you are after is the performance of the group, but **the manager is there to *add value* in some way. You need to determine what that is.**" Ask: Is he doing anything with his group? Is he hiring? Is he training the people he has and doing other things likely to improve the team's future output?

- **The Potential Trap**: "At all times you should force yourself to **assess performance, not potential.** By 'potential' I mean **form rather than substance.**"
  - **The rule**: **"The performance rating of a manager cannot be higher than the one we would accord to his organization!"**
  - **Why it matters institutionally**: had Grove approved the high rating, "Intel would have signaled to all at the company that to do well, **you must 'act' like a good manager, talk like one, and emulate one — but you don't need to perform like one.**"

- **Promotion as the Loudest Signal**: **"no action communicates a manager's values to an organization more clearly and loudly than his choice of whom he promotes. By elevating someone, we are, in effect, creating role models for others."**
  - On the old objection that promoting your best salesman "ruins a good salesman and gets a bad manager": **"But if we think about it, we see we have no choice but to promote the good salesman. Should our worst salesman get the job? When we promote our best, we are saying to our subordinates that performance is what counts."**

- **The Three L's of Delivery**:
  1. **Level** — "the credibility and integrity of the entire system depend on your being **totally frank**. And don't be surprised to find that **praising someone in a straightforward fashion can be just as hard as criticizing him** without embarrassment."
  2. **Listen** — Grove's redefinition: not hearing the subordinate, but **verifying that your message landed.** "The aim of communication is to transmit thoughts from the brain of person A to the brain of person B." A tape recorder to confirm the words used? **"The answer is an emphatic no. Words themselves are nothing but a means; getting the right thought communicated is the end."** The subordinate may be too emotional to understand, too preoccupied formulating answers to listen, or tuned out and thinking of going fishing — **"all the more so when A's message is laden with conflict."** Is a paraphrase enough? "I don't think so." **You must employ your entire arsenal of sensory capabilities — *watch* the person, read verbal and nonverbal responses — and "if his responses do not completely assure you that what you've said has gotten through, it is *your responsibility* to keep at it until you are satisfied that you have been heard and understood."**
     - **The bad-professor warning**: "All of us have had professors who lectured by looking at the blackboard, mumbling to it, and carefully avoiding direct eye contact with the class. **The reason: knowing that their presentation was murky and incomprehensible, these teachers looked away from their audience to avoid confirming visually what they already knew.** So don't imitate your worst professors while delivering performance reviews."
  3. **Leave yourself out** — "the performance review is **about and for your subordinate.** So your own insecurities, anxieties, guilt, or whatever should be kept out of it. At issue are the subordinate's problems, not the supervisor's, and **it is the subordinate's day in court.**" Emotions well up like stage fright no matter how many reviews you've given; control them.

- **The Finite-Capacity Principle** (for the ordinary mixed review): "your subordinate, like most people, has only a **finite capacity** to deal with facts, issues, and suggestions. **You may possess seven truths about his performance, but if his capacity is only four, at best you'll waste your breath on the other three.** At worst you will have left him with a case of **sensory overload**."
  - **"The purpose of the review is not to cleanse *your* system of all the truths you may have observed about your subordinate, but to improve *his* performance. So here less may very well be more."**

- **The Worksheet Method** — how to get from everything you know to the few things you'll say:
  1. **Scan the material** — progress reports, performance against quarterly objectives, one-on-one meeting notes.
  2. **Sit down with a blank piece of paper and write everything down. "Do *not* edit in your head."** Major, minor, and trivial, in no particular order, "knowing that doing so doesn't commit you to do anything." Then put the supporting documentation away.
  3. **Look for relationships** — "certain items are different manifestations of the same phenomenon, and… there may be some indications **why** a certain strength or weakness exists." These become **"messages."**
  4. **Draw conclusions and gather specific examples to support them.**
  5. **Apply the memory test**: "ask yourself if your subordinate will be able to **remember** all of the messages you have chosen to deliver. If not, **you must delete the less important ones.** Remember, what you couldn't include in this review, you can probably take up in the next one."

- **On Surprises**: if you've held regular one-on-ones and given guidance, there should never be surprises at a review, right? **"Wrong.** When you are using the worksheet, sometimes you come up with a message that will startle **you**." What then? "If the purpose of the review is to improve your subordinate's performance, **you must deliver it. Preferably, a review should not contain any surprises, but if you uncover one, swallow hard and bring it up.**"

- **The Five Stages of Problem-Solving** (used in "the blast" — the review for someone who could get fired):
  | Stage | What the subordinate does | What the supervisor needs |
  |---|---|---|
  | 1. **Ignore** | Passively ignores the problem | **Facts and examples** to demonstrate its reality |
  | 2. **Deny** | *Actively* denies it — "progress of some sort" over ignoring | Evidence |
  | 3. **Blame others** | Admits a problem exists but says it isn't *his* — "a standard defense mechanism… he can continue to avoid the responsibility and burden of remedying the situation" | **This is where things get stuck.** The first three "usually follow one another in fairly rapid succession." |
  | 4. **Assume responsibility** | "He has to say not only that there is a problem but that it is **his** problem. This is fateful, because it means work: *'If it is my problem, I have to do something about it. If I have to do something, it is likely to be unpleasant and will definitely mean a lot of work on my part.'*" | **The emotional step** — and the reviewer's actual job |
  | 5. **Find the solution** | "Relatively easy" once responsibility is assumed | **An intellectual step**, and shared between the two |
  - **The key insight**: "the move from blaming others to assuming responsibility constitutes an **emotional** step, while the move from assuming responsibility to finding the solution is an **intellectual** one, and the latter is easier."
  - **The supervisor's discipline**: "**keep track of what stage things are in.** If the supervisor wants to go on to find the solution when the subordinate is still denying or blaming others, **nothing can happen.**"

- **The Three Outcomes, and What's Acceptable**:
  1. Accepts your assessment **and** your cure, and commits.
  2. **Disagrees completely with your assessment but still accepts your cure.**
  3. Disagrees and does **not** commit.
  - **"I feel very strongly that any outcome that includes a *commitment* to action is acceptable."** Agreement is more *desirable*, so try for it up to a point — "but if you can't, accept his commitment to change and go on."
  - **"Don't confuse emotional comfort with operational need. To make things work, people do not need to side with you; you only need them to commit themselves to pursue a course of action that has been decided upon."** There seems to be something not quite nice about expecting someone to walk a path he'd rather not — "but on the job we are after a person's **performance**, not our psychological comfort."
  - **If they're stuck at blame-others**: assume the formal role endowed with position power — *"This is what I, as your boss, am instructing you to do. I understand that you do not see it my way. You may be right or I may be right. But I am not only empowered, I am **required** by the organization for which we both work to give you instructions, and this is what I want you to do…"* — then secure the **commitment** and **monitor performance against that commitment**.

- **Reviewing the Ace — The Reversed Priority**: Grove had ~20 middle managers analyze a review they'd received against the chapter's criteria. This group were achievers with mostly very high ratings, and the reviews were exceptionally well written. **But**: "they tended to be **retrospective assessments**… a majority of the reviews made little or no attempt to define what the subordinate needed to do to improve his performance **or even to maintain his current level.**"
  - **The pattern**: "for an achiever the supervisor's effort goes into **determining and justifying the judgment** of the superior performance, while giving little attention to how he could do even better. But for a poor performer, the supervisor tends to concentrate heavily on ways he can improve, providing detailed and elaborate **'corrective action programs.'**"
  - **Grove's verdict**: **"I think we have our priorities reversed. Shouldn't we spend more time trying to improve the performance of our stars? After all, these people account for a disproportionately large share of the work in any organization. Put another way, concentrating on the stars is a high-leverage activity: if they get better, the impact on group output is very great indeed."**
  - **"No matter how stellar a person's performance level is, there is *always* room for improvement. Don't hesitate to use the 20/20 hindsight provided by the review to show anyone, even an ace, how he might have done better."**

- **Three Practice Questions Answered**:
  | Question | Grove's answer | Why |
  |---|---|---|
  | Should the subordinate write a **self-review** first? | **No.** | "If you have to tell your supervisor about your accomplishments, he obviously doesn't pay much attention to what you are doing." Reviewing is a formal act of leadership; "if supervisors permit themselves to be prompted in one way or another, their leadership and their capacity for it will begin to appear false." Supervisors must commit to an **up-front judgment**. |
  | Should you ask subordinates to **evaluate you**? | **"This might be a good idea"** — with a boundary. | Make clear that assessing his performance is your job, while **his assessment of you has only advisory status**. "He is not your leader; you are his. And under no circumstances should you pretend that you and your subordinates are equal during performance reviews." |
  | Deliver the **written review** before, during, or after the discussion? | **Before.** | *After*: he finds a phrase he didn't "hear" and blows up. *During, in paragraph chunks*: "How can a supervisor ask a subordinate to stop at paragraph three when he is so eager to read the rest?" *Read aloud to control the session*: he's still eager to know what comes next and won't attend to what's being said; he also has no time to think and "is likely to walk away muttering, 'I should have said this…'" **Before**: "He can read the whole thing privately and digest it. He can react or overreact and then look at the 'messages' again. By the time the two of you get together, he will be much more prepared, both emotionally and rationally." |

## Key Concepts
- **Task-relevant feedback** — the review is its most important form.
- **The potential trap** — rating form rather than substance.
- **Messages** — the consolidated themes a review actually delivers, distinct from the raw observations behind them.
- **The finite capacity** — the constraint that makes editing a review a duty, not a courtesy.
- **Commitment vs. agreement** — the same distinction as Ch 5's support-vs-agreement.

## Mental Models
- **You are the judge and jury, once a year, by mandate.** No other managerial act is framed this way, and nothing in ordinary social training prepares you for it. Grove notes that when he arrived from Hungary, using the word "argument" for a difference of opinion drew corrections — *"you mean 'debate,' you mean 'discussion.'"* **"We are taught that the well-mannered skirt potentially emotional issues."**
- **Output can be light from distant stars.** Read today's numbers for the year they were actually earned.
- **A manager's rating is capped by his organization's.**
- **Listening means confirming receipt, not taking turns talking.**
- **Spend your best review effort on your best people** — that's where leverage lives.
- **If performance matters in your operation, performance reviews are absolutely necessary** — from "the insurance agent with two office assistants to administrators in education, government, and nonprofit organizations."

## Anti-patterns
- **Laundry lists, clichés, and superficiality**: leave the subordinate bewildered and improve nothing.
- **Cleansing your own system** of every truth you've observed.
- **Rating potential instead of performance**: teaches the organization to imitate good managers rather than be them.
- **Avoiding negatives** — one of the seven complaints managers made about their own reviews.
- **Considering only recent performance.**
- **Suppressing a surprise** because reviews "shouldn't have surprises."
- **Trying to reach the solution while the subordinate is still denying or blaming.**
- **Insisting on agreement for your own comfort** — see Grove's own error below.
- **Retrospective reviews for stars**: justifying the rating instead of raising the ceiling.
- **Prompted reviews** (subordinate-written self-reviews adopted wholesale).
- **Handing over the written review during or after the discussion.**

## Worked Example — The Review Worksheet
Grove's actual worksheet, showing raw observations condensing into deliverable messages:

```
POSITIVES                                    NEGATIVES
— planning process much better!              — spec process: zero!
  (quick start)
— good report to Materials Council           — debating society meetings — all mushy
— helped on Purchasing cost                  — poor kick-off for spec training
  analysis project

Messages
  1. Good results on planning system (analytical/financial background useful)
  2. Hard time setting clear, crisp goals — satisfied with activities instead of
     driving results!
  3. (No — let's just concentrate on #2!)
```

**What to notice**: (1) six raw observations across both columns collapse into **two** messages; (2) message 2 finds the *common cause* behind three separate negatives — the spec process, the mushy meetings, and the poor training kick-off are all one phenomenon, "satisfied with activities instead of driving results," which is precisely the activity-vs-output confusion of Ch 3; (3) **message 3 is struck out in the act of writing it** — the memory test applied in real time, "let's just concentrate on #2"; (4) the surviving positive names the *reason* for the strength ("analytical/financial background useful"), not just the fact of it.

## Worked Example — The Light From Distant Stars
"The organization of one of the managers reporting to me had had a **superb year.** All output measures were excellent, sales increased, profit margins were good, the products worked — **you could hardly even think of giving anything but a superior review** to the person in charge."

**The ignored signals**: "Yet I had some misgivings. **Turnover in his group was higher than it should have been, and his people were grumbling too much.** There were other such straws in the wind, but **who could give credence to elusive signs when tangible, measurable performance was so outstanding?** So the manager got a very positive review."

**The next year**: "his organization took a nose dive. Sales growth disappeared, profitability declined, product development was delayed, and the turmoil among his subordinates deepened."

**The reconstruction**: "I concluded that in fact **the manager's performance was improving in the second year, even as things seemed to go to hell.** The problem was that his performance had not been good a year earlier. **The output indicators merely represented work done years ago — the light from distant stars, as it were — which was still holding up. The time offset between the manager's work and the output of his organization was just about a year.**"

**The lesson Grove draws on himself**: "Greatly embarrassed, I regretfully concluded that the superior rating I had given him was **totally wrong.** **Trusting the internal measures, I should have had the judgment and courage to give the manager a much lower rating than I did in spite of the excellent output indicators** that did not reflect the year under review."

## Worked Example — "Why Do You Insist on Wanting to Convince Me?"
In one of Grove's first reviews, he was "trying very hard to persuade my subordinate to see things my way. He simply would not go along with me and finally said to me: **'Andy, you will never convince me, but why do you insist on wanting to convince me? I've already said I will do what you say.'**"

**"I shut up, embarrassed, not knowing why. It took me a long time before I realized I was embarrassed because my insistence had a lot to do with making me feel better and little to do with the running of the business."**

**The mirror case, from the other side.** A subordinate wrote a review Grove considered "superficial, lacking analysis and depth." After discussion the subordinate **agreed with the assessment** but "considered the issue not important enough… to spend time rewriting the review." Deadlocked after more spirited discussion, Grove "took a deep breath" and said: *"Look, I understand that you don't consider it worth your time to do it. **But I want you to do it.** I guess there is a basic difference between us. **The integrity of the performance review system is just more important to me than it is to you. That is why I have to insist.**"* The subordinate looked back and said simply, **"Okay."**

**The outcome**: "He thought I was out in left field and resented the fact that I made him spend time on something he thought was unimportant, but **he committed himself to redo the review, and, in fact, he did it well.** His subordinate ended up getting the reworked, much more thorough and thoughtful review, and **the fact that his review was rewritten without the agreement of my subordinate made no difference to him.**"

## Key Takeaways
1. The review's one governing purpose is to improve future performance; every other purpose is subordinate to it.
2. Weigh output measures against internal measures explicitly — know which two variables you are trading off, even without a formula.
3. Correct for the time offset between activity and output in both directions; judge performance rather than recording visible results.
4. Never rate potential over performance, and never rate a manager above his organization.
5. Promote your best performers — it is the loudest statement of values you will ever make.
6. Deliver with the three L's: level, listen (verify receipt with all your senses), and leave yourself out.
7. Respect finite capacity: use the worksheet, consolidate observations into a few messages, and cut anything the person won't remember.
8. Deliver an uncovered surprise anyway — swallow hard and bring it up.
9. Move a poor performer through ignore → deny → blame → assume responsibility; the emotional step is the one that matters and the one you must not skip past.
10. Accept commitment without agreement, and never confuse your emotional comfort with operational need.
11. Spend disproportionate review effort on your stars — that is where the leverage is.
12. Don't take self-reviews as input; take upward feedback as advisory; hand over the written review before the conversation.
