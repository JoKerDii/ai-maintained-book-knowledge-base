# Chapter 9: Dual Reporting

## Core Idea
Hybrid organizations only work if a person can have **two supervisors** — one supplying mission priorities, one supplying technical standards and career development — and if peer groups can supervise, which requires the **voluntary surrender of individual decision-making** underwritten by a strong corporate culture.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Dual Reporting**: an employee reports jointly to a mission-oriented supervisor and a functional one. The split of duties is the whole point:
  | | **Mission-oriented supervisor** (e.g. divisional general manager) | **Functional supervisor** (e.g. finance manager) |
  |---|---|---|
  | Provides | Priorities — asks the person to work on specific business problems | Professional methods, practices, and standards |
  | Monitors | Day-to-day performance, immediacy, operating priorities | Technical proficiency |
  | Owns | The business result | **Training and the career** — promoting the controller to a bigger, more complex division if he performs well |
  - Origin at NASA: putting a man on the moon required coordinating many contractors and subcontractors so that a slip in one place didn't subvert the schedule (resources could be diverted from a strong organization to a slipping one). The unintended byproduct was **matrix management**, whose core idea was that **a project manager outside any contractor could wield as much influence on a unit's work as the company's own management**. But the basic idea "had been quietly at work for many years, enabling hybrid organizations of all types to function, from the neighborhood high school to Alfred Sloan's General Motors."

- **Why Dual Reporting Is Fundamental — The Promotion Ladder Argument**: trace how a manager comes to be. Individual contributor (salesman) → sales manager (supervising his own functional specialty) → regional sales manager (now also supervising **field application engineers who know more about technical matters than he does**) → general manager of a business division, **with no experience whatsoever in manufacturing**.
  - He can supervise his manufacturing manager on the general aspects of the job, "but the new boss has no choice but to leave the technical aspects to his subordinate." In other divisions, manufacturing managers report to people who rose through engineering or finance.
  - **Why not just centralize?** Making all manufacturing managers report to a senior manufacturing manager moves you toward a totally functional form — and then "a general manager could no longer coordinate the activities of the finance, marketing, engineering, and manufacturing groups toward a single business purpose responsive to marketplace needs."
  - **"We want the immediacy and the operating priorities coming from the general manager *as well as* a technical supervisory relationship. The solution is dual reporting."**

- **Peer Groups as the Second Supervisor**: the technical supervisor need **not** be a single individual.
  - How one forms, in Grove's telling: two manufacturing managers from different divisions chat over coffee, realize they share technical problems, decide to meet more often; meetings become regularly scheduled; managers from other divisions join; **a committee or council of peers comes into existence.** "In effect, they now have supervision that a general manager competent in manufacturing could have given them, but that supervision is being exercised by a peer group."

- **The Voluntary Surrender of Individual Decision-Making**: making such a body work "requires the voluntary surrender of individual decision-making to the group. Being a member means you no longer have complete freedom of individual action, because you must go along with the decisions of your peers in most instances."
  - **The two-couples-vacation analogy**: "You know that if you go together you will not be free to do exactly what you want to do when you want to do it, but you go together anyway because you'll have more fun, even while you'll have less freedom."
  - **The precondition is trust** — "trust in no way relates to an organizational principle but is instead an aspect of the **corporate culture**… a set of values and beliefs, as well as familiarity with the way things are done and should be done in a company. **A strong and positive corporate culture is absolutely essential if dual reporting and decision-making by peers are to work.**"

- **The Ambiguity Trade** — why you accept it anyway:
  | Form | What it gives | What it costs |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Strictly functional** | Conceptual clarity | Removes engineering and manufacturing from the marketplace, "leaving them with no idea of what the customers want" |
  | **Highly mission-oriented** | Crisp reporting relationships, clear unambiguous objectives at all times | A fragmented state of affairs causing inefficiency and poor overall performance |
  | **Hybrid + dual reporting** | Both responsiveness and technical leverage | Ambiguity, which most people dislike |
  - **"It's not because Intel loved ambiguity that we became a hybrid organization. We have tried everything else, and while other models may have been less ambiguous, they simply didn't work. Hybrid organizations and the accompanying dual reporting principle, like a democracy, are not great in and of themselves. They just happen to be the best way for any business to be organized."**
  - **The standing caveat**: dual reporting is "not an excuse for needless busywork" — "we should mercilessly slash away unnecessary bureaucratic hindrance, apply work simplification to all we do, and continually subject all established requirements for coordination and consultation to the test of common sense. **But we should not expect to escape from complexity by playing with reporting arrangements.**"

- **The Two-Plane (Multi-Plane) Organization**: when a person takes on coordination work outside their regular daily job, their name appears on **two organization charts serving two separate purposes** — one to operate the unit, one to coordinate across units. The charts exist on different planes.
  - **The church analogy**: if Cindy belonged to a church, her supervisor there would be the local pastor, himself in a church hierarchy. "No one would confuse these two roles: clearly operating on different planes, each has its own hierarchies, and that Cindy is a member of both groups at the same time would hardly trouble anyone."
  - **Three planes work too** — Cindy could also join a task force needing her expertise: Intel, a church, and advisory work for the town's parks department. Separate roles, no conflict, "though they all do vie for Cindy's time."
  - **Everyday instance**: people mostly work at an operating task but also plan — "the hierarchy of the corporation's planning bodies lies on a plane separate from the one on which you'll find the operating groups."

- **Reversed Hierarchy Across Planes**: "I am president of Intel, but in another plane I am a member of a strategic planning group, where I report to its chairman, who is one of our division controllers."
  - **The Army Reserve analogy**: "on our weekend exercises I found myself under the command of a regimental leader who happens to be this division controller. Back at the operating ranch, I may be his supervisor or his supervisor's supervisor, but in the Army Reserve he is my commanding officer."
  - **Why this matters**: "Without it I could only participate if I were in charge of everything I was part of. I don't have that kind of time, and often I'm not the most qualified person around to lead. The multi-plane organization enables me to **serve as a foot soldier rather than as a general** when appropriate and useful. This gives the organization important flexibility."

- **Transitory Teams**: many such groups are temporary — task forces formed for a purpose, or informal collections of people solving one problem — and both dissolve once the problem is handled. **"The more varied the nature of the problems we face and the more rapidly things change around us, the more we have to rely on such specially composed transitory teams."** In electronics, "we can't possibly shift formal organization fast enough to keep up with the pace of advancing technology." Dual/multiple reporting and peer-group decision-making are both **necessary** for transitory teams to work — and **the key factor common to all is the use of cultural values as a mode of control** (Ch 10).

## Key Concepts
- **Matrix management** — NASA's grand-scale elaboration of dual reporting.
- **Functional supervision** — standards, technical monitoring, and career ownership, distinct from priority-setting.
- **Peer-group supervision** — a council of counterparts supplying the technical oversight no single boss can give.
- **Two-plane organization** — one person on two charts serving two purposes.
- **Transitory team** — a temporary body formed faster than the formal organization can change.
- **Trust as a cultural, not structural, property** — the load-bearing precondition of the whole system.

## Mental Models
- **A person can have two bosses if the two supervise different things.** The question "who is in charge here?" is the wrong question; ask "in charge of what?"
- **Promotion guarantees technical obsolescence.** By the time someone runs a division, they cannot technically supervise most of it — so someone else must.
- **Membership costs freedom and buys effectiveness** — the vacationing couples.
- **Rank is plane-specific.** You can be a general on one chart and a foot soldier on another, and that flexibility is an asset.
- **You cannot reorganize your way out of complexity.** Cut busywork ruthlessly, but don't expect a reporting diagram to remove the underlying ambiguity.

## Anti-patterns
- **Insisting every person has exactly one boss**: the staff's initial objection — "A person has to have a boss, so who is in charge here?" — which delayed the answer. Grove: "It was a slow, laborious birth."
- **Reporting security only to the plant manager**: an engineer or manufacturing person "who knows very little about security issues and cares even less."
- **Reporting security only to the corporate security manager**: he sits at headquarters and cannot know whether outlying officers even showed up or came in late.
- **Solving the general-manager gap by full functional centralization**: kills the GM's ability to coordinate toward a single market-responsive business purpose.
- **Letting a division hire its own advertising agency**: ads sell the whole corporation, not just one product, so the image must be consistent.
- **Treating dual reporting as license for busywork**: apply work simplification and the common-sense test to every coordination requirement.
- **Joining a peer body without surrendering individual decision-making**: the body then cannot supervise anything.

## Worked Example — Where Should Plant Security Report?
Intel's accidental discovery of dual reporting, worked through at a staff meeting when the company was young and small:

**Option 1 — report to the plant manager.** Problem: a plant manager is typically an engineer or manufacturing person "who knows very little about security issues and cares even less."

**Option 2 — report to the corporate security manager.** He hired them, he is the expert, he sets the standards they must follow, and security practices at outlying plants clearly had to conform to a corporate standard. Problem: **he works at headquarters.** "How would he know if the security personnel outside the main plant even showed up, or came in late, or otherwise performed badly? He wouldn't."

**The resolution**: report **jointly**. "The first would specify **how the job ought to be done**, and the second would monitor **how it was being performed day by day**."

**The cultural obstacle**: "While the arrangement seemed to solve both problems, the staff couldn't quite accept it. We found ourselves asking, 'A person has to have a boss, so who is in charge here?' Could an employee in fact have two bosses? The answer was a tentative 'yes,' and the culture of joint reporting relationships, dual reporting, was born."

## Worked Example — Advertising in a Multi-Division Company
**The case for divisional control**: each division understands its own strategy best, and therefore best understands what its advertising message should be and whom it should target.

**The case for corporate control**: the products of various divisions often serve one market and together represent a more complete solution than any single division provides — so the customer *and* the manufacturer benefit when the stories are told coherently. And advertising sells the entire corporation, not just a product, so the image must be consistent for everybody.

**The dual-reporting answer**:
- **Divisional marketing managers control most of their own advertising messages** — the specific selling message of an individual ad stays with divisional people.
- **A coordinating body of peers** — the divisional marketing managers, perhaps chaired by the corporate merchandising manager — provides functional supervision. It **chooses the advertising agency**, **determines the graphic image all divisional ads must conform to**, and **defines how divisions deal with the agency**, which can reduce space cost through volume buying.

**Grove's honest note on the cost**: "Dual reporting can certainly tax the patience of the marketing managers, as they are now also required to understand the needs and thought processes of their peers. **But no real alternative exists when you need to communicate individual product and market messages and maintain a corporate identity at the same time.**"

## Worked Example — Cindy on Two Planes
Cindy is a process engineer responsible for maintaining and improving one manufacturing process.
- **Plane 1 — operating the plant (80% of her time)**: a clear, crisp reporting line to her supervising engineer, and through him to the plant engineering manager. Daily work: manipulating equipment, watching process monitors, making adjustments.
- **Plane 2 — coordinating across plants**: monthly formal meetings with counterparts from the other production plants to identify, discuss, and solve shared process problems and to **standardize procedures across all plants**. Here she is supervised by the group's chairman. That group is in turn supervised by a more senior body, the **Engineering Managers Council**, made up of the engineering managers from all plants.

**The leverage payoff**: "In her main job, her knowledge affects the work that takes place in **one plant**; in her second, through what she does in the process coordinating group, she can influence the work of **all plants**. So we see that the existence of such groups is a way for managers, especially know-how managers, to increase their leverage."

## Worked Example — Ohio University (Wall Street Journal, with Grove's bracketed reading)
> "A university is an odd place to manage. The president of the University said, 'There's clearly a shared responsibility for decision-making between **administration [functional organization]** and **faculty [mission-oriented organization]**.' A **University Planning Advisory Council [a group of peers]** was formed with representation from the faculty and administration to help **allocate limited resources [a most difficult and common problem]** in the face of severe budget cuts. 'We are being educated to think institutionally,' said one council member. **'I'm representing student affairs, which had some projects up for consideration this year. But I made a big pitch for buying a new bulldozer.'**"

**Why Grove ends on the bulldozer line**: it is the voluntary surrender of individual decision-making in action — a representative advocating for another constituency's need over his own. That, not the org chart, is what makes a peer allocation body work.

## Key Takeaways
1. Dual reporting splits supervision: mission supervisor gives priorities and daily monitoring; functional supervisor gives standards, technical oversight, and career ownership.
2. It is fundamental, not exotic — promotion paths guarantee that senior managers cannot technically supervise most of what reports to them.
3. The second supervisor can be a peer group, which supplies the competent technical oversight no single boss possesses.
4. Peer bodies require voluntarily giving up individual decision-making, which requires trust, which is a property of culture, not structure.
5. Accept the ambiguity: functional-only loses the market, mission-only fragments, and hybrid+dual reporting is simply the least-bad arrangement.
6. Slash bureaucratic busywork ruthlessly, but never expect a reporting diagram to remove real complexity.
7. Use two- and multi-plane membership to multiply know-how managers' leverage from one unit to all units.
8. Let rank reverse across planes so people can be foot soldiers where they aren't the most qualified leader.
9. Rely on transitory teams when change outruns the formal organization's ability to shift.
