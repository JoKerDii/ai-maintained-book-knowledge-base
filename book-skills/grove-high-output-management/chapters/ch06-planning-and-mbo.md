# Chapter 6: Planning: Today's Actions for Tomorrow's Output

## Core Idea
Planning is a three-step gap analysis — **environmental demand vs. present status, reconciled by action** — and its real output is not a document but **the tasks you start today** to prevent tomorrow's problem. MBO is that same process compressed to daily work.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Three-Step Planning Process** (derived directly from factory production planning: determine market demand → establish what the factory will produce unadjusted → reconcile by adjusting the schedule):
  1. **Establish projected need or demand** — what will the environment demand from you, your business, or your organization?
  2. **Establish your present status** — what are you producing now? What will you produce as pipeline projects complete? **"Where will your business be if you do nothing different from what you are now doing?"**
  3. **Compare and reconcile 1 and 2** — what more (or less) must you do to produce what the environment will demand?

- **Defining Your Environment**: treat your group as a stand-alone company. Its environment is the other groups that directly influence what you do — **customers** (the rest of the company), **vendors** (capability suppliers), and **competitors**.
  - You have no internal competitors, but you can benchmark against an external analogue — Grove's mailroom manager compares his service to United Parcel "as a way to judge performance and set standards."
  - What to examine: customers' expectations **and their perception of your performance**; technological developments and alternative ways of doing your job (e.g. electronic mail replacing physical mail); vendor performance; and the performance of other groups you depend on (does the traffic department limit how well you can work? can it meet your needs?).

- **Difference Analysis** — the crux of Step 1. Examine the environment in **two time frames: now, and roughly a year out.**
  - What do my customers want from me now? Am I satisfying them? **What will they expect from me one year from now?**
  - Focus on the **difference** between the two, because if current activities satisfy current demand, then **anything more and new should be undertaken to match this difference.** "How you *react* to this difference is in fact the key outcome of the planning process."

- **Never Pre-Constrain the Demand Forecast**: at Step 1, do *not* consider what practical steps you could take — "that will just confuse the issue."
  - Grove's illustration: if marketing knew it could sell 100 widgets a month but believed manufacturing could deliver only ten, and therefore submitted a forecast of ten, **manufacturing would never tool up to satisfy the real demand.** Demand and capability must be assessed separately, then reconciled.

- **Present-Status Rules** (Step 2):
  - **Use the same "currency"** in which you stated demand. If demand is in completed product designs, work-in-process is *partially* completed product designs.
  - **Account for timing** — when does each project exit the pipeline?
  - **Factor in loss.** Not every project in flight will finish; some get scrapped or aborted. Benchmark: in semiconductor manufacturing **only ~80% of material started actually gets finished**. "It is prudent to factor in some percentage of loss for managerial projects as well."

- **Two Questions to Close the Gap** (Step 3): ask them **separately**.
  1. What do you **need** to do to close the gap?
  2. What **can** you do to close the gap?
  Then decide what you actually will do, evaluating **what** effect each action has on narrowing the gap and **when**. **"The set of actions you decide upon is your strategy."**

- **Strategy vs. Tactics** (Grove's working definition — he notes the distinction "is rarely of practical significance"): as you put your plan into words, **the most abstract and general summary of those actions meaningful to you is your strategy; what you'll do to implement it is your tactics.**
  - **"Frequently, a strategy at one managerial level is the tactical concern of the next higher level."**
  - Example: the corporate communications manager's **strategy** is to install electronic mail between all plants. The mailroom manager's **strategy** is then to install printers and set up a delivery service — which is merely the communications manager's **tactics**.

- **The Real Output of Planning**: **"the true output of the planning process is the set of tasks it causes to be implemented."**
  - Grove on Intel's annual plan: **"I, for one, hardly ever look at the bound volume finally called the Annual Plan."** The output is the decisions made and actions taken as a result of the thinking process throughout the organization.
  - The governing question: **"What do I have to do *today* to solve — or better, avoid — *tomorrow's* problem?"**
  - **"Today's gap represents a failure of planning sometime in the past."** Scrambling to decide how to close today's gap "is like scurrying after our car has already run out of gas. Clearly we should have filled up earlier."

- **Planning Horizon and Cadence**: Intel runs an annual strategic long-range plan examining five years out — but **"what is really being influenced here? It is the next year — and only the next year."** Year two gets replanned next year when it becomes year one.
  - **Rule**: you implement only the portion of a plan lying **within the window between now and the next time you run the exercise.** Everything else you can look at again.
  - **Don't plan too frequently** — you need time to judge the impact of decisions and see whether they were on the right track. That feedback is indispensable to the next round.

- **Who Plans**: the **operating management** of the organization. **"The idea that planners can be people apart from those implementing the plan simply does not work."** Planning cannot be a separate career; it is a key managerial activity with enormous leverage — but that leverage "can only be realized through a marriage, and a good collaborative one at that, between planning and implementation."

- **The "Yes" Implies a "No"**: "by saying 'yes' — to projects, a course of action, or whatever — you are implicitly saying 'no' to something else. Each time you make a commitment, you forfeit your chance to commit to something else." Planners must have **"the guts, honesty, and discipline to drop projects as well as to initiate them, to shake their heads 'no' as well as to smile 'yes.'"**

- **Management by Objectives (MBO)** — the planning process applied to daily work. Because concerns are short-range, you already know what the environment demands, so **MBO concentrates on steps 2 and 3** and makes them specific. The premise: **"If you don't know where you're going, you will not get there"** — or, the old Indian saying, *"If you don't know where you're going, any road will get you there."*
  - **A successful MBO system needs to answer only two questions:**
    1. **Where do I want to go?** → the **objective**.
    2. **How will I pace myself to see if I am getting there?** → the **key results** (milestones).
  - **The airport example**: objective — catch a plane in an hour. Key results — reach towns A, B, and C at 10, 20, and 30 minutes. "If I have been driving for 20 minutes and haven't yet made town A, I know I'm lost." The value is knowing *in time* to get off the highway and ask directions.
  - **Time frame**: feedback must arrive **very soon after the activity it measures**. If you plan annually, the MBO cycle should be **at least quarterly, perhaps monthly**.
  - **Focus is the point**: "The one thing an MBO system should provide par excellence is focus. This can only happen if we keep the number of objectives **small**." In practice we fall victim to our inability to say no — "**if we try to focus on everything, we focus on nothing.** A few extremely well-chosen objectives impart a clear message about what we say 'yes' to and what we say 'no' to."
  - **Nesting hierarchy**: a manager's objectives are supported by his key results; his objectives tie to his supervisor's objectives, so that **if the subordinate meets his objectives, the supervisor meets his.**
  - **MBO is a stopwatch, not a legal document**: "The MBO system is meant to pace a person — to put a stopwatch in his own hand so he can gauge his own performance. It is not a legal document upon which to base a performance review, but should be **just one input** used to determine how well an individual is doing."
  - **Both failure modes are unprofessional**: a supervisor who mechanically relies on MBO to evaluate a subordinate, *and* a subordinate who rigidly forgoes an emerging opportunity because it wasn't a specified objective, "are behaving in a petty and unprofessional fashion."
  - **The system cannot be run mechanically by a computer** — setting the hierarchy of objectives and the supporting key results requires judgment and common sense, as does using MBO day to day.

## Key Concepts
- **Difference analysis** — comparing current demand to demand a year out; the gap is what new work exists to fill.
- **Currency matching** — stating status in the same units as demand.
- **Pipeline loss factor** — the ~20% (in semiconductors) of started work that never finishes; apply an analogous discount to managerial projects.
- **Strategy** — the most abstract meaningful summary of your chosen actions.
- **Objective** — where you want to go. **Key result** — a dated, unambiguous milestone that paces you toward it.
- **Nesting hierarchy of objectives** — the subordinate's objective often *is* the supervisor's key result.

## Mental Models
- **Planning is checking the gas gauge, not a lofty ritual.** Estimate the trip, read the tank, decide whether to stop — everyone does this constantly without fanfare.
- **Today's problem is a receipt for yesterday's planning failure.** The only useful question is what to start now.
- **Forecast demand as if capability were unlimited**, then reconcile — otherwise you silently cap your own future.
- **A five-year plan influences exactly one year.** Everything past the next replanning cycle is provisional.
- **Every "yes" spends a "no."** Finite resources make dropping projects as much a planning act as starting them.

## Anti-patterns
- **Planning as a separate career**: planners apart from implementers "simply does not work."
- **Pre-constraining the forecast** by what you believe you can deliver.
- **Chasing today's gap with today's decisions**: you're scurrying after the tank is empty.
- **Treating the bound Annual Plan as the deliverable** rather than the decisions and actions it caused.
- **Planning too often**: you never get the feedback loop that makes the next plan better.
- **Too many objectives**: the failure to say no, which destroys the one thing MBO exists to provide.
- **Mechanical MBO** in either direction: as a performance-review contract by the supervisor, or as an excuse to ignore an opportunity by the subordinate.
- **Vague key results**: "to be useful a key result must contain very specific wording and dates, so that when deadline time arrives, there is no room for ambiguity."

## Worked Example — Columbus as MBO
Grove's deliberately liberal retelling:

1. **Spain's annual planning process of 1491** concluded that the war against the Moors — the supreme goal of Queen Isabella's government — could not continue without money for weapons and ammunition.
2. **Isabella's objective**: increase Spain's wealth, by dramatically improving the foreign trade balance.
3. **She told her subordinate, Columbus, about her objective.** He went away, thought about ways to achieve it, and returned with **several suggestions** — including a pirate-free passage to England and a new route to the Orient.
4. **Free discussion, then a clear decision** (Ch 5's model): he would look for a new route to the East. **This becomes Columbus's objective, agreed between them.**
5. **Columbus formulated his own key results**: obtain several ships, train crews, conduct a shakedown cruise, set sail — **each with a specific deadline.**
6. **The nesting**: if Columbus meets his objective, Isabella meets hers.

**The instructive twist**: *"the key results can come in like clockwork, but the objectives can still be missed."* Columbus hit his key results relatively easily and **failed his objective — he did not find a new trade route to China.** Yet he discovered the New World, "a source of incalculable wealth for Spain." **So it is entirely possible for a subordinate to perform well and be rated well even though he missed his specified objective.** This is exactly why MBO is an input to a review, not the review itself.

## Worked Example — The Philippine Plant Expansion as MBO
The same decision from Ch 5, expressed in MBO form:

**Far East construction manager — Objective**: *"Obtain decision on Philippine plant expansion."*
**Key results:**
1. Do a study of land availability near the present plant and at other acceptable locations **by June**.
2. Do financial analyses showing the trade-offs between land costs and construction costs, plus operating costs at the two locations.
3. Present the results to the plant location steering group and obtain a decision from them.
4. **Have Grove ratify the decision by October.**

**His supervisor — Objective**: *"Ensure that all plant expansion projects stay on schedule."*
**Supporting key result**: *"Obtain Philippine plant expansion decision by October."*

**What to notice**: (1) the subordinate's **objective** is nearly identical to the supervisor's **key result** — that is the nesting hierarchy made concrete; (2) the objective is deliberately short-range; (3) each key result is so specific that "a person knows without question whether he has completed them and done it on time or not."

## Worked Example — Two Managers Doing Step 3
**Bruce (marketing manager) — a gap that cannot be closed.** Present status: three people against a huge inventory of projects. Desired status: every project completed, since not finishing means significant extra cost and far more effort later. Budget forbids hiring. **He concludes a complete match is impossible** and that the best he can do is converge partially. His actions: (a) move as many **noncritical** tasks as possible to other, less-qualified but less-busy groups; (b) arrange with his manager for a **summer student** on easily definable tasks; (c) **monitor his group's performance tightly**; (d) longer term, split work with similar marketing groups and eliminate duplication; (e) **initiate a request to increase headcount** — where the documented, honest non-convergence *after going the extra mile* becomes the basis of the request.

**Cindy (manufacturing process engineer) — a gap caused by a vendor.** She defines her environment as "objects" (untested new processes and tools) and "influences" (development engineers wanting *less* experimentation and documentation; production engineers wanting *more*; product engineers needing chips out the door). Her customer is the production area; her vendors are the engineering groups. **Present status**: the data and experimentation she needs from development "always came in incomplete" — because completeness and schedules weren't high on the development engineers' priorities. **Desired status**: every new process and machine tested, debugged, demonstrated, and accompanied by the data production engineers now demand. **Her strategy**: specify exactly which steps must complete before any process is implemented or tool deployed; use **time offsets** (Ch 1) to date each step backward from the deadline; **get the development manager to agree to the detailed schedule**, negotiating what each side must do and by when; then **monitor all "vendors" weekly and publish their performance against schedule** — simultaneously an indicator that motivates them to meet key dates and a window into the black box that surfaces problems early.

## Key Takeaways
1. Plan in three steps: demand, present status, reconciliation — and forecast demand without pre-discounting it by your capability.
2. Do difference analysis across two time frames; the *difference* is what new work must exist.
3. State status in the same currency as demand and apply a realistic loss factor to work in flight.
4. Ask "what do I need to do" and "what can I do" separately; the resulting action set is your strategy.
5. The output of planning is the tasks started today, not the document — ask what today's action prevents tomorrow.
6. A five-year plan really commits only the next year; don't replan so often that you never get feedback.
7. Planners must be implementers, and must be willing to kill projects, because every yes spends a no.
8. Run MBO on two questions, keep objectives few, cycle at least quarterly, and nest them so subordinates' objectives roll up.
9. Treat MBO as a stopwatch and an input to review — never a contract, in either direction.
