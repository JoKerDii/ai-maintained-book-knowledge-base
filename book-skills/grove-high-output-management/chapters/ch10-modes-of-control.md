# Chapter 10: Modes of Control

## Core Idea
Work behavior is governed by exactly **three invisible modes of control — free-market forces, contractual obligations, and cultural values** — and the manager's job is to identify which one fits the situation, using the **CUA factor** and the person's motivation to choose.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Three Modes of Control**:
  | Mode | Basis | When it works | Management's role | Overhead |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | **Free-market forces** | **Price.** Goods and services exchanged between two entities, each seeking only to enrich itself. "Neither party here cares if the other goes bankrupt, nor do they pretend to." | Only when what changes hands has a **very clearly defined dollar value** | **None** — "no one supervises sales made at a flea market" | Zero |
  | **Contractual obligations** | Agreed rules defining the kind of work and the standards governing it, plus generalized authority to monitor, evaluate, and correct | When value **cannot** easily be pinned to a price | Setting and modifying rules, monitoring adherence, evaluating and improving performance | Supervisors, policemen, auditors — "with them… we introduce **overhead**" |
  | **Cultural values** | **The interest of the larger group takes precedence over the interest of the individual** | When the environment changes faster than rules can change, or circumstances are so ambiguous that a covering contract would be prohibitively complicated | Developing and nurturing the common values, objectives, and methods that make trust possible | Requires accumulated shared experience |

- **The Three Everyday Illustrations** (Grove's driving sequence, the chapter's spine):
  1. **Buying tires** — you compare dealers and consumer magazines and decide on **self-interest** alone: the tires that meet your needs at the lowest cost. "There's not much chance that you would say to him that he isn't charging you enough."
  2. **Stopping at a red light** — you don't think about it. It's a societal law you unquestioningly accept. **"Vehicular chaos would reign if all drivers had not entered into a contract to stop."** The traffic cop monitors adherence and penalizes breakers.
  3. **Coming upon a major accident** — you forget the law against stopping on a freeway *and* your own self-interest, and expose yourself to danger to help. "What motivates you now is not at all what did when you were shopping for tires or stopping at the red light: not self-interest or obeying the law, but **concern about someone else's life**."

- **Why Free Markets Can't Govern Everything**: value must have a clearly defined dollar figure. When it takes a **group** to accomplish a task, how much does each person contribute to the value added? **"If we bought engineering work by the 'bit,' I think we would end up spending more time trying to decide the value of each bit of contribution than the contribution itself is worth."** So you shift to a contract: "I'll retain your services for a year for a set amount of money, and you will agree to do a certain type of work in return."

- **Why Contracts Require Generalized Authority**: "Because I can't specify in advance exactly what you will do from day to day, I must have a fair amount of generalized authority over your work. So you must give to me as part of the contract **the right to monitor and evaluate and, if necessary, correct** your work."

- **Trust as the Price of Cultural Control**: "some emotionally loaded words come into play — words like **trust** — because you are **surrendering to the group your ability to protect yourself**." For that you must believe you share a common set of **values**, **objectives**, and **methods** — "which, in turn, can only be developed by a great deal of common, shared experience."

- **How Management Builds Cultural Control — Two Levers**:
  1. **Articulation** — spelling out the values, objectives, and methods.
  2. **Example** — "the other, **even more important**, way… If our behavior at work will be regarded as in line with the values we profess, that fosters the development of a group culture."

- **The CUA Factor**: an imaginary composite index of an environment's **Complexity, Uncertainty, and Ambiguity.** Grove's three named cases:
  - **Complexity** — *Cindy*, the process engineer: tricky technologies, new and not fully operational equipment, and development engineers and production engineers pulling her in opposite directions.
  - **Uncertainty** — *Bruce*, the marketing manager: he asked to hire more people for a grossly understaffed group; his supervisor waffles, so he has no idea if he'll get the go-ahead or what to do if he doesn't.
  - **Ambiguity** — *Mike*, an Intel transportation supervisor: so many committees, councils, and divisional manufacturing managers that "he didn't know which, if any, end was up. **He eventually quit**, unable to tolerate the ambiguity of his working environment."

- **The Control Mode Matrix** — two variables: individual **motivation** (self-interest ↔ group-interest) and the environment's **CUA factor** (low ↔ high):
  | | **Low CUA** | **High CUA** |
  |---|---|---|
  | **Self-interest** | **Free-market mode** — buying tires | ❌ **CHAOS** — "no mode of control will work well… like every man for himself on a sinking ship" |
  | **Group-interest** | **Contractual mode** — stopping at a red light | **Cultural values mode** — helping at the accident scene |
  - **The critical cell is the empty one.** High CUA plus self-interest cannot be managed by any mode. The fix is to move one of the two variables: reduce the CUA, or build group-interest.

- **The Career Progression Implied by the Matrix**:
  1. A **new employee**'s motivation is very much self-interest, so **give him a clearly structured job with a low CUA factor.**
  2. If he does well, "he will begin to feel more at home, worry less about himself, and start to care more about his team. He learns that **if he is on a boat and wants to get ahead, it is better for him to help row than to run to the bow.**"
  3. He can then be promoted into a more complex, uncertain, ambiguous job. *(Grove's parenthetical: "These tend to pay more.")*
  4. Accumulating shared experience with peers, supervisors, and subordinates, he learns the organization's values, objectives, and methods and becomes ready for more CUA.
  - **This is why promotion from within is favored by corporations with strong cultures**: bring young people in at low-level, well-defined, low-CUA jobs, and over time "they will gradually accept, even flourish in, the complex world of multiple bosses and peer decision-making."

- **The Senior Outside Hire Problem** — the matrix's warning. A senior external hire arrives, like anyone, with **high self-interest**. But "inevitably we will give her an organization to manage that is **in trouble; after all, that was our reason for going outside.**" So her working environment has a **very high CUA**, and she has **no base of common experience** with the organization and **no knowledge of its methods**. That places her precisely in the chaos quadrant. **"All we can do is cross our fingers and hope she quickly forgets self-interest and just as quickly gets on top of her job to reduce her CUA factor. Short of that, she's probably out of luck."**

- **Expectations Are as Binding as Contracts**: Intel's sales-force training program isn't a mandated requirement spelled out in any formal policy statement, yet salespeople **expect** each division to provide training regularly. **"The point is, expectations can be as binding as a legal document."**

## Key Concepts
- **CUA factor** — Complexity, Uncertainty, Ambiguity; the environmental variable that determines which control mode fits.
- **Overhead as the cost of contractual control** — supervisors, policemen, tax auditors, a Public Utilities Commission.
- **Shared experience** — the only input that produces the common values, objectives, and methods cultural control needs.
- **Articulation vs. example** — the two levers of culture-building, with example the stronger.

## Mental Models
- **Ask two questions before choosing how to manage something**: is this person's motivation self- or group-oriented, and how complex/uncertain/ambiguous is the environment? The answer picks the mode.
- **Don't idealize culture.** "There is a temptation to idealize what I've called cultural values as a mode of control because it is so 'nice,' even utopian… But this is not the most efficient mode of control under all conditions. **It is no guide to buying tires, nor could the tax system work this way.**"
- **All three modes operate on the same person on the same day.**
- **Match CUA to the person, then raise it.** Careers are a controlled escalation of ambiguity tolerance.
- **If you're in the chaos quadrant, change a variable — don't try harder.** Either simplify the environment or invest in shared experience.

## Anti-patterns
- **Using cultural appeals where a price exists**: inefficient and unnecessary.
- **Using market mechanisms where value can't be priced**: paying engineering "by the bit" costs more in valuation than the work is worth.
- **Putting a self-interested newcomer into a high-CUA job**: the chaos quadrant, which is how the senior-outside-hire failure mode is manufactured.
- **Building incentives that then get blamed on the people who respond to them** — the sales-contest case below.
- **Waffling supervision**: Bruce's uncertainty comes from a supervisor who won't decide (see Ch 3 on waffling as negative leverage).
- **Unmanaged ambiguity**: Mike quit — ambiguity is a real, quantifiable cost, not a personality problem.

## Worked Example — All Three Modes in One Day, and One Project
**Bob, a marketing supervisor, in a single day:**
- **Free-market**: buying lunch in the cafeteria — choices are well defined, based on what he wants to buy and what he wants to pay.
- **Contractual**: coming to work at all — a set salary for doing his best, "which implies that he has to show up."
- **Cultural**: participating in strategic planning — "work outside of his 'regular' job as defined contractually, and so represents extra effort for him. But he does it because **he feels the company needs what he has to contribute.**"

**Barbara's sales-training project, in a single workstream:**
- **Free-market**: buying training materials — binders of the required quality at the lowest possible price.
- **Contractual**: the training program's existence — salespeople *expect* regular training from each division. Not written in any policy statement, yet binding.
- **Cultural**: coordinating messages across divisions that share a common sales force. Each division has a vested interest in promoting its own products; **"unless the divisions are willing to sacrifice self-interest in favor of the common interest, the training sessions can easily become disjointed free-for-alls and confuse everybody."**

## Worked Example — Who Created the Monster?
**The complaint**: a group of factory marketing managers claimed Intel's salespeople were "governed only by self-interest" — devoting attention to items producing the most commissions and bonuses. The managers were "irritated and a bit self-righteous," feeling they were far more concerned with the common good than their field colleagues.

**Grove's verdict**: **"But the marketing departments themselves created the monster."** To get the sales force to favor particular products, divisions had for some time run contests with prizes "ranging from cash bonuses to trips to exotic places." **"The marketing managers were competing against one another for a finite and valuable resource: the salesmen's time. And the salesmen merely responded as one might expect."**

**The counter-case, same population**: one Intel division had serious problems, leaving its sales engineers **with no product to sell for nearly a year**. "They could have left Intel and immediately gotten other jobs and quick commissions elsewhere, but by and large they stayed with us. They stayed because they **believed in the company and had faith that eventually things would get better.**" Grove's conclusion: **"Belief and faith are not aspects of the market mode, but stem from adherence to cultural values."**

**The transferable lesson**: the same people behave under market control or cultural control depending on which mode management installed. If you built a market inside your organization, don't moralize when people optimize it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Three modes govern all work behavior: market, contract, culture. Name which one you're relying on.
2. Free markets need clearly priceable value and no supervision; contracts need rules, monitoring authority, and overhead; culture needs trust built from shared experience.
3. Culture is not universally superior — it cannot govern tire purchases or a tax system.
4. Score the environment's Complexity, Uncertainty, and Ambiguity, and read the mode off the matrix.
5. High CUA plus self-interest is chaos, manageable only by lowering CUA or raising group-interest.
6. Give new people low-CUA, well-structured work; escalate CUA as shared experience accumulates. This is the mechanism behind promote-from-within.
7. Treat the senior outside hire into a troubled organization as a structurally high-risk placement and act to lower their CUA fast.
8. Build culture by articulation and, more powerfully, by example.
9. Unstated expectations bind as tightly as contracts.
10. Behavior follows the mode you installed — audit your incentives before blaming the people responding to them.
