# Chapter 12: Task-Relevant Maturity

## Core Idea
**There is no optimal management style.** The right style is determined by the subordinate's **task-relevant maturity (TRM)** for *that specific task in that specific environment* — and TRM can collapse overnight when the task or pace changes.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Task-Relevant Maturity (TRM)**: "a combination of the degree of their **achievement orientation and readiness to take responsibility**, as well as their **education, training, and experience**. Moreover, all this is **very specific to the task at hand**, and it is entirely possible for a person or a group of people to have a TRM that is **high in one job but low in another**."

- **The TRM → Style Table** (the chapter's central artifact):
  | **TRM of subordinate** | **Characteristics of the effective management style** |
  |---|---|
  | **Low** | Structured; task-oriented; tell **"what," "when," "how"** |
  | **Medium** | Individual-oriented; emphasis on **two-way communication, support, mutual reasoning** |
  | **High** | Involvement by manager **minimal**: establishing objectives and monitoring |
  - **The invariant across all three rows**: "regardless of what the TRM may be, **the manager should always monitor a subordinate's work closely enough to avoid surprises.** The presence or absence of monitoring… is the difference between a supervisor's **delegating** a task and **abdicating** it."
  - **The mandatory caveat**: "**do not make a value judgment and consider a structured management style less worthy than a communication-oriented one.** What is 'nice' or 'not nice' should have no place in how you think or what you do. Remember, we are after what is most **effective**."

- **Why No Optimal Style Exists — Two Lines of Evidence**:
  1. **The research record**: management style historically tracked the motivation theory in fashion — turn-of-the-century "do it or you're fired" produced crisp hierarchy; the 1950s humanistic shift produced a nicer style. When behavioral science departments finally ran carefully controlled experiments, **"none of the early intuitive presumptions could be borne out: the hard findings simply would not show that one style of leadership was better than another."**
  2. **Grove's own natural experiment**: Intel frequently rotates middle managers between groups similar in background and work type but varying greatly in output. **"Neither the managers nor the groups maintain the characteristic of being either high-producing or low-producing as the managers are switched around. The inevitable conclusion is that high output is associated with particular *combinations* of certain managers and certain groups of workers."**

- **TRM Collapses When the Environment Changes**: "a person's TRM can be very high given a certain level of complexity, uncertainty, and ambiguity, but **if the pace of the job accelerates or if the job itself abruptly changes, the TRM of that individual will drop.**"
  - **The driving analogy**: "a person with many years' experience driving on small country roads being suddenly asked to drive on a crowded metropolitan freeway. **His TRM driving his own car will drop precipitously.**"

- **The Parenting Parallel** — style tracks "life-relevant maturity" (age):
  | Stage | Parental style | Managerial equivalent |
  |---|---|---|
  | Toddler | Tell him not to touch things. "The child cannot understand that the vase he wants to play with is an irreplaceable heirloom, but he can understand **'no.'**" | Structured / what-when-how |
  | First two-wheeler | Doesn't simply send him out alone — **accompanies him to keep the bicycle from tipping over while talking about street safety** | Communicating / mutual reasoning |
  | Older child riding alone | "The parent no longer has to recite the litany of safety rules" | Reduced instruction |
  | Leaves for college | "The parent merely **monitors** the child's progress" | Objectives + monitoring |
  | **Severe academic trouble** | **"the parent may have to revert to a style used earlier"** | Revert on environment change |

- **Structure Moves From External to Internal**: as supervision moves from structured → communicating → monitoring, **the degree of structure governing behavior does not actually change.** "A teenager **knows** it is not safe to cross a busy interstate highway on his bicycle, and the parent no longer has to tell him not to do it. **Structure moves from being *externally imposed* to being *internally given*.**"
  - **The precondition**: "If the parent (or supervisor) imparted early on… the right way to do things (**the correct operational values**), later the child would be likely to make decisions the way the parent would. In fact, **commonality of operational values, priorities, and preferences — how an organization works together — is a must if the progression in managerial style is to occur.**"
  - **Whose job it is**: "Without that commonality, an organization can become easily confused and lose its sense of purpose. Accordingly, **the responsibility for transmitting common values rests squarely with the supervisor.** He is, after all, accountable for the output of the people who report to him; then, too, **without a shared set of values a supervisor cannot effectively delegate.**"

- **The Tuition Principle**: an associate of Grove's, who had always done outstanding work, hired a junior person to take over old tasks. The subordinate did poor work. **The associate's reaction: "He has to make his own mistakes. That's how he learns!"**
  - **Grove's verdict**: "The problem with this is that **the subordinate's tuition is paid by his customers. And that is absolutely wrong.** The responsibility for teaching the subordinate must be assumed by his supervisor, and **not paid for by the customers of his organization, internal or external.**"

- **Why Raising TRM Is High Leverage** — three pragmatic reasons to accelerate it:
  1. The style appropriate to high TRM **takes less of your time** than detailed structured supervision.
  2. Once operational values are learned and TRM is high enough, **you can delegate — increasing managerial leverage.**
  3. At the highest TRM the subordinate's training is presumably complete and **"motivation is likely to come from within, from self-actualization, which is the most powerful source of energy and effort a manager can harness."**

- **Reverting Is a Skill, Not a Failure**: "a manager's ability to operate in a style based on communication and mutual understanding **depends on there being enough time for it.** Though monitoring is on paper a manager's most productive approach, **we have to work our way up to it in the real world.** Even if we achieve it, if things suddenly change we have to revert quickly to the what-when-how mode."
  - **The prejudice that costs you**: "That mode is one that we don't think an enlightened manager should use. As a result, **we often don't take it up until it is too late and events overwhelm us.** We managers must learn to fight such prejudices and regard any management mode not as either good or bad but rather as **effective or not effective**… **This is why researchers cannot find the single best way for a manager to work. It changes day by day and sometimes hour by hour.**"

- **The Two Self-Perception Traps**:
  1. **Personal preference overrides the correct choice.** "Even if a manager sees that his subordinate's TRM is 'medium,' in the real world the manager will likely opt for either the 'structured' or 'minimal' style. In other words, **we want either to be fully immersed in the work of our subordinates, making their decisions, or to leave them completely alone, not wanting to be bothered.**" The medium row is the one managers skip.
  2. **We overrate our own delegation.** Grove asked a group of managers to assess their supervisors' style, then asked those supervisors what they thought their own style was. **"Some 90 percent of the supervisors saw their style as more communicating or delegating than their subordinates' view."** Two causes: managers "think of themselves as perfect delegators," and **"sometimes a manager throws out suggestions to a subordinate who receives them as marching orders."**

- **Social Relationship ≠ Communicating Style**: a manager told Grove his supervisor definitely practiced an effective communicating style "because they skied and drank together." **"He was wrong. There is a huge distinction between a social relationship and a communicating *management* style, which is a caring involvement in the *work* of the subordinate."**
  - **The counter-case**: two people in a supervisor-subordinate relationship spent one week each year fishing together in a remote area — but **never talked about work, "it being tacitly understood that work was off conversational limits." "Oddly enough, their work relationship remained distant, their personal friendship having no effect on it."**

- **The Friendship Test**: on whether supervisor-subordinate friendship is a good thing, Grove refuses a blanket answer and gives a decision rule.
  - **The plus**: "If the subordinate is a personal friend, the supervisor can move into a communicating management style quite easily."
  - **The minus**: "the what-when-how mode becomes harder to revert to when necessary. **It's unpleasant to give orders to a friend.**" Grove has seen a supervisor make a subordinate-friend toe a disciplinary line: "In one case, **a friendship was destroyed**; in another, the supervisor's action worked out because the subordinate felt, thanks to the strength of the social relationship, that the supervisor was looking out for his professional interests."
  - **The test**: **"Imagine yourself delivering a tough performance review to your friend. Do you cringe at the thought? If so, don't make friends at work. If your stomach remains unaffected, you are likely to be someone whose personal relationships will strengthen work relationships."**

## Key Concepts
- **TRM** — task-specific readiness, not general competence or seniority.
- **Operational values** — the shared sense of how things are done that lets structure become internal.
- **Externally imposed vs. internally given structure** — what actually changes as style relaxes.
- **Delegation vs. abdication** — separated solely by monitoring, at every TRM level.
- **Tuition paid by customers** — the hidden cost of "let him make his own mistakes."

## Mental Models
- **Style is a function of two arguments, not one**: the person *and* the task. Rotate either and recompute.
- **High output lives in combinations**, not in managers or in groups — which is why rotating a star manager doesn't transfer the star performance.
- **A promotion is a TRM reset.** The person didn't change; their TRM for the new job did.
- **The what-when-how mode is a tool, not a moral failing.** Prejudice against it is what makes managers reach for it too late.
- **Never skip the middle row.** The medium-TRM communicating style is the one personal preference will push you past in both directions.

## Anti-patterns
- **Confusing general competence with task-relevant maturity**: Grove's own admitted error below.
- **Judging styles as "nice" vs. "not nice"** instead of effective vs. ineffective.
- **Abdicating instead of delegating**: dropping monitoring at high TRM.
- **"He has to make his own mistakes"**: charges the subordinate's tuition to customers.
- **Waiting until events overwhelm you** before reverting to structured direction.
- **Believing your own delegation rating**: 90% of supervisors overrate it relative to their subordinates' view.
- **Throwing out casual suggestions** that land as marching orders.
- **Mistaking skiing and drinking together for a communicating management style.**
- **Making friends at work when you'd cringe at delivering a tough review to them.**

## Worked Example — The Sales Manager Who Moved Into the Plant
Intel moved "an extremely productive sales manager from the field into the plant, where he was placed in charge of a factory unit. **The size and scope of the two jobs were comparable**, yet the performance of the seasoned manager **deteriorated, and he started to show the signs of someone overwhelmed by his work.**"

**The diagnosis**: "while the **personal maturity** of the manager obviously did not change, his **task-relevant maturity in the new job was extremely low**, since its environment, content, and tasks were all new to him."

**The recovery**: "In time he learned to cope, and **his TRM gradually increased.** With that, his performance began to approach the outstanding levels he had exhibited earlier, which was why we promoted him in the first place."

**Grove's admission**: **"What happened here should have been totally predictable, yet we were surprised: we confused the manager's general competence and maturity with his task-relevant maturity."**

**The practical implication**: every promotion or lateral move is a TRM reset. The correct response is to *revert to a structured style temporarily* — which the manager's seniority makes psychologically hard for both parties.

## Worked Example — The Sergeant and the Jeep
An army encampment where nothing ever happens:
1. **Steady state, high TRM.** "The sergeant in command has come to know each of his soldiers very well, and by and large maintains an **informal relationship** with them. The routines are so well established that he rarely has to tell anyone what to do; appropriate to the high TRM of the group, **the sergeant contents himself with merely monitoring their activity.**"
2. **The environment changes in an instant.** "One day a jeepload of the enemy suddenly appears, coming over the hill and shooting at the camp. **Instantly the sergeant reverts to a structured, task-oriented leadership style, barking orders at everyone, telling each of his soldiers what to do, when, and how.**"
3. **The new task becomes routine.** "After a while, if these skirmishes continue and the group keeps on fighting from the same place for a couple of months, this too will eventually become routine. With that, **the TRM of the group for the new task — fighting — will increase.** The sergeant can then gradually ease off telling everybody what to do."

**What the example establishes**: (1) TRM is per-task, so a group with maximum TRM at camp routine has near-zero TRM at combat; (2) the *speed* of reversion matters — the sergeant switches instantly, not after deliberation; (3) reversion is temporary and self-correcting as the new task's TRM builds; (4) the informal relationship built in peacetime is not what fails — the *style* has to change while the relationship persists.

## Key Takeaways
1. No management style is universally best; the effective one is set by the subordinate's TRM for the specific task and environment.
2. TRM is task-specific — high in one job, low in another — and is not the same as general competence, age, or seniority.
3. Use the three-row table: low → what/when/how; medium → two-way communication and mutual reasoning; high → objectives and monitoring.
4. Monitor at every level; that alone separates delegating from abdicating.
5. TRM drops when the task changes or the pace accelerates — revert fast and without embarrassment.
6. Style relaxation works only because structure moves from external to internal, which requires the supervisor to have transmitted operational values.
7. Teaching the subordinate is the supervisor's job; letting customers fund the learning is absolutely wrong.
8. Raise TRM deliberately — it saves your time, enables delegation, and hands the person over to self-actualization.
9. Watch for the two self-perception traps: skipping the medium row, and overrating your own delegation.
10. Social closeness is not a communicating style; apply the tough-review test before befriending subordinates.
