# Chapter 5: Decisions, Decisions

## Core Idea
In a know-how business, **position power and knowledge power diverge**, so decisions must be made by mixed groups running a three-stage process: **free discussion → clear decision → full support** — with the six framing questions settled *before* the fact.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Knowledge Power vs. Position Power**: in traditional industries, authority to decide went with position in the hierarchy. In information/know-how businesses the two diverge rapidly, because the knowledge base changes fast.
  - The mechanism: a technical graduate is fully current on joining and holds real **knowledge power**. If he does well he is promoted, so **position power grows while intimate familiarity with current technology fades**. "At Intel, anyway, we managers get a little more obsolete every day."
  - The faster the know-how or customer preferences change, the wider the divergence.
  - **The middle manager is the key**: not just a link in the chain of command but the person who makes the holders of the two kinds of power **mesh smoothly**.

- **The Ideal Decision-Making Model** — three stages:
  1. **Free discussion** — all points of view and all aspects openly welcomed and debated. **"The greater the disagreement and controversy, the more important becomes the word *free*."**
  2. **Clear decision** — **"the greater the disagreement about the issue, the more important becomes the word *clear*."** Take particular pains to frame the terms with utter clarity. The instinct to obscure a controversial decision doesn't avoid the argument, it merely postpones it: "People who don't like a decision will be a lot madder if they don't get a prompt and straight story about it."
  3. **Full support** — **support is not agreement.** So long as participants commit to back the decision, that is a satisfactory outcome. "An organization does not live by its members agreeing with one another at all times about everything. It lives instead by people committing to support the decisions and the moves of the business." All a manager can expect — and must get from everyone — is that the commitment to support is honestly present.

- **The Lowest Competent Level**: work out and reach each decision at the lowest level competent to make it, because those people are closest to the situation and know the most.
  - But "know" means more than technical understanding. Expertise **must be tempered with judgment, developed through experience and learning from errors**. Decisions should occur in the middle ground between technical knowledge and "the bruises one has received from having tried to implement and apply such knowledge."
  - If no one has both, **assemble the best possible mix** — Intel adds a more senior manager for experience.
  - **Critical condition**: during free discussion everybody must voice opinions **as equals**, forgetting or ignoring status differentials.

- **The Six Questions** — settle these *in advance*, before the decision-making starts:
  1. **What decision needs to be made?**
  2. **When does it have to be made?**
  3. **Who will decide?**
  4. **Who will need to be consulted prior to making the decision?**
  5. **Who will ratify or veto the decision?**
  6. **Who will need to be informed of the decision?**
  - Beyond expediting the decision, this **prevents the late veto**. Someone with say-so who overturns a decision afterward "can be regarded as a Johnny-come-lately who upsets the decision-making applecart," frustrating and demoralizing people who worked on it for months. A surprise veto, however legitimate on the merits, "inevitably creates an impression of political maneuvering." **Politics and manipulation, or even their appearance, should be avoided at all costs** — and the six questions applied before the fact are the best defense.

- **The Peer-Group Syndrome** and the **Peer-Plus-One Approach**: a group of organizational equals asked to decide will go in circles without noticing.
  - Discovered at Intel's very first management training session: peers were given a live issue with the chairman (one level higher) deliberately sent out of the room. They "did nothing but go around in circles for some fifteen minutes, and none of them noticed they weren't getting anywhere." The chairman returned, listened, leaned forward, "a black cloud form[ed] over his head," and he slapped the table: *"What's going on here? You people are talking in circles and getting nowhere."* The problem was then resolved in very short order.
  - **The remedy**: peers need a **peer-plus-one** — a more senior person, "even if he is not the most competent or knowledgeable person involved," to take over and shape the meeting. He acts as **a godfather, a repository of knowledge about how decisions should be made**, giving the group the confidence to decide.
  - **Escalation order** when no formal chairman exists: (1) the person with the **most at stake** takes charge; (2) failing that, ask the **senior person present** to assume control.

- **The Two Withholding Dynamics** (both produce the same failure — people not speaking their minds):
  | | Wait-for-the-boss | Wait-for-consensus |
  |---|---|---|
  | Source | Culture rewards echoing the superior | Fear of differing from the group |
  | Grove's evidence | A fired auto executive was told: *"Bill, in general, people who do well in this company wait until they hear their superiors express their view and then contribute something in support of that view."* | An Intel engineer, John: the group "wanders around for a while, feeling each other out, waiting for a consensus to develop before anyone risks taking a position." Someone then states it as a **group** opinion ("I think *our* position seems to be…"), not a personal one; if the rest buy in, it solidifies and is restated forcefully. |
  | Result | Decisions based on less complete information and insight than were available | Same |

- **The Three Fears That Suppress Input**:
  - **Fear of sounding dumb** — paralyzes *both* knowledge and position holders. It keeps the senior person from asking the questions he should ask, and makes others think their thoughts privately or whisper them to a neighbor. **"Each time an insight or fact is withheld and an appropriate question is suppressed, the decision-making process is less good than it might have been."**
  - **Fear of being overruled** — for junior people, being vetoed by a senior manager means losing face in front of peers. Grove: this, "even more than fear of sanctions or even of the loss of job," makes junior people hang back.
  - **Genuine uncertainty** — some issues are complex enough that participants honestly don't know how they feel. Knowledge people say "we don't know what the company wants of us"; position people know they lack the technical detail. The antidote is not certainty but will: "We are all human beings endowed with intelligence and blessed with willpower."
  - **The cure for peer-group paralysis**: self-confidence — partly from familiarity and experience, but "in the end self-confidence mostly comes from a gut-level realization that **nobody has ever died from making a wrong business decision**, or taking inappropriate action, or being overruled. And everyone in your operation should be made to understand this."

- **When Position Power Is Legitimate**: if no consensus emerges and the time for a decision has arrived, the senior person **must** decide.
  - **Legitimate — in fact sometimes unavoidable** — to wield position-power authority *once the clear-decision stage is reached and no consensus has developed*.
  - **Not legitimate — in fact destructive** — to wield it any earlier.
  - The American reluctance to give orders ("it is just 'not nice'") is itself a failure mode: it prolongs free discussion past the optimum point and the decision gets put off.

- **The Timing Criterion**: "**Don't push for a decision prematurely.** Make sure you have heard and considered the real issues rather than the superficial comments that often dominate the early part of a meeting. But if you feel that you have already heard everything, that all sides of the issue have been raised, it is time to push for a consensus — and failing that, to step in and make a decision."
  - The specific danger of overrunning: "free discussion goes on in an unending search for consensus… people can drift away from the near consensus when they are close to being right, diminishing the chances of reaching the correct decision."

## Key Concepts
- **Peer-plus-one** — adding one hierarchical level above a peer group so decisions actually close.
- **Lowest competent level** — the level combining nearness to the facts with judgment earned from errors.
- **Support vs. agreement** — the distinction that makes group decision-making survivable.
- **Godfather role** — the senior person as repository of *how* decisions get made, not necessarily *what* is right.
- **Egalitarian signals as necessity** — informal dress, partitions instead of offices, no reserved parking. Asked by a journalist whether this was "just so much affectation," Grove: **"this is not affectation but a matter of survival."** Knowledge people and position people must mix daily; **"status symbols most certainly do not promote the flow of ideas, facts, and points of view. What appears to be a matter of style really is a matter of necessity."**

## Mental Models
- **Managers get more obsolete every day.** Design the decision process around that fact instead of pretending otherwise.
- **Nobody ever died from a wrong business decision.** The realization that unlocks candor.
- **Support is a commitment, not a feeling.** Ask for the behavior, not the belief.
- **Two interest groups need roughly equal representation** at the table — "only from such balance will an even-handed decision emerge."
- **A vetoed decision that surprises people looks like politics**, no matter how right it is.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rewarding echoing of the boss**: "This is a terrible way to manage. All it produces is bad decisions."
- **Being mealy-mouthed about a controversial decision**: postpones the argument and makes people angrier.
- **Wielding position power during free discussion**: destructive; it prejudices the input you need.
- **Refusing to wield position power at the decision stage**: prolongs discussion and delays the decision.
- **Searching endlessly for consensus**: people drift away from a near-consensus that was close to right.
- **The late veto**: demoralizes the team and creates the appearance of political maneuvering.
- **Announcing a shock decision and walking away**: instead, adjourn, let people "adjust, rationalize, and in general put their heads back together," reconvene, and solicit their views then.
- **The "John Wayne" mentality**: the Intel middle manager from a top business school who quit, frustrated with group decision-making, for a company that let individuals decide and implement alone. **He came back four months later, explaining that "if he could make decisions without consulting anybody, so could everybody else."**
- **Middle managers' three specific difficulties** with the model (Grove notes it comes easily only to long-tenured senior managers and new graduates, who used it in lab teams): expressing views forcefully, making unpleasant decisions, and supporting a decision they don't agree with.

## Worked Example — The Philippine Plant Location Decision
**The situation**: Intel had decided to roughly double its Philippine manufacturing capacity. Where?
- *Option A — adjacent lot*: shared overhead and communications, near-zero transport cost between plants, easy employee transfer. But limited space meant a **high-rise**, and a high-rise semiconductor plant is not efficient.
- *Option B — outlying lot*: cheaper and more plentiful land, allowing an inexpensive one- or two-story building.

**The six questions applied**:
1. **What decision?** Multistory building next to the existing plant, or a one-/two-story building at a new outlying location.
2. **When?** Long-range plans required the plant in 2–2.5 years; applying **time offsets** (Ch 1), the decision had to be made **within a month**.
3. **Who will decide?** Not obvious — and deliberately balanced. Facilities/construction people are sensitive to construction cost and difficulty and would lean toward the new location; plant management knows the operational benefits of adjacency and would lean toward the high-rise. So the body was **parallel levels from both organizations**: the Far East construction manager and his supervisor (corporate construction manager), plus the Far East plant network manager and his supervisor (senior manufacturing manager). "It is important to give to the two sides **roughly equal representation**, because only from such balance will an even-handed decision emerge."
4. **Who consulted?** Each participant had consulted their own staff and gathered all relevant knowledge and views beforehand.
5. **Who ratifies or vetoes?** Grove — the first common person to whom both organizations' senior managers report, it was big enough to involve the president, and he was somewhat familiar with the Philippine locations and how such a plant operates.
6. **Who is informed?** Gordon Moore, chairman of the board — not directly involved with such plants, "but we don't build a new one in the Far East every day, so he should know."

**The outcome**: after studying maps, construction plans and costs, land costs, and traffic patterns, the group chose to **build next to the existing plant but cap it at four stories** — beyond that, cost escalated. They presented alternatives and reasoning at the meeting whose agenda appears in Ch 4. Grove **probed both the group's information and its thinking process** with a series of questions, then ratified. He then informed Moore.

**The transferable move**: the ratifier's job is not to redo the analysis but to **test the quality of the thinking** — the same technique as delegating a decision by monitoring the decision-making process (Ch 3).

## Key Takeaways
1. In know-how businesses, knowledge power and position power separate; the middle manager's job is to mesh them.
2. Run every significant decision through free discussion → clear decision → full support, and note that *free* and *clear* matter most exactly where disagreement is greatest.
3. Demand support, not agreement — and get it honestly from everyone.
4. Decide at the lowest competent level, blending technical knowledge with judgment earned from mistakes.
5. Answer the six questions before the process starts; this is the antidote to the surprise veto and the appearance of politics.
6. Expect the peer-group syndrome; supply a peer-plus-one, or have the person with the most at stake take charge.
7. Teach people that nobody died from a wrong business decision — candor depends on it.
8. Withhold position power during discussion, then use it decisively if no consensus arrives on time.
9. Strip status symbols not as style but as a survival requirement for information flow.
