# Chapter 4: Meetings — The Medium of Managerial Work

## Core Idea
A meeting is not an activity — it is the **medium** through which managerial work is performed. Stop fighting their existence and instead push work out of costly ad hoc meetings into well-run regular ones.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Process-Oriented vs. Mission-Oriented Meetings**:
  | | Process-oriented | Mission-oriented |
  |---|---|---|
  | Purpose | Share knowledge, exchange information | Solve a specific problem |
  | Schedule | Regularly scheduled | Ad hoc, can't be scheduled far ahead |
  | Output | Understanding, alignment | Usually a **decision** |
  | Types | One-on-one, staff meeting, operation review | Any called-for-purpose meeting |
  - The goal: **regular meetings should absorb ~80% of problems and issues; only ~20% should need ad hoc meetings.**

- **Grove's Malorganization Test** (a deliberate revision of Drucker): Drucker said spending more than 25% of time in meetings signals malorganization. Grove: **"the real sign of malorganization is when people spend more than 25 percent of their time in ad hoc mission-oriented meetings."** Total meeting time is not the diagnostic — the *ad hoc share* is.

- **The One-on-One** — a meeting between supervisor and subordinate; "the principal way their business relationship is maintained." Main purpose: **mutual teaching and exchange of information.**
  - **Frequency**: set by the subordinate's **task-relevant maturity** on the specific task — weekly for someone inexperienced in a situation, once every few weeks for an experienced veteran. Also adjust for the pace of change in the job area (marketing may need frequent ones; research may not).
  - **Duration**: **an hour at a minimum.** "Anything less tends to make the subordinate confine himself to simple things that can be handled quickly."
  - **Location**: in or near the **subordinate's** work area. You learn a great deal by going there — is he organized? does he lose documents? is he constantly interrupted, or never?
  - **Ownership**: it is **the subordinate's meeting**, with agenda and tone set by him. The reason is arithmetic: a supervisor with eight subordinates would otherwise prepare eight times; the subordinate prepares once. The subordinate produces a written **outline** in advance (which forces him to think through his issues) and **walks the supervisor through the material**.
  - **Content**: performance indicators — *emphasizing those that signal trouble*; anything important since the last meeting; hiring, people, and organizational problems; future plans; and **very, very important — potential problems**. "Even if it's only an intuition that something's wrong, a subordinate owes it to his supervisor to tell him, because it triggers a look into the organizational black box." The governing criterion: **the issues that preoccupy and nag the subordinate**.
  - **Supervisor's role**: facilitate, learn, and coach. Drucker, quoted approvingly: *"The good time users among managers do not talk to their subordinates about their problems but they know how to make the subordinates talk about theirs."*

- **Grove's Principle of Didactic Management — "Ask one more question!"** When the supervisor thinks the subordinate has said all he wants on a subject, ask another question. Keep prompting until **both** feel they've reached the bottom of the problem.

- **One-on-One Mechanics**:
  - Both parties hold a copy of the outline and **both take notes on it**. Grove takes notes almost always and rarely rereads them — the point is to stop his mind drifting and to force logical categorization that aids absorption. More important is what writing it down **symbolizes**: a note taken right after a suggestion "implies a commitment, like a handshake." The supervisor's own notes let him follow up next time.
  - Keep a **"hold" file** — both parties accumulate important-but-not-urgent items for the next meeting. This is batching applied to the relationship, and it directly reduces phone calls and drop-in interruptions.
  - Encourage **heart-to-heart issues** — satisfaction with own performance, gnawing frustrations, doubts about direction. The one-on-one is the perfect forum.
  - Beware the **"zinger"**: a heart-to-heart issue raised at an awkward time, usually near the end, leaving five minutes to handle "I'm unhappy and have been looking outside for a job."
  - **Long-distance one-on-ones** work with preparation: supervisor must have the outline beforehand, both take notes, and since note-taking can't be observed, **exchange notes afterward** to confirm what each committed to.
  - **Schedule on a rolling basis** — book the next one as this one ends, so vacations and conflicts don't silently kill the meeting the way a fixed "every second Wednesday" does.
  - **Leverage**: 90 minutes every two weeks can raise the quality of a subordinate's work across ~80+ hours *and* upgrade your understanding of what they're doing.

- **The Staff Meeting** — supervisor plus all subordinates; the venue for **peer interaction**, which is difficult but "key to good management" and the precondition for both group decision-making (Ch 5) and dual reporting (Ch 9).
  - **What to discuss**: anything affecting **more than two** of the people present. If it degenerates into a two-person conversation, the supervisor breaks it off and moves on, suggesting they continue later.
  - **Structure**: mostly controlled, with an **agenda issued far enough in advance** for people to prepare — plus a designated **"open session"** for anything staff want to raise, where housekeeping is disposed of and important issues get a tentative first look (possibly earning scheduled time at a future meeting).
  - **Supervisor's role**: leader, observer, expediter, questioner, decision-maker — **all of them. Lecturer is not on the list.** "A supervisor should never use staff meetings to pontificate, which is the surest way to undermine free discussion." Most important roles: **moderator, facilitator, and controller of pace and thrust**, keeping things on track while subordinates bear the brunt of working the issues.
  - **Why it teaches the supervisor**: "I get a much better understanding of an issue with which I am not familiar by listening to two people with opposing views discuss it than I do by listening to one side only."
  - **The family-dinner analogy**: a staff meeting is like dinner-table conversation — formal and informal authority is settled, everyone knows who spouts off and who daydreams. Other forums are "a group of strangers having to make a decision together," which is why staff meetings are an ideal medium for decisions.

- **The Operation Review** — formal presentations by managers to managers who are *not* their immediate supervisors and to peers elsewhere in the company. **Purpose: keep teaching and learning flowing between employees several organizational levels apart** who share no one-on-ones or staff meetings. The junior gains senior comment and criticism; the senior gains a feel for problems from people who know the details; and it motivates, since presenters want to impress their supervisor's supervisor and outside peers.

- **The Four Operation-Review Roles**:
  - **Organizing manager** (supervisor of the presenters): decides with presenters what to cover, what to emphasize, and what level of detail; handles housekeeping (room, visual materials, invitations); acts as **timekeeper**, pacing presenters with **inconspicuous gestures** so no one runs out of time with half their points uncovered.
  - **Reviewing manager** (the senior at whom the review is aimed): asks questions, makes comments, **imparts the appropriate spirit** — the catalyst provoking audience participation, encouraging free expression by example. **Should never preview the material**, since that prevents spontaneous reaction. As a role model for junior managers, must take the role extremely seriously.
  - **Presenters**: use visual aids — "people are endowed with eyes as well as ears." **Rule of thumb: four minutes of presentation and discussion time per visual aid.** Highlight what matters with a color pen or pointer. **Watch the audience like a hawk** — facial expressions and body language tell you to repeat, slow down, or speed up.
  - **Audience**: participation by questions and comments is "one of the distinguishing marks of a good meeting." Avoiding the presenter's eyes, yawning, or reading the newspaper "is worse than not being there at all" — lack of interest undermines the presenter's confidence. Jot down things to try, ask when unclear, speak up when you disagree, and **go on record when a presenter makes a factual error**. "You are being paid to attend… Regard attendance for what it is: work."

- **Chairing a Mission-Oriented Meeting** — "the key to success here is what the chairman does… most of what he contributes should occur **before** it begins." Often no one holds the title, but one person has more at stake and is the de facto chairman. **"When a mission-oriented meeting fails to accomplish the purpose for which it was called, the blame belongs to the chairman."**
  - **Before calling it**: know the objective — what needs to happen, what decision must be made. **"If you don't know what you want, you won't get it."** Then ask: is a meeting necessary? desirable? justifiable? **Don't call it unless all the answers are yes.**
  - **Attendance**: identify who must attend and *get commitments* — don't ask and hope. If someone can't come, ensure they **send a person with the power to speak for them**.
  - **Size cap**: hard to keep moving beyond six or seven; **eight is the absolute cutoff. "Decision-making is not a spectator sport, because onlookers get in the way."**
  - **Discipline**: "It is criminal for him to allow people to be late and waste everyone's time." Confront the late arriver — "just as you would not permit a fellow employee to steal a piece of office equipment worth $2,000, you shouldn't let anyone walk away with the time of his fellow managers."
  - **Logistics**: equipment present; an agenda sent out stating the purpose **and what role each person is expected to play**.
  - **After**: send **minutes** quickly, before people forget — summarizing the discussion, the decision, and the actions, stating clearly **what is to be done, who is to do it, and when**. "A small additional investment (an activity with high leverage)."

- **The $100/Hour Meeting Cost Rule**: estimate a manager's time, including overhead, at **~$100 per hour**. Ten managers for two hours = **$2,000**. Most $2,000 expenditures need senior approval (a copier, a transatlantic trip) "yet a manager can call a meeting and commit $2,000 worth of managerial resources at a whim."
  - **The invitee's duty**: ask whether the meeting and your attendance are desirable and justified, and **tell the chairman if you think not**. Get it called off early, at a **low-value-added stage**, and find a cheaper medium — a one-on-one, a phone call, a note.
  - *(These are 1983 dollars; scale the number, keep the discipline.)*

## Key Concepts
- **Medium vs. activity** — meetings are where managerial work happens, not the work itself.
- **Task-relevant maturity** — sets one-on-one frequency (see Ch 12).
- **Hold file** — a batching device for non-urgent items between one-on-ones.
- **Zinger** — a heavy issue dropped when there's no time to handle it.
- **Open session** — the unscheduled portion of a staff meeting.
- **Rolling schedule** — booking the next meeting at the end of the current one.

## Mental Models
- **Think of a meeting as a production run with set-up costs.** Regular process-oriented meetings amortize the mental set-up across many transactions; ad hoc meetings pay it fresh each time.
- **The one-on-one belongs to the subordinate.** Preparation should land where it costs least and teaches most.
- **A chairman is a producer, not an attendee.** If you show up hoping it goes your way, you have already failed.
- **The operating-room standard**: some may dislike a surgeon insisting on precision, "but I am one patient who would much prefer a disciplined operating room to any other kind."
- **Meeting time is money already spent.** Treat unjustified attendance the way you'd treat an unapproved purchase order.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating meetings as inherently wasteful** ("non-contributory labor") rather than fixing which kind you hold.
- **Relying on casual encounters instead of scheduled one-on-ones**: "Oh no, I don't need scheduled meetings with my supervisor; I see him several times a day…" — Grove's answer is that there is an enormous difference.
- **A 15-minute one-on-one**: guarantees only trivia gets raised.
- **The supervisor preparing the one-on-one agenda**: multiplies preparation by the number of subordinates and removes the subordinate's thinking work.
- **Fixed-date one-on-ones**: a vacation silently cancels the meeting.
- **Pontificating in a staff meeting**: the surest way to undermine free discussion.
- **Letting a staff meeting become a two-person debate** on something affecting only those two.
- **The reviewing manager previewing the material**: kills the spontaneous reaction that gives the review its value.
- **Flipping through every slide to get done**: "his message gets lost even while all his charts get flipped."
- **A disengaged operation-review audience**: worse than not attending.
- **Calling a meeting without knowing the decision you need**.
- **Meetings with more than eight decision-makers**.
- **Tolerating latecomers**: theft of colleagues' time.
- **No minutes, or slow minutes**: forfeits most of the meeting's value.

## Worked Example — A Mission-Oriented Meeting Agenda
Grove's model agenda, showing purpose, time boxes, and named owner for each segment:

```
To:      Far East Plant Manager
         Manufacturing Manager
         Corporate Construction Manager
         President
From:    Far East Construction Manager
Subject: Philippines Plant Location Decision Meeting
         Friday, October 1, 11:00 a.m.–1:00 p.m.
         Santa Clara Conference Room 212
         Teleconference connection to Phoenix Conference Room 4

Purpose of meeting: To decide specific location for Philippine plant expansion

Agenda
11:00–11:30  Manufacturing considerations              (F.E. Plant Manager)
11:30–12:00  Construction considerations               (F.E. Construction Manager)
12:00–12:45  Review of alternatives, incl. preferred choice (F.E. Construction Manager)
12:45–1:00   Discussion                                (All)
```

**What to notice**: (1) the **purpose line names the decision**, not a topic; (2) four attendees — well inside the eight cap; (3) every block has a **named owner**, so each person knows the role they're expected to play; (4) the remote site is specified in advance as logistics, not improvised; (5) the alternatives *including a preferred choice* are presented before open discussion, so the group reacts to a proposal rather than starting from blank.

## Worked Example — A One-on-One That Moved the Company
In a one-on-one, Grove's sales organization head reviewed trend indicators of incoming orders. Grove was "vaguely familiar" with them; the subordinate laid out specific information and **proved the slowdown was not merely seasonal summer softness**. They pondered the data against other industry indicators and reached the reluctant conclusion that business was in fact slowing — meaning a conservative approach to near-term investment.

**The leverage chain**: the subordinate left having decided to scale back growth in his own area. Grove left having decided to share the conclusion with every business group he supervised. "Thus, this one-on-one produced substantial leverage: the Intel sales manager affected all the other managers who reported to me."

**The mechanism named**: sharing a base of information produced "a congruent attitude, approach, and conclusion" — which is precisely the shared common base that makes efficient delegation possible (Ch 3).

## Key Takeaways
1. Meetings are the medium of managerial work; the question is never whether to meet but which kind.
2. Judge organizational health by the share of time in **ad hoc** meetings, not total meeting time — over 25% ad hoc signals malorganization.
3. Run one-on-ones as the subordinate's meeting: their outline, their agenda, their workspace, minimum one hour, frequency set by task-relevant maturity, scheduled on a rolling basis.
4. In a one-on-one, ask one more question, take notes as a commitment device, keep a hold file, and invite heart-to-heart issues early enough to handle them.
5. Staff meetings build the peer interaction that group decision-making and dual reporting require; moderate, don't lecture.
6. Operation reviews carry teaching across organizational levels — and every one of the four roles has real obligations, including the audience's.
7. For a mission-oriented meeting, the chairman does most of the work before it starts: know the decision, secure the right eight-or-fewer people, enforce time discipline, and send fast, specific minutes.
8. Price meetings explicitly and refuse the unjustified ones early, at the low-value stage.
