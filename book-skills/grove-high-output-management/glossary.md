# Glossary — High Output Management

**Abdication** — Delegating a task and then dropping the monitoring. "Delegation without follow-through is abdication." (Ch 3, Ch 12)

**Achiever** — In the ring-toss experiment, the person who stands just far enough away that success is a genuine challenge, working at the boundary of capability. Contrasted with gamblers (high risk, no influence) and conservatives (minimal risk). (Ch 11)

**Achievement-driven** — One of two roads to self-actualization: an abstract need to achieve in everything, held by a minority. Compare competence-driven. (Ch 11)

**Assembly** — Production operation combining components into a new entity. One of the three fundamental operations. (Ch 1)

**Batching** — Grouping similar tasks so one mental set-up cost serves many transactions: reading all reports in a block, accumulating one-on-one items in a hold file. (Ch 3, Ch 4)

**Beauty contest** — The failure mode of a performance bonus based on subjective elements. (Ch 15)

**The blast** — A performance review for someone who could be fired unless turned around; essentially conflict resolution. (Ch 13)

**Black box** — Representation of any production-like activity as inputs + labor → output, into which you cut "windows" to see internal workings and predict future output. (Ch 2)

**Build to forecast** — Committing resources against "a contemplation of future orders," accepting inventory risk. Necessary when throughput time exceeds the customer's tolerable wait. (Ch 2)

**Build to order** — Waiting for the order, then finding a hole in the schedule. No inventory risk, long customer wait. (Ch 2)

**Building czar** — The resident senior manager who periodically scores a building's upkeep against other buildings — Grove's example of manufacturing a racetrack. (Ch 11)

**Can't/won't diagnostic** — Underperformance has only two causes. Test: if their life depended on it, could they do it? Yes → not motivated (motivation). No → not capable (training). (Ch 11)

**Competence-driven** — One of two roads to self-actualization: task mastery pursued for its own sake, like the violinist or the skateboarder. (Ch 11)

**Conservatives** — Ring-toss participants who stood over the pegs and dropped the rings; minimal risk. Punishing failure converts achievers into these. (Ch 11)

**Constructive confrontation** — Intel's favored problem-solving approach, taught in its management courses. (Ch 16)

**Continuous operation** — Automated high-volume flow buying lower cost and predictable quality at the price of flexibility; a continuous egg-boiler cannot make a four-minute egg. (Ch 1)

**Contractual obligations** — The control mode based on agreed rules defining work and standards, plus generalized authority to monitor, evaluate, and correct. Requires overhead: supervisors, police, auditors. (Ch 10)

**Corporate citizenship** — Treating a valued employee as the company's asset, pursuing every avenue to retain them even by transferring them to a peer's department. (Ch 14)

**CUA factor** — Composite index of an environment's **Complexity, Uncertainty, and Ambiguity**, which together with individual motivation determines the appropriate control mode. (Ch 10)

**Difference analysis** — Comparing what the environment demands now against what it will demand in about a year; the gap defines what new work must exist. (Ch 6)

**Dual reporting** — Reporting jointly to a mission-oriented supervisor (priorities, daily monitoring) and a functional one (standards, technical oversight, career). The practice that makes hybrid organizations work. (Ch 9)

**Free discussion → clear decision → full support** — The three-stage ideal decision-making model. *Free* and *clear* matter most exactly where disagreement is greatest. (Ch 5)

**Free-market forces** — The control mode based on price, requiring clearly defined dollar value and no supervision. (Ch 10)

**Functional organization** — Totally centralized form: each function serves all units. Wins on scale, reallocation, specialist leverage, and focus. (Ch 8)

**Functional test** — A test that consumes or destroys the unit tested. Prefer in-process inspection. (Ch 1)

**Gamblers** — Ring-toss participants who casually tossed rings at faraway pegs: high risk, no influence on outcome. (Ch 11)

**Gate-like inspection** — Holding all material until tests complete. Slows throughput but limits escapes. Compare monitoring. (Ch 2)

**Grove's Law** — "All large organizations with a common business purpose end up in a hybrid organizational form." The one exception is the conglomerate, which lacks that common purpose. (Ch 8)

**Grove's Principle of Didactic Management** — "Ask one more question!" When you think the subordinate has said all he wants, ask another — until *both* feel you've reached the bottom. (Ch 4)

**Hold file** — Where supervisor and subordinate accumulate important-but-not-urgent items for the next one-on-one; batching applied to a relationship. (Ch 4)

**Hybrid organization** — A mix of mission-oriented business divisions and centralized functional groups. Balances responsiveness and leverage. (Ch 8)

**In-process inspection** — Monitoring a process variable instead of destroying product — a thermometer in the egg-boiler water. Always preferred to destructive testing. (Ch 1, Ch 2)

**Incoming (receiving) inspection** — Testing raw material at the moment of receipt; the lowest-value inspection point. (Ch 1, Ch 2)

**Indicator** — A measurement focused on a specific operational goal. Must cover output not activity, be physical and countable, and be paired. Any measurement beats none. (Ch 2)

**Information hierarchy** — Verbal (fastest, sketchiest — the headline) → written report (the article) → reiteration and perspective (the news magazine). Sources should complement each other and be deliberately redundant. (Ch 3)

**Internal measures** — What goes on inside the black box — including what sets the stage for future periods' output. Weighed against output measures in a review. (Ch 13)

**Internal subcontractor** — How to think of a functional group: providing services to all business units, presumably cheaper and more responsive than an outside vendor. (Ch 8)

**Key result** — A dated, unambiguous milestone that paces you toward an objective. "Reaching towns A, B, and C in 10, 20, and 30 minutes." (Ch 6)

**Knowledge power vs. position power** — In know-how businesses these diverge rapidly because managers "get a little more obsolete every day." The middle manager's job is to mesh them. (Ch 5)

**Know-how manager** — An individual contributor who gathers and disseminates knowledge, exerting great influence on neighboring organizations. A middle manager by influence, not authority. (Intro, Ch 3)

**Leverage** — The output generated by a given work activity. Can be positive or negative. Raised by breadth (many people affected), duration (brief act, long effect), or uniqueness (key knowledge). (Ch 2, Ch 3)

**Light from distant stars** — Output indicators reflecting work done in earlier periods, masking a manager's current performance. Grove's own review error. (Ch 13)

**Limiting step** — The longest, most difficult, most sensitive, or most expensive step in a flow. Design the whole flow around it, working backward from delivery. (Ch 1)

**Linearity indicator** — Cumulative actual progress plotted against the ideal straight line to target; flashes an early warning while correction is still possible. (Ch 2)

**Lowest competent level** — Where a decision should be made: closest to the situation, combining technical knowledge with judgment earned from errors. (Ch 5)

**Lowest-value-stage rule** — Detect and fix problems at the lowest-value stage possible. "Reject before investing further value." (Ch 1, Ch 2)

**Malorganization test (Grove's)** — Not total meeting time (Drucker's 25%), but **the share of time in *ad hoc, mission-oriented* meetings**. Over 25% signals malorganization. (Ch 4)

**Managerial meddling** — A supervisor exploiting superior knowledge to assume command of a subordinate's situation. Negative leverage: the subordinate narrows his view of what's expected and shows less initiative. (Ch 3)

**MBO (Management by Objectives)** — Planning applied to daily work, answering two questions: where do I want to go (objective) and how will I pace myself (key results). A stopwatch, not a legal document. (Ch 6)

**Messages** — The consolidated themes a performance review actually delivers, distilled from raw observations on the worksheet. (Ch 13)

**Mission-oriented meeting** — Ad hoc, held to produce a specific output, usually a decision. Cap at eight attendees; the chairman does most of the work before it starts. (Ch 4)

**Mission-oriented organization** — Totally decentralized form: each unit pursues its mission independently. Has exactly one advantage — responsiveness — which is decisive. (Ch 8)

**Monitoring (inspection)** — Sampling while the bulk keeps moving; stop the line after repeated failures. Far more coverage per dollar than gate inspection. Lean toward it when big problems are unlikely. (Ch 2)

**Monitoring (managerial)** — Checking that an activity proceeds in line with expectations. The presence or absence of it is what separates delegation from abdication. Not meddling. (Ch 3, Ch 12)

**Nudging** — Advocating a course of action without commanding — a call, a note, a comment. "For every unambiguous decision we make, we probably nudge things a dozen times." (Ch 3)

**Objective** — Where you want to go. Nests: a subordinate's objective is often the supervisor's key result. (Ch 6)

**Offset (time offset)** — Deliberately delaying a shorter step so it completes in sync with the limiting step. Also, the lag between a manager's activity and his organization's output. (Ch 1, Ch 13)

**One-on-one** — A regularly scheduled supervisor-subordinate meeting for mutual teaching and information exchange. **The subordinate's meeting**: his outline, his agenda, his work area, minimum one hour, frequency set by task-relevant maturity, scheduled on a rolling basis. (Ch 4)

**Operation review** — Formal presentations by managers to managers who are not their immediate supervisors, carrying teaching across organizational levels. Four roles: organizing manager, reviewing manager, presenters, audience. (Ch 4)

**Operational values** — The shared sense of how things are done that lets structure become internally given rather than externally imposed, and without which a supervisor cannot effectively delegate. (Ch 12, Ch 14)

**Opportunity at risk** — What a shutdown would actually cost: lost customers plus the cost of luring them back. The real justification for inventory. (Ch 1)

**Output measures** — The black box's output: completed designs, sales quotas met, yield increases. Weighed against internal measures. (Ch 13)

**Paired indicators** — Measuring both effect and counter-effect, because indicators direct behavior. Inventory level ↔ shortages; vouchers processed ↔ errors found. (Ch 2)

**Peer-group syndrome** — A group of organizational equals going in circles without noticing, because people fear differing from the group. (Ch 5)

**Peer-plus-one** — The remedy: adding one hierarchical level above a peer group, who acts as "a godfather, a repository of knowledge about how decisions should be made." (Ch 5)

**Performance bonus** — The variable portion of pay, scaled up with total compensation: ~50% for senior managers, 10–25% for middle managers. (Ch 15)

**Potential trap** — Rating form rather than substance. "The performance rating of a manager cannot be higher than the one we would accord to his organization." Deliberately inverted when hiring. (Ch 13, Ch 14)

**Present value (applied to reviews)** — Weighing long-term-oriented work against short-term: how much will the future-oriented activity pay back, and what is that worth today? (Ch 13)

**Process** — Production operation that physically or chemically changes material. One of the three fundamental operations. (Ch 1)

**Process-oriented meeting** — Regularly scheduled, held to share knowledge and exchange information. Should absorb ~80% of problems. Three types: one-on-one, staff meeting, operation review. (Ch 4)

**Programmed visit** — A formal errand ("Mr. Clean" inspections) that creates the occasion for informal ad hoc information-gathering. (Ch 3)

**Racetrack** — A deliberately created arena of measured competition — rules of the game plus a way to measure yourself. (Ch 11)

**Recycling** — Returning an over-promoted person to the job they did well, openly and with support, naming management's misjudgment rather than forcing them out. (Ch 15)

**Reliability problem** — A defect that could cause complete failure at the customer. Accept/reject is normally economic; **reliability is the exception where no compromise is allowed.** (Ch 2)

**Role model** — The manager's pervasive, unavoidable activity. "Nothing leads as well as example." How you handle your own time is the most important aspect of it. (Ch 3, Ch 10, Ch 16)

**Self-actualization** — "What I can be, I must be." The only need that does not extinguish itself on satisfaction, and therefore the only limitless source of motivation. (Ch 11)

**Six questions (decision-making)** — What decision? When? Who decides? Who is consulted? Who ratifies or vetoes? Who is informed? Settled *before* the fact, they prevent the surprise veto and the appearance of politics. (Ch 5)

**Six-to-eight rule** — The right span of control for a supervisory manager, derived from allocating about a half day per week to each subordinate. Half-day-per-week coordinating-group memberships count as equivalents. (Ch 3)

**Slack** — Deliberate looseness — in inventory, absorbing mismatch between the sales and manufacturing flows, or in a calendar, so one unanticipated call doesn't ruin the day. (Ch 2, Ch 3)

**Stagger chart** — A multi-month forecast re-issued monthly alongside all prior forecasts with actuals marked, showing not just the outlook but how the outlook is changing. Grove's best indicator of business trends. (Ch 2)

**Stages of problem-solving** — Ignore → deny → blame others → **assume responsibility** → find the solution. The move from blaming to assuming is emotional; from assuming to solving is intellectual and easier. (Ch 13)

**Strategy / tactics** — Strategy is "the most abstract and general summary of those actions meaningful to you"; tactics is what you do to implement it. "A strategy at one managerial level is the tactical concern of the next higher level." (Ch 6)

**Task-relevant feedback** — Measures tied to the actual work and the organization's workings, letting a person gauge progress. The performance review is its most important form; compensation is another. (Ch 11, Ch 13, Ch 15)

**Task-relevant maturity (TRM)** — Achievement orientation and readiness to take responsibility, plus education, training, and experience — **specific to the task at hand**. Determines the effective management style, one-on-one frequency, and monitoring intensity. (Ch 3, Ch 4, Ch 12)

**Test** — Production operation examining characteristics. One of the three fundamental operations. (Ch 1)

**Three L's** — Level, listen, leave yourself out. The delivery discipline for a performance review. "Listen" means verifying your message landed, using all your sensory capabilities. (Ch 13)

**Throughput time** — Total elapsed time from starting the first step to delivering the finished product. (Ch 1)

**Transitory team** — A temporary task force or informal group formed faster than the formal organization can change; dissolves once the problem is handled. (Ch 9)

**Trend indicator** — Output measured against time and against a standard, forcing extrapolation and forcing you to think through why results differed from expectation. (Ch 2)

**Tuition principle** — "The subordinate's tuition is paid by his customers. And that is absolutely wrong." Teaching is the supervisor's job, not the customer's expense. (Ch 12)

**Two-plane (multi-plane) organization** — One person on two org charts serving two purposes — operating a unit and coordinating across units. Rank can reverse across planes. (Ch 9)

**Variable inspection** — Varying inspection frequency with observed quality. Applied to management, it is what makes a manager's deep dive sampling rather than meddling. (Ch 2)

**Voluntary surrender of individual decision-making** — What membership in a peer decision body requires. Underwritten by trust, which is a property of culture, not structure. (Ch 9)

**Waffling** — Putting off a decision that affects others' work. "The lack of a decision is the same as a negative decision; no green light is a red light." Unlimited negative leverage. (Ch 3, Ch 10)

**Windows** — Cuts into the black box that reveal internal workings and predict output: leading indicators, linearity indicators, trend indicators, stagger charts. (Ch 2)

**Work simplification** — Flow-chart every real step, count them, target a 30–50% reduction, and question why each exists. Intel achieved ~30% across administrative activities. (Ch 2)

**Worksheet (review)** — Blank-paper method for a review: dump everything without editing, find relationships, distill into "messages," then cut what the subordinate won't remember. (Ch 13)

**Zinger** — A heart-to-heart issue raised at an awkward time, usually near the end of a one-on-one, leaving no time to handle it. (Ch 4)
