# Chapter 1: The New Rules

## Core Idea
Negotiation is not a rational, logic-driven problem-solving exercise — it is emotional, psychological, and irrational. Master your counterpart's feelings (System 1) and you steer their reasoning (System 2). The FBI's emotion-based, listening-first approach beats the academic "Getting to Yes" model in the real world.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Tactical Empathy** (the book's centerpiece): listening as a martial art — deliberately recognizing the counterpart's feelings *and* what's behind them to gain access to their mind and influence them.
  - When to use: every negotiation, from hostage crises to a kid's bedtime.
  - How: observe and label emotions, slow down, make it about the other person, balance emotional intelligence with assertive influence.
- **System 1 / System 2 (Kahneman)**: System 1 is fast, instinctive, emotional; System 2 is slow, logical. System 1 steers System 2. Influence the emotional mind by how you frame and deliver questions, and the rational mind follows.
  - How: e.g. "How am I supposed to do that?" nudged Andy's System 1 into feeling his offer was inadequate; his System 2 then rationalized a better offer.

## Key Concepts
- **Calibrated (open-ended) question**: a query the other side can answer but that has no fixed answer; buys time, gives the illusion of control.
- **Cognitive Bias**: unconscious, irrational brain processes that distort how we see the world (Kahneman/Tversky found 150+).
- **Loss Aversion**: people act more strongly to avert a loss than to achieve an equal gain.
- **BATNA / ZOPA**: the academic "rational actor" tools (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement / Zone of Possible Agreement) the book critiques as overrated.
- **Never split the difference**: a hostage negotiator must win everything without conceding substance — compromise ("two hostages each") is unthinkable.

## Mental Models
- Think of negotiation as **information-gathering + behavior-influencing**, not a battle of arguments.
- Treat people as "crazy, irrational, impulsive, emotionally driven animals" — because they are, and so are you.
- "Life is negotiation": most interactions reduce to "I want." Get over your aversion to it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Pure rational problem-solving** (the classic *Getting to Yes* model): fails because emotions *are* the problem, not a separable obstacle.
- **Scripted a→b→c→d sequences**: the real world is unpredictable; you may need a→d→q.

## Worked Example
At Harvard, Mnookin role-plays a kidnapper: "Give us one million dollars or he dies." Instead of arguing, Voss responds calmly, using the counterpart's first name and an apology to seed warmth, then repeatedly asks the open-ended question "How am I supposed to do that?" / "How do I know he's even alive?" Every threat is met with a question that shifts the burden back onto the professor. Mnookin gets flustered, then Blum tries and fails too. Voss wins by refusing to play the logic game — demonstrating that emotionally attuned questioning beats credentialed rational tactics.

## Key Takeaways
1. Negotiation is emotional; design your approach around psychology, not logic.
2. Influence System 1 (emotion) and System 2 (reason) follows.
3. Calibrated questions ("How am I supposed to do that?") give the other side the illusion of control while you frame the conversation.
4. Listening is the cheapest and most effective concession you can make.
5. Don't split the difference — embrace the discomfort of holding out for the real win.
