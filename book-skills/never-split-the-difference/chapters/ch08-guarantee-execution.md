# Chapter 8: Guarantee Execution

## Core Idea
"Yes" is nothing without "How." A deal only counts when implemented. Be a decision architect: use "How" questions to force the counterpart to articulate execution, identify deal-killers "behind the table," verify commitment with the Rule of Three, and read nonverbal/verbal tells.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"How" questions to guarantee execution**: make the counterpart explain implementation in their own words so they own it.
  - Two key questions: "How will we know we're on track?" and "How will we address things if we find we're off track?"
- **The Rule of Three**: get agreement to the same thing three times in one conversation (vary the tactic: a commitment, then a summary→"That's right," then a calibrated "How"). Hard to fake conviction three times.
- **The 7-38-55 Percent Rule** (Mehrabian): 7% of a message is words, 38% tone, 55% body language. When tone/body don't match words, label the incongruence.
- **The multi-step "No"** (four ways before the word): (1) "How am I supposed to do that?"; (2) "Your offer is very generous, I'm sorry, that just doesn't work for me"; (3) "I'm sorry but I'm afraid I just can't do that"; (4) "I'm sorry, no." — gets them to bid against themselves.

## Key Concepts
- **Behind-the-table / "Level II" players**: deal-killers not at the table. Probe with "How does this affect the rest of your team?" / "How on board are the people not on this call?"
- **The Pinocchio Effect**: liars use more words and more third-person pronouns to distance themselves from the lie.
- **Pronoun importance tell**: lots of "I/me/my" → low decision power; "we/they/them" → likely the real decision-maker keeping options open.
- **The Chris Discount / forced empathy**: use *your own* name ("My name is Chris. What's the Chris discount?") to humanize yourself.
- **Watch for "I'll try" and "You're right"**: both signal the counterpart isn't bought in ("I'll try" = "I plan to fail").

## Mental Models
- Success isn't "we have a deal" — it's the freed hostage saying "Thank you."
- One bit player can sink a deal (the walkie-talkie nearly cost a finger; the Florida division head killed a lucrative training deal).

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming the people on the call are the only decision-makers** (lost the Florida deal).
- **Accepting "Yes," "You're right," or "I'll try"** as commitment.
- **Ignoring incongruence** between words and tone/body.

## Worked Example
José's kidnapping in Ecuador: Voss has Julie answer *every* demand with a "How" question — "How do I know José is alive?", to $5M: "How can we raise that much?", later "How can I do that when I have to sell my cars?" The questions stall for time and drive the ransom from $5M to $16,500. Crucially (revealed in the debrief), Julie's persistent questions so confused the designated negotiator that the whole gang kept meeting to coordinate answers — forcing internal alignment and creating the chaos that let José escape. "How" questions guaranteed the *team* was engaged, not just one negotiator.

## Key Takeaways
1. "Yes" is nothing without "How" — make them define implementation.
2. Identify and influence behind-the-table players with "How" questions.
3. Use the Rule of Three to test real commitment.
4. Apply 7-38-55: watch tone and body; label incongruence.
5. Read pronouns; use your own name for a personal discount.
