# Chapter 9: Bargain Hard

## Core Idea
When it comes to brass-tacks haggling, identify your counterpart's negotiating type, prepare relentlessly, learn to take and throw a punch without anger, and run the Ackerman system to land on the number you want while they feel they squeezed you dry.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The three negotiator types** (treat them as they need, not as you'd want — the Black Swan rule):
  - **Analyst**: methodical, data-driven, hates surprises; silence = thinking. Be prepared, use data, warn early, give them time.
  - **Accommodator**: relationship-first, friendly, win-win; silence = anger. Be sociable but use calibrated questions to drive action.
  - **Assertive**: time = money, wants to be heard first; silence = a chance to talk more. Mirrors, labels, and getting a "that's right" work best.
- **The Ackerman Model** (offer-counteroffer haggling system):
  1. Set your **target price**.
  2. First offer = **65%** of target (extreme anchor).
  3. Three raises of decreasing increments to **85%, 95%, 100%**.
  4. Use empathy + many ways to say "No" to make them counter before each raise.
  5. Final number = **precise, non-round** (e.g. $37,893).
  6. On the final number, throw in a **non-monetary item** they don't want, to signal your limit.
- **Punch tactics**: deflect an extreme anchor with "How am I supposed to accept that?" or "What are we trying to accomplish here?"; pivot to terms; if pushed to go first, allude to a high number someone *else* would charge.
- **Assertion tools**: real (not faked) controlled anger, threats with poise, strategic umbrage, "Why would you do that?" turned to your favor, and "I-messages" to set boundaries.

## Key Concepts
- **"I am normal" paradox**: assuming the world looks to others as it does to you — 66% chance your counterpart's style differs.
- **No neediness / ready-to-walk mindset**: no deal is better than a bad deal.
- **"You fall to your highest level of preparation."**
- **The adversary is the situation, not the person.** Never create an enemy.

## Mental Models
- "Everybody has a plan until they get punched in the mouth" — prepare to take the extreme anchor.
- Any response that isn't an outright rejection means you have the edge.
- Decreasing increments (each ~half the last) convince them they've squeezed every drop; non-round numbers have credibility.

## Anti-patterns
- **Projecting your own style** onto the counterpart (the "I am normal" trap).
- **Folding after the first punch** / fleeing to your maximum.
- **Faked anger**: backfires, destroys trust.
- **Wimp-win / neediness**: aiming low to protect self-esteem.

## Worked Example
Voss wants a $30,000 Salsa Red 4Runner listed at $36,000. He opens with empathy and a firm, apologetic anchor: "I can pay $30,000, cash today. I'm sorry, I just can't pay more." To each counter ($34,000, then $32,500 on a "YOU WIN" sheet) he warmly praises the offer and repeats a gentle, non-round "No": "You've been very generous… I'm sorry, I just can't do that." Silence does the rest. The salesman walks back and forth, and finally: "We can do that" — $30,000. (See also Mishary's rent negotiation: research + decreasing Ackerman offers ending at $1,829 beat a proposed $2,100 increase.)

## Key Takeaways
1. Identify the counterpart's type (Analyst/Accommodator/Assertive) and adapt.
2. Prepare, prepare, prepare — you fall to your preparation.
3. Get ready to take a punch (extreme anchor) without fleeing to your max.
4. Set boundaries and punch back without anger; the situation is the adversary.
5. Run the Ackerman plan: 65 / 85 / 95 / 100, non-round, plus a token item.
