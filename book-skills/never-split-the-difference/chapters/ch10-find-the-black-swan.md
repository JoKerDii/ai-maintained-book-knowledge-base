# Chapter 10: Find the Black Swan

## Core Idea
In every negotiation there are 3–5 hidden pieces of information ("Black Swans") that, once uncovered, change everything. Finding them requires a beginner's mind, deep listening, face time, and understanding the counterpart's worldview ("religion"). Black Swans are leverage multipliers.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Black Swans (unknown unknowns)**: game-changing info you didn't know existed. Distinguish: *known knowns* (facts you have), *known unknowns* (gaps you're aware of), *unknown unknowns* (Black Swans).
- **The three types of leverage**:
  - **Positive**: your ability to give/withhold what they want ("I want…" = you have it).
  - **Negative**: your ability to make them suffer (based on loss aversion/threats — use sparingly; label it rather than shove it down their throat).
  - **Normative**: using their own norms/standards/beliefs against inconsistencies (no one likes to look like a hypocrite).
- **Know their "religion"**: understand the worldview — God, market, values, what they think is fair/just — that has authority over them.
- **The Similarity Principle**: we trust those we see as similar; trigger in-group belonging.
- **"When someone seems crazy, they're not"** — diagnose one of three causes: (1) ill-informed, (2) constrained (can't reveal limits), (3) other/hidden interests.
- **Get face time & observe unguarded moments**: Black Swans surface in person, at the fringes (start/end of meetings, interruptions), not over email.

## Key Concepts
- **Leverage ≠ power**: leverage is fluid; whoever feels they have more to lose has less of it. "If they're talking to you, you have leverage."
- **The power of hopes and dreams**: express passion for the counterpart's aspirations and they'll follow you.
- **"Religion as a reason"**: requests followed by "because…" (referencing their worldview) gain compliance (Langer copier study: 60% → 90%+).

## Mental Models
- Every case is new: let known knowns guide but not blind you (no hostage-taker had ever killed on deadline — until Griffin did).
- "It's not crazy, it's a clue" — the moment you want to declare "they're crazy" is often the best moment to find a Black Swan.
- Listen, listen again, listen some more; use backup listeners.

## Anti-patterns
- **Labeling the counterpart "crazy"** and disengaging.
- **Confirmation-seeking research** that only verifies known knowns.
- **Negotiating only over email** (too much time to re-center; no tone/body).
- **Shoving negative leverage down their throat** (threatens autonomy; people die before surrendering autonomy).

## Worked Example
Dwight Watson, a tobacco farmer, threatens to blow up his tractor on the National Mall. Voss's team listens for his worldview rather than arguing. They learn he's a veteran (normative leverage: military honor lets him withdraw "with honor" after three days) and wants to be heard (positive leverage). Agent Winnie Miller, listening between the lines, identifies he's a devout Christian and suggests framing tomorrow as the "Dawn of the Third Day" — when Christ left the tomb. They tell Watson; long silence; "I'll come out." A 48-hour standoff ends peacefully — solved by a Black Swan in his religion.

## Key Takeaways
1. Assume 3–5 Black Swans exist; stay flexible — every case is new.
2. Black Swans multiply positive, negative, and normative leverage.
3. Understand the counterpart's "religion"/worldview — that's where Black Swans live.
4. "Crazy" usually means ill-informed, constrained, or hidden interests.
5. Get face time; mine unguarded moments; exploit the similarity principle.
6. Embrace honest conflict — the adversary is the situation, not the person.
