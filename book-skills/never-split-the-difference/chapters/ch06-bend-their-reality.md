# Chapter 6: Bend Their Reality

## Core Idea
Value is relative and decisions are emotional. Never compromise ("split the difference"). Instead, shape how the counterpart perceives the deal — through deadlines, fairness, anchoring, loss aversion, and odd numbers — so they want to give you what you wanted all along.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Don't compromise**: "No deal is better than a bad deal." Splitting the difference = one black + one brown shoe.
- **Anchor their emotions**: open with an accusation audit that anchors expectations low, then introduce your offer so it triggers loss aversion.
- **Let the other side anchor first (usually)**: you lack full market info; their first offer may exceed your target. But brace for an extreme anchor from a pro.
- **Establish a range / bolstering range**: cite a "ballpark" from comparable deals (low end = what you actually want); raises their reference point.
- **Pivot to nonmonetary terms**: offer things cheap to them but valuable to you (e.g. the magazine cover story).
- **Use odd, non-round numbers**: $37,263 feels calculated and firm; numbers ending in 0 feel like placeholders.
- **Surprise with a gift**: after a rejection, an unrelated gift triggers reciprocity.

## Key Concepts
- **Prospect Theory (Kahneman/Tversky)**: how people choose under risk.
- **Certainty Effect**: drawn to sure things over better-odds gambles.
- **Loss Aversion**: take more risk to avoid a loss than to achieve an equal gain — so make them feel they have something concrete to *lose*.
- **The F-word "Fair"**: three uses — (1) defensive accusation ("We just want what's fair") to destabilize you; (2) "We've given you a fair offer" to distract; (3) the positive setup: "I want you to feel treated fairly at all times — stop me if I'm ever unfair."
- **Anchor-and-adjustment effect**: we extrapolate from the first number we hear.

## Mental Models
- All negotiations sit on a network of subterranean desires (the Haitian kidnappers just wanted "party money").
- Deadlines are usually imaginary, flexible, self-inflicted — and cut both ways; revealing yours often gets a better deal.
- Know the emotional driver: a babysitter sells a relaxed evening, a locksmith sells security.

## Anti-patterns
- **Splitting the difference / wimp-win** compromise.
- **Letting a deadline take you hostage** (rushing into bad choices).
- **Conceding when someone drops the F-bomb**: instead, "I apologize. Let's go back to where I started treating you unfairly and fix it," or mirror "Fair?"
- **Going first on price when you don't know the market** (Jerry asked $110k; everyone else started at $125k).

## Worked Example
The Haitian aunt's ransom: kidnappers demand $150,000. Voss coaches the nephew to never say "No" but anchor that he has no money, question fairness ("how are we supposed to pay if you'll hurt her?"), and let them name a number first — dropping them to $50k, then $25k. Then the nephew sets an extreme low anchor ($3,000), endures the silence, answers $10k with the oddly precise **$4,751**, and "spontaneously" throws in a portable CD stereo. The kidnappers accept $4,751. Aunt home safely.

## Key Takeaways
1. Don't compromise — meeting halfway often produces bad deals for both.
2. Anchor emotions low first, then make them feel a potential loss.
3. Let them anchor price when you lack info; use a bolstering range when you must speak.
4. Pivot to nonmonetary value; use odd numbers; surprise with a gift.
5. Handle "Fair" deliberately — don't get suckered into conceding.
