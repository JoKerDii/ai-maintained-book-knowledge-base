# Chapter 12: Turn Your Words into Deeds

## Core Idea
Words without deeds are empty rhetoric. Effective speakers don't just diagnose a problem — they offer a prescription and make sure the audience knows exactly what to do. Every good presentation needs a clear call to action.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Have an Ask, Every Time**: Every good speech has a call to action.
  - Be Specific — don't ask for vague "support"; ask for one small, concrete action ("sign our petition," "donate ten dollars," "call your member of Congress"). Big asks intimidate; the best call to action is often a small action.
  - List Your Demands — for change, state exactly what you want, one by one (Kimberly Mata-Rubio's gun-safety list), so leaders can't shirk.
  - Asks can be intangible too (a retirement toast asks coworkers to emulate; a eulogy asks mourners to carry on the person's values).
- **Ways to Turn Words Into Deeds**:
  - Share Some Lessons (McRaven's "ten lessons… make your bed").
  - Issue a Challenge (Obama's Athens "summons to citizenship").
  - Lead by Example — "Do as I do," Step Up and Do Something, Put Your Money Where Your Mouth Is (Robert F. Smith paying off Morehouse loans; 6-year-old Sophia's piggy bank).
  - Offer a Vision — give a goal to imagine, carry, and work toward ("think big, be bold, then boil it down… make it catchy").

## Key Concepts
- **Empowering language**: Lay out concrete actions the audience can take (Michele Gelfand).
- **Road map / signposts**: Tell the audience the structure — "I believe we need four steps… First… Second… Finally…" — since they can't see the speech.
- **Vision**: A picture of the world as it ought to be, which "organizes and measures the best of our energies" (Kennedy on the moon); central to religions, movements, and companies (Jobs "reinvent the phone").

## Mental Models
- If the audience leaves unsure what to do, the talk missed the mark — always have an ask.
- "Talk alone doesn't cook rice" — describing a problem without a solution rings hollow.
- To make a vision spread, keep it catchy, simple, and specific so people "understand, get excited, and then get to work" (fill-in-the-blank: "Our vision is a world free from ___").

## Anti-patterns
- **Vague promises**: Geithner's Financial Stability Plan speech promised details "in the next few weeks" instead of specifics; the Dow dropped ~400 points — Obama called it a "disaster."
- **Mealymouthed appeals**: Vague calls for "progress" or "change" mean anything and therefore nothing.
- **All words, no deeds**: Preaching a cause without backing it yourself.

## Worked Example
Two weeks after her 10-year-old daughter Lexi was murdered in the Uvalde school shooting — before Lexi was even buried — Kimberly Mata-Rubio testified to Congress. She humanized Lexi ("intelligent, compassionate… quiet. Shy, unless she had a point to make," dreamed of law school), then turned to an unmistakable list of demands: "we demand action. We seek a ban on assault rifles and high-capacity magazines… raise the age to purchase… red-flag laws, stronger background checks… repeal gun manufacturers' liability immunity." Combined with a swelling public movement, it helped produce — a month later — the first major gun-safety law in nearly 30 years. Say exactly what you want; specificity makes it harder for leaders to shirk.

## Key Takeaways
1. Always end with a clear, specific call to action.
2. Ask for a small concrete step; list your demands one by one for change.
3. Give a road map with signposts (First, Second, Finally).
4. Lead by example — back words with your own deeds and money.
5. Offer a big, bold, catchy vision to organize and inspire the work.
