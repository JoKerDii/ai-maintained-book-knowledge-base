# Chapter 7: Appeal to Values

## Core Idea
To persuade, don't argue the points that make sense *to you* — frame your argument around the values your *audience* cherishes most. This is Values-Based Public Speaking, and it can build bridges across even deep divides.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Values-Based Public Speaking (Moral Framing)**: Wrap your argument in the listener's values.
  - When to use: Whenever persuading, especially across ideological or cultural difference.
  - How: Identify which values your audience prioritizes, then frame your idea in that language (immigration reframed as loyalty/patriotism persuades conservatives; military spending reframed as fairness persuades liberals).
- **The Six Moral Foundations**: The prisms through which people see the world.
  - Care/Harm, Fairness/Cheating, Authority/Subversion, Loyalty/Betrayal, Sanctity/Degradation, Liberty/Oppression. Progressives lean Care/Fairness; conservatives lean Authority/Loyalty/Sanctity; everyone likes Liberty. (See the value→theme table in the book's Download.)

## Key Concepts
- **Moral empathy gap**: We fail to appreciate that others have a different moral worldview; not feeling what they feel makes it hard to understand how they think (UC Irvine).
- **Moral framing**: Presenting an argument in the value language of the persuadee.
- **Values are universal**: Care, fairness, loyalty, liberty aren't owned by any group — they transcend fault lines (Obama's Brazil/Ghana/Myanmar speeches used near-identical value language to similar applause).
- **Shared values, different opinions**: Same value ("freedom," "protecting life") can yield opposite policy views — but the shared value is still the bridge.
- **Values are (usually) good for business**: Employees and customers increasingly prefer values-aligned companies (Benioff/Salesforce); but back words with deeds or risk "greenwashing" charges and backlash.

## Mental Models
- Use "This is true, but this is also true" — acknowledge the value on the other side rather than contradicting it.
- Before speaking on values as a leader, run the Ch 1 questions: Who am I? What do I/my company believe? What do stakeholders expect? What price for speaking — or not speaking?
- Persuasion often *fails* the more we talk when we use our own values; talking past each other entrenches both sides (the Twitter-follow study).

## Anti-patterns
- **Arguing in your own values**: In the study, 74% of liberals argued same-sex marriage via fairness/equality (liberal values); only 9% used loyalty/unity (conservative values), and a third *contradicted* conservative values. "Talk about not reading the room."
- **Attacking the audience's values**: "your religion should play no part in the laws" — guaranteed to fail.
- **Values as a cover**: CEOs preaching against inequality while paying little tax; sustainability rhetoric masking harmful practice.

## Worked Example
After *Roe v. Wade* fell, Kansas — deeply conservative — was first to vote on abortion. Ashley All (a self-described "pretty darn progressive" Democrat) and Kansans for Constitutional Freedom deliberately did *not* demonize opponents or lead with liberal values like "equality" or "right to choose" — often not even saying "abortion." Instead they framed it in values that resonate broadly, including with conservatives: the "freedom to make their own private medical decisions," opposing "government interference" and "a strict government mandate." "Don't let politicians take away your freedom… stand for liberty." Result: 59-41 to protect abortion rights, even in rural counties. "We can't just yell at people… we have to use language that brings people together around common values."

## Key Takeaways
1. Frame arguments in the audience's values, not your own.
2. Learn the Six Moral Foundations and which your audience leans toward.
3. Most people share your basic values even when they disagree on issues.
4. Values persuade across cultures and are usually good for business.
5. Back values talk with deeds; be honest about the price of speaking (or not).
