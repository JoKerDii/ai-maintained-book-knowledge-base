# Chapter 8: Speak from Your Heart . . . and to Theirs

## Core Idea
Facts and statistics rarely change minds or motivate action. The most moving part of a speech is the emotion — pathos beats logos. Speak from your own heart, and speak to the hearts of your audience.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Speak From Your Heart**: Bring genuine emotion to your remarks.
  - Feel What You're Saying (you can't tap the audience's emotion unless you feel it first).
  - Use Emotional Words (an "Emotions Wheel"/"Feelings Wheel": not "happy" but "proud/hopeful/joyful"; not "angry" but "frustrated/furious/indignant").
  - Get Emotional (show how you feel; sharing emotion is strength, not weakness).
  - Be Vulnerable (share setbacks, pain, trauma — it gives the audience space to reflect on their own).
- **Speak To Their Heart**: Reach the listener emotionally.
  - Speak to One Person ("find your Uncle Dan" — write for one real, specific person; say "you," not "they").
  - Speak to Their Experience (empathy — acknowledge the histories, pride, and pain that shaped them).
  - Show a Human Face (humanize an issue with one identifiable person — "look at the one").

## Key Concepts
- **Pathos over logos**: Emotion (Aristotle's pathos) is more moving than argument (logos).
- **Psychic numbing**: The larger the scale of suffering, the more overwhelmed and *less* likely to help people become (donations *fell* as the number of children rose).
- **Identifiable victim effect**: We empathize more with one specific person (little Rokia; Omran Daqneesh) than with statistics.
- **Confirmation bias / cognitive dissonance**: Audiences hear what they want and reject data that challenges beliefs; "facts are no match for fear."
- **Oxytocin ("the moral molecule")**: Human-centered stories release oxytocin, raising compassion, generosity, and donations (Paul Zak — +56%).
- **The moral-emotional word effect**: On Twitter, each moral-emotional word made a post ~20% more likely to be shared.

## Mental Models
- If you must use statistics, ask: Will it clarify? Will it increase action? Does it tell a story? If no, cut it. Use data to tell a story with a beginning, middle, end.
- To motivate, don't inject oxytocin — "do the next best thing": tell the story of the people you're trying to help, ideally a single person.
- "He saw us. He understands us." — audiences remember how you made them *feel*, not your best line.

## Anti-patterns
- **Blizzard of data**: Greta Thunberg's Madrid speech rattled off gigatons and page numbers — "it felt like no one understood a word I said."
- **Backfire from more information**: Giving vaccine-skeptical parents more facts made some *more* skeptical.
- **Cold, empty presentations**: "an empty, cold speech with no emotion is always a risk — for anyone."

## Worked Example
After the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing, Szuplat had ~48 hours to draft Obama's memorial remarks. He wrote for one specific person — his conservative, Obama-skeptical, Boston-to-the-core Uncle Dan — asking "What would he want to hear?" Obama turned the sermon into a rally, channeling "Boston Strong": "they picked the wrong city… Not here in Boston!… We will finish the race! Bet on it!" The cathedral erupted; the city took it in together in bars and restaurants — a communal experience. That afternoon Uncle Dan, who rarely called, left a voicemail: "He did a good job. It was a very fine speech." Obama had connected with someone he had little in common with by speaking to his heart. "When you give a speech, find your Uncle Dan."

## Key Takeaways
1. Pathos beats logos; emotion is the most moving element.
2. Use statistics only to clarify, drive action, or tell a story.
3. Feel it yourself, use emotional words, get emotional, be vulnerable.
4. Write and speak to one specific person; say "you."
5. Show empathy for the audience's experience.
6. Humanize issues with a single identifiable person — "look at the one."
