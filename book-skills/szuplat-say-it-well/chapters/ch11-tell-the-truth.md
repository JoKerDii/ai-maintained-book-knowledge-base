# Chapter 11: Tell the Truth

## Core Idea
In a "post-truth" world where falsehoods travel faster than facts, your credibility depends on truthfulness. To be persuasive you must be believable; to be believable, credible; to be credible, truthful. When you speak, tell the truth.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Ten Commandments for Telling the Truth**: A checklist for honest speaking.
  1. **Thou shalt not steal** — don't plagiarize; credit anything borrowed (Robert Caslen resigned days after copying McRaven's speech).
  2. **Thou shalt not make shit up** — it's wrong, you'll get caught, credibility gone (George Santos).
  3. **Thou shalt get thy facts right** — check every fact/figure against reputable sources.
  4. **Thou shalt look for the source** — trace internet stats/quotes to the original; if you can't back it up, don't say it (or "As the old saying goes…").
  5. **Thou shalt be honest about the past** — the blemishing effect; humility builds credibility (Nikki Haley on the Confederate flag).
  6. **Thou shalt avoid "happy talk"** — accentuate the positive but acknowledge the negative.
  7. **Thou shalt not hide from elephants** — make the elephant in the room the main event (Obama's Nobel).
  8. **Thou shalt use absolutes sparingly** — most issues aren't black and white.
  9. **Thou shalt not fear nuance** — acknowledge the gray, even if it takes more words.
  10. **Thou shalt speak hard truths** — tell audiences what they *need* to hear, not just what they *want* to hear.

## Key Concepts
- **Illusory truth effect**: The more we hear something, even if false, the more we believe it (the Goebbels "big lie").
- **Blemishing effect**: Sharing a little negative information can *strengthen* a positive impression.
- **Happy talk**: One-sided positivity; Obama's note — "don't want to have just happy talk," acknowledge remaining dangers.
- **Elephant in the room**: A controversy/crisis; hiding it makes the speaker look out of touch and the elephant bigger; face it early.
- **Quantitative honesty**: "Some" <50%, "Most" >50%, "Vast majority" ≥70%, "Nearly/almost all" ≥90%; beware "many" (true but misleading — "many love / many hate social media").
- **Reliable sources**: Primary sources, Britannica, FactCheck.org/PolitiFact, Pew, wire services (AP/Reuters); for quotes, Bartlett's, Oxford Dictionary of Quotations, QuoteInvestigator.com.

## Mental Models
- "Let's start with what's true" (Obama's meeting opener) — begin from facts.
- Circle every fact, figure, and assertion in your draft and verify against multiple sources; be very skeptical of Wikipedia, chatbots ("hallucinations"), and social media.
- Prefer "most/often/rarely" over "all/always/never"; "This is true, but this is also true" acknowledges reality.

## Anti-patterns
- **Plagiarism**: A single stolen speech can cost you your job (Caslen).
- **Absolutist framing**: "Everyone believes…," "Liberals never…" — comforting but false, and it can license "any means necessary."
- **Fearing nuance**: Oversimplifying to win applause; Obama took "pride in… being able to see both sides."

## Worked Example
Accepting the 2009 Nobel Peace Prize, Obama faced a huge elephant: days earlier he'd ordered 30,000 more troops to Afghanistan — receiving a peace prize while escalating a war. Rather than bury it, he made the elephant the star. Two minutes in: "perhaps the most profound issue surrounding my receipt of this prize is the fact that I am the Commander-in-Chief of the military of a nation in the midst of two wars… Some will kill, and some will be killed." He built the entire speech around the tension between war and a just peace — often considered one of the best of his presidency. Face the elephant squarely and early; your candor enhances credibility.

## Key Takeaways
1. Truthfulness is the root of credibility and persuasion.
2. Don't plagiarize, fabricate, or get facts wrong; trace and cite sources.
3. Be honest about the past and the negatives; avoid happy talk.
4. Confront the elephant in the room early, not as an afterthought.
5. Use absolutes sparingly; embrace nuance; speak hard truths.
