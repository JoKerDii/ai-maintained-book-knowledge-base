# Chapter 14: Make It Better

## Core Idea
The final 25% of the 50–25–25 Rule is for editing and rehearsing — taking your draft from good to great. Less is more: brevity forces depth, and practice builds the muscle memory that calms nerves.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **How to Edit**: Get to "less."
  - **Cut 15 Percent** — no matter how short, cut roughly 15% (the Santiago speech, a page shaved on descent); even if you can't hit 15%, you'll cut more than you thought.
  - **Slash Needless Words** — "Omit needless words" (Sorensen's "Fresh Fish for Sale Here Today" → just "Fish"). Fewer pages, paragraphs, sentences, words, syllables. When in doubt, cut it out.
  - **Get a Second Opinion (and a third, fourth)** — share the draft; ask "Is this clear? What am I missing? What can I cut?" and ask what they think your main message is (the *schicksalsgemeinschaft*/Hitler near-miss). But no good speech is written by committee — keep your voice.
- **How to Rehearse**: Practice like a performer.
  - **Print It Out** — 24-point font, 1.5 spacing, bold, end each page on a period, leave the bottom third blank (so your chin stays up); binder + plastic sleeves outdoors.
  - **Don't Try to Memorize It** — forget one word and you risk derailing; most speakers don't memorize.
  - **Hear Your Words** — practice aloud to time it and catch tongue-twisters (swap "underserved" for "marginalized"; use one-syllable words).
  - **Feel Your Fears** — interoceptive exposure: get your heart pounding (jumping jacks) then rehearse, to build tolerance.
- **Still Nervous? Self-talk + Prep**: "I will make mistakes"; "When I make a mistake, I will recover" (make fears smaller and specific + a recovery plan); "The audience won't see my nerves" (illusion of transparency); "I'll be better than I think" (98% rated themselves better on video than predicted). Plus Visualize Your Success and Do a Dry Run.

## Key Concepts
- **The beauty is the brevity**: It's harder to prepare a short speech than a long one (Gettysburg = 272 words; Everett spoke 2 hours, forgotten). "You don't have to talk a lot to say a lot."
- **Earbashing**: The Australian word for too much talking; take the oath never to bash your audience's ears.
- **Illusion of transparency**: The audience usually can't see your nerves — "You didn't look nervous."
- **Muscle memory**: The more you say the words, the more organic and easy they become (Axelrod on the 2004 rehearsal — "he could have delivered it from memory").

## Mental Models
- Edit like a surgeon (scalpel) most of the time; when you must cut a whole page, use a machete.
- Assume feedback is right and look for a way to incorporate it — especially if multiple people say the same thing — but trust your gut in the end.
- Use a bot as an editor ("Remove 50 words from the following text"; "make this more/less formal") — you can't hurt its feelings.

## Anti-patterns
- **"I could deliver this speech today"**: The six words Obama's writers dreaded — meaning he *wouldn't*; "let's make it even better."
- **Rejecting honest feedback**: Instinctively defending your draft wastes the whole point of sharing it — accept criticism with humility.
- **Trying to memorize**: For most people, one forgotten word derails the whole talk.

## Worked Example
Flying into Santiago aboard Air Force One, Szuplat thought a six-page speech was final. Obama stood over him: "This is a good six-page speech. It would be a great five-page speech… See if you can shave a page off" — no suggestions on what to cut, with the plane already descending. Szuplat swapped his scalpel for a machete: a too-long sentence, gone; a detailed paragraph, bye-bye; a whole section, adios. The speech dropped ~15%. Obama spoke ~30 minutes instead of ~40; no policy expert complained; the core themes survived and it was well received. Over time Szuplat realized Obama was teaching them to decide what was truly necessary — the cuts made it "shorter, sharper, better — every time."

## Key Takeaways
1. Reserve the last 25% for editing and rehearsing.
2. Cut 15% and slash needless words — the beauty is the brevity.
3. Get honest feedback from several people; keep your own voice.
4. Print in large, bold, well-spaced font; don't memorize; practice aloud.
5. Rehearse while anxious (feel your fears); visualize success; do a dry run.
