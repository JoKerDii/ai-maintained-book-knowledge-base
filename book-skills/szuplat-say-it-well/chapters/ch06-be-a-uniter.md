# Chapter 6: Be a Uniter

## Core Idea
A truly good speech can have only one purpose — to do good by uniting people around a good cause. Early in your remarks (ideally right after the opening), get to your point and connect through a shared challenge, a shared identity, or a shared idea — without becoming a demagogue.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Shareds**: Ways to unite an audience.
  - Rally around a **Shared Challenge**: describe the problem concretely and vividly ("the more a group perceives a threat, the more it works together"). Use the **Threat Dictionary** vocabulary (attack, chaos, collapse, deadly, devastating, suffering, victims…). Don't be vague — "Gun violence is killing our children" beats "our community is impacted by gun violence."
  - Evoke a **Shared Identity**: the Power of You (lead with the identity that best connects — "I'm speaking as a father / a veteran / a child of immigrants"), the Power of "We" (inclusive "we/our/all of us" — "the single most powerful word in our democracy is 'We'"), the Power of What We Do (turn actions into identities — "a voter" beats "planning to vote").
  - Appeal to a **Shared Idea**: wrap the talk in a larger belief; the bigger the idea, the better. You can also **Challenge an Idea** (reframe/push a new one) or **Defend an Idea** under threat.

## Key Concepts
- **Demagogue**: A speaker who villainizes whole groups by appealing to prejudice; the opposite of a uniter.
- **The hallmarks of a demagogue**: otherize ("us" vs. "them"), demonize ("sinister/evil"), dehumanize ("vermin/animals/hordes"), or frame opponents as "virus/poison" to "eradicate."
- **Threat Dictionary**: ~240 words (Michele Gelfand) that instantly attract attention and encourage collective action.
- **Polarization Dictionary**: ~200 words used by the most partisan accounts (idiots, liars, right-wing fascists, coastal elites) — avoid them, especially when persuading.
- **Turning actions into identities**: Jonah Berger — fewer verbs, more nouns ("be a voter").

## Mental Models
- Focus on the **problem, not the people** — attack arguments, not character ("never wrestle with a pig… the pig likes it").
- Don't question motives: we rarely know what's in another's heart; claiming opponents "want to hurt children" fuels distrust and fails to persuade.
- "When they go low, we go high" is empirically sound — threatened people can't listen or change (Alana Conner).
- Tell them what you *believe*, not just what you think ("I believe our community is stronger when…").

## Anti-patterns
- **Demagoguery**: Otherizing/demonizing/dehumanizing — words have consequences (Nazis' "rats," Rwanda's "cockroaches," "superpredators" and mass incarceration).
- **Threatening or inciting violence**: "fight," "take back our country," "enemies to be destroyed" — and watch your *buts* ("no room for violence, but…" negates everything before it).
- **Vague abstractions**: "Climate change touches us all" instead of "the storms, fires, and droughts… are destroying our homes."

## Worked Example
Amanda Jones — a lifelong Republican, Christian, LSU-loving school librarian in Watson, Louisiana — sees "Book Content" on her library board's agenda, recognizing a book-banning push targeting works by/about people of color and the LGBTQ community. Her first draft was "guns blazing"; fellow librarians urged a conciliatory tone, so she toned it down. At the meeting she controlled her anger, didn't attack or question motives, invited the board member to learn about censorship's harms, tapped shared identity ("as a lifelong resident of Livingston Parish, parent… and taxpayer"), described the concrete challenge, and defended a shared idea — the freedom to read: "no one portion of the community should dictate what the rest… have access to." The board removed no books. (She was then harassed and threatened but refused to back down: "I'm going to roar.")

## Key Takeaways
1. Every good speech has one purpose: unite people around a good cause.
2. State your purpose early, right after the opening.
3. Rally around a shared challenge with concrete, vivid language.
4. Evoke shared identity (You / We / What We Do).
5. Appeal to, challenge, or defend a shared idea; say what you *believe*.
6. Never otherize, demonize, dehumanize, or incite — attack ideas, not people.
