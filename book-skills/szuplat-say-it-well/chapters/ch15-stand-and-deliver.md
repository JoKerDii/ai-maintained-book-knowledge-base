# Chapter 15: Stand and Deliver

## Core Idea
On the day of your speech, get in the zone and deliver — but remember what matters most is not your body language, it's your message. You can be a great communicator even if you can't stand, deliver, or speak.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Get in the Zone (pre-speech)**: Let Your Clothes Do the Talking (attire reinforces message; but wear what feels safe); Make Some Friends (mingle beforehand so you're speaking *with* people, not strangers); Keep Correcting… but Stop Editing (fix errors until you speak, but no major surgery — "final final"); Count Your Pages (and number them; carry the speech even if memorized); Your Nerves Are Natural (fight/flight/freeze/appease); Just Breathe (box breathing 4-4-4-4); Channel Your Anxiety into Excitement (emotional reappraisal — say "I am excited," not "I am calm"); Get Pumped (a hype song).
- **Nail It (at the podium)**: Smile! (write SMILE! atop your draft — smiling is contagious and builds likability/trust); Look 'em in the Eyes (the only place to look; move person to person across the room); Vary Your Voice (~150 wpm; lower to pull in, raise to stress, slow to emphasize, speed for energy, pause for effect, be okay with a moment of silence over "ums"); Body Language: Don't Overthink It.
- **Two Rules for Body Language**: (1) Relax — carry yourself as in conversation (stand straight, stay loose, don't lock knees, don't flail or pound the podium); (2) Let Your Body Match Your Words — simple natural gestures ("Thank you from the bottom of my heart" + hand on heart).
- **Keep Calm and Carry On**: Water at hand; stumble → don't apologize, breathe, find your place; audience flat → tell a story or ask a question; tech fail → own it, make a joke.
- **End With Four Words**: "Thank you very much" — expresses gratitude and cues the applause.

## Key Concepts
- **Emotional reappraisal**: Anxiety and excitement feel physically identical; relabel "I am excited" to speak longer and seem more persuasive, competent, confident (Alison Wood Brooks).
- **Illusion of transparency (recap)**: "How we feel is often not how we look to our audience."
- **The 7-38-55 myth**: The claim that 93% of communication is nonverbal is a misreading — the study was only about identifying *emotions*; the author issued a clarification. What you say matters far more than how you say it.
- **Power-pose caveat**: The original study's author later called its data "flimsy" and said she doesn't believe the effect is real — but if a pose makes you feel confident, fine.
- **What matters most**: Good body language can amplify a message but "will never save a bad speech."

## Mental Models
- "The more I sweat, the more I'll succeed" — reframe the stress response as your body preparing you.
- Talk *with* the audience as you would friends — natural, not noisy; don't yell into a good mic ("we're not in the 1800s").
- Don't obsess over gestures; you can't be authentic while managing your every move. Watch Donovan Livingston for natural body language.

## Anti-patterns
- **Overthinking body language**: Choreographing steps and hand positions — "we're giving a speech, not dancing the salsa."
- **Editing to the last second / losing pages**: Obama's Vegas speech mid-binder gap forced him to improvise; number and count pages.
- **Over-enunciating / monotone / filler words**: "pros-per-i-ty… se-cur-i-ty" sounds robotic; "um, ah, like" signals you don't know your subject.

## Worked Example
Elizabeth Bonker, nonspeaking since 15 months due to autism, learned to communicate by pointing to letters, graduated valedictorian at Rollins College, and was chosen to give the commencement address — unable to smile on cue, modulate her voice, stand still, or make eye contact. She wrote for ~50 hours ("polished every word"), "practiced" by replaying her text-to-speech software, and did a dry run walking to the podium. A computer-generated voice delivered the speech only she could give: how typing "unlocked my mind from its silent cage," how a principal once said "the retard can't be valedictorian" — "Yet today, here I stand." She dedicated her life to the world's nonspeakers with autism and closed: "God gave you a voice. Use it." Standing ovation; the speech went viral. What matters most is the message, not the delivery mechanics.

## Key Takeaways
1. Get in the zone: dress with intent, make friends, stop editing, breathe, relabel anxiety as excitement, get pumped.
2. At the podium: smile, make eye contact, and vary your voice (~150 wpm).
3. Don't overthink body language — relax and let simple gestures match your words.
4. Ignore the 7-38-55 myth: what you say matters more than how you say it.
5. Keep calm when things go wrong; own tech fails with humor.
6. End with "Thank you very much."

## Epilogue — After the Applause
You won't be perfect; a speech is just a speech, and the point is that you put yourself out there. You get better by *doing it* — more reps, more practice. You'll know your voice made a difference when the audience shows you (they laugh, nod, lean in, applaud, stand), when the world changes (deeds follow your words), and when *you* change. Obama's best moments came "when your heart and your head, the emotion and the idea, the passion and the logic come together… loose and in the moment." Speak up, and you'll know not just what to say — but how to say it well.
