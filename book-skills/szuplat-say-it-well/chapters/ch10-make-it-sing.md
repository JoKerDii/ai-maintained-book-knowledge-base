# Chapter 10: Make It Sing

## Core Idea
Just as there's more to a song than its lyrics, there's more to a speech than its words. A good speech has cadence — a beat, tempo, rhythm — that makes the audience *feel* and want to move. Write for the ear, not the eye.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Turn Your Prose Into Poetry**: Replace monotonous words with mellifluous ones here and there for surprise and memorability.
  - How: Speeches are auditory; "poets pinch words until they hurt." Use elegant words sparingly (alluring, luminous, resplendent, ephemeral, sublime…). Search "beautiful words" or ask a bot for synonyms.
- **Write Your Speech Like a Script**: Every sentence starts at the left margin, with a blank line between sentences.
  - Why: You can *see* the flow on the page; it forces the rhythm; you Build in Your Breaths (eyes darting left = instinctive breath); you See Your Sentence Structure and Vary It (mix long and short); long sentences that spill to a second line get caught and shortened.
- **Bring On the Rhythm (techniques)**: Loosen It Up (not every line at a "ten"; aim for sixes and sevens too); Create Rhythm with Repetition (a refrain/anaphora; the Rule of Three, but don't be tied to it); Amplify with Alliteration but Avoid an Avalanche (max ~3 alliterative words per sentence); Turn Your Speech Into a Song (break lines like verse to build in pauses — "there's power in a pause").

## Key Concepts
- **Cadence**: Rhythmic flow of sound — a beat, tempo; the difference between writing for the eye and for the ear.
- **Anaphora**: Starting several sentences in a row with the same word/phrase (Obama's "We, the people, still believe…"; "It was… / Yes, we can").
- **Rule of Three**: Ideas in threes please the ear ("of the people, by the people, for the people") — but four is fine if you have four.
- **The power of a pause / silence**: The notes you don't play (Obama's Miles Davis note — "Find me some silences"); a pause adds emphasis, drama, and lets meaning sink in.
- **Loosen it up**: Turn tight declaratives into conversational lines so the speech can "breathe" (the sweater metaphor).

## Mental Models
- Be unpredictable — play "Finish That Sentence": if the audience can complete your line ("we shouldn't divide people, we should ___ people"), it's a cliché; surprise them instead.
- Read every draft *out loud* — hear how "graceful" sounds graceful; if it doesn't sing, it isn't done.
- Vary sentence length deliberately: a long sentence immediately followed by "Yes, we can" creates the beat.

## Anti-patterns
- **Predictable applause lines**: Obama had "an allergy to applause lines"; contrived parallelism reads as packaged.
- **Alliteration avalanche**: "an absurdly assembled antic… an aggressive auditory assault" — overdone alliteration is showboating that alienates. "Always avoid annoying alliterations."
- **Everything at a "ten"**: Every sentence a barn burner exhausts the audience; give them sixes and sevens.

## Worked Example
Obama's 2008 New Hampshire primary-night speech reads flat as a paragraph online, but he delivered it with rhythm. Written like a script — each sentence on the left margin, a blank line between — the "Yes, we can" refrain stands alone (three syllables, three beats) after each longer, escalating sentence: "It was a creed written into the founding documents… Yes, we can. It was whispered by slaves and abolitionists… Yes, we can." Two refrains run at once (every sentence begins "It was…" and ends "Yes, we can"), with parallelism in the finale. Broken further into verse-like lines, the built-in pauses become audible — which is why will.i.am could literally turn it into a song. Read it aloud and you deliver it the way it was meant to be heard.

## Key Takeaways
1. Write for the ear; give your speech cadence and a beat.
2. Be unpredictable — avoid the phrase everyone can finish.
3. Swap in mellifluous words sparingly for surprise.
4. Write like a script (or a song) to see and hear the rhythm and build in breaths and pauses.
5. Vary sentence length; loosen tight lines; use refrains, the Rule of Three, and light alliteration.
