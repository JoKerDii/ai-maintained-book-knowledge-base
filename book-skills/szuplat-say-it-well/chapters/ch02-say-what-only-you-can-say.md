# Chapter 2: Say What Only You Can Say

## Core Idea
The answer to "What should I say?" is simple: give the speech only *you* can give. Personalize it; tell the stories only you can tell — especially your own. Great speakers are great storytellers, and the most compelling stories are usually personal.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The "Only You" Test**: A check for whether your remarks are truly yours.
  - When to use: After drafting, to confirm your material is personal, not generic.
  - How: Imagine someone else delivering your script. If another person could stand up, deliver it, and it still makes perfect sense, your remarks are generic — rewrite them around what only you can say.
- **Share Your [X] menu**: Match your personal story to your speaking situation — Share Your Motivation (why the cause matters to you), Share Your Spirit (your company's founding story/identity), Share Your Journey (your origin/path).

## Key Concepts
- **Authenticity premium**: Audiences want originality and authenticity — if you were invited, someone thinks you have something unique to offer.
- **The "I/me/my" audit**: Count how often a draft says "I, me, my, myself" and delete as many as you can — personal is not the same as narcissistic.
- **Motivation story**: For advocacy, never skip *why* the cause means so much to you (Nancy Brinker's sister Susan).
- **Story of self as memory advantage**: Studies show audiences remember morals/messages better inside a story; no one else has lived your life.
- **Collator problem (bots)**: Chatbot content is by design *not original* — an amalgamation; it doesn't know your life, values, or beliefs.

## Mental Models
- Think of every speech as asking: "What could *I* say that no one else could say?" (the question Obama's team asked of him).
- Use personal stories as *anxiety reducers* — you know your own memories better than anyone, so they're the least scary material to deliver.
- Grounding remarks in your own experience is insurance ("It Will Save You") against a prior speaker taking your generic points.

## Anti-patterns
- **Cutting the personal story to save time**: Szuplat once cut Nancy Brinker's story of her sister Susan from a medical-conference speech; Brinker: "Susan is our story. It's what sets us apart." Never cut your differentiator.
- **Generic corporate boilerplate**: Describing a bootstrapped veteran-staffed firm as "a consulting firm and systems integrator" offering "data solutions" — the story a hundred others tell.
- **Outsourcing personal remarks to AI**: Feeding a bot enough prompts to personalize a toast takes longer than just writing it yourself.

## Worked Example
"John," a Virginia CEO, felt clients didn't grasp what made his $200M satellite-comms firm different. Szuplat immersed himself, then peppered John with questions: Why did you start it? How did it feel? Were you afraid to fail? He learned John built the company in his basement with $25,000 of personal savings, staffed largely by military veterans supplying gear to comrades still in uniform. They swapped "systems integrator / data solutions" for "founded by a husband-and-wife team… operating out of our basement… still driven by the same entrepreneurial spirit," employees described as "proud veterans" whose "lives once depended on" the technology. Result: better morale, clearer differentiation, a 50% sales boost, and a successful acquisition — "Good messaging and good financials go hand in hand."

## Key Takeaways
1. Give the speech only you can give — tell the stories only you can tell.
2. Apply the Only You Test; if anyone could deliver it, rewrite.
3. In a job interview, replace generic answers with short specific stories that prove the skill.
4. For a toast/eulogy, tell 2-3 brief stories that capture the person's essence.
5. As an advocate/executive/candidate, lead with your motivation, founding, or origin story.
6. Thought leadership is earned: do the work, get results, *then* tell the story of how.
