# Chapter 9: Talk Like a Human

## Core Idea
Avoid jargon, buzzwords, and bureaucratese that confuse or exclude your audience. Talk like a human — clear, everyday language, because we're humans talking to other humans and a speech is heard, not read.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The BBQ Rule**: If you wouldn't say it at a BBQ with your family (grandma, crazy uncle, 13-year-old niece), don't say it in a speech.
  - How: Use everyday words; many speeches (even State of the Unions) are written at ~8th-grade level, the average American reading level. Instead of "utilize" say "use"; "align" → "coordinate"; "leverage" → "harness"; "a paradigm shift" → "a change." (Full swap table in the book.)
- **Talk About People, Not Programs (Get Real)**: Talk about outcomes and the people you help, not inputs/processes/concepts ("we'll save children's lives," not "this initiative will reduce infant mortality").
- **SWAP — Stop With Acronyms that Perplex**: Someone always won't know them; "if you have to explain it, don't say it."

## Key Concepts
- **Curse of Knowledge**: Assuming your audience has the same knowledge and vocabulary you do (the "That's a fancy word for bruising" problem).
- **Speak for the ear (contractions)**: Avoid *negative* contractions ("can't" sounds like "can," "didn't" like "did") — spell out "cannot/did not"; use positive contractions to sound human and emotional ("I'm excited").
- **Double negatives**: The enemy of clarity ("not asymptomatic") — the only word remembered is the opposite of what you mean.
- **Legalese**: A speech is not a legal memo; write for the court of public opinion, not a court of law ("we achieved a favorable result" vs. "we won").
- **Loftiness**: You're not a president giving an inaugural — "Let's," not "Let us," except on the most solemn occasions; be colloquial ("Clearly…" not "Let there be no doubt…").

## Mental Models
- Can't find a plain word for your jargon? Ask a bot: "Give me ten simpler words for ___."
- Exception: if your entire audience is subject-matter experts, speak their technical language — "rock their world."
- Speak *with* your audience, not *at* them (Obama's Selma speech was "colloquial," not lofty).

## Anti-patterns
- **Word salad / bafflegab**: "creating synergy so we can catalyze solutions at scale… a real paradigm shift" — jargon breeds mistrust and signals you either aren't candid or don't know your subject.
- **Programs over people**: "redefine personal mobility" / "a new global compact on human mobility" — similar words, no humanity.
- **Dangerous acronyms**: WTF (Wisconsin Tourism Federation), DOA (Department of Aging), SLUT (South Lake Union Trolley).

## Worked Example
Ellen Moy runs The Clothesline for Arlington Kids, giving donated clothes free to children in need. Appealing for donations and volunteers, she was "too clinical" — focused on "the business model," "the store," "the process." Working with Szuplat, she reimagined it around people and purpose: a "welcoming, fun, retail-like" setting where kids pick "a season's worth of quality clothing," "staffed entirely by local volunteers… neighbors helping neighbors." Most of all a human story: "This is about more than giving a child a coat… It's about kids feeling comfortable going to school so they can learn. It's about dignity." The new message recruited volunteers and staff and clothed thousands of kids.

## Key Takeaways
1. Apply the BBQ Rule — everyday words everyone understands.
2. Talk about people and outcomes, not programs, inputs, and processes.
3. SWAP — cut perplexing acronyms.
4. Avoid negative contractions and double negatives; write for the ear.
5. Skip legalese and lofty language; be colloquial and speak *with* your audience.
