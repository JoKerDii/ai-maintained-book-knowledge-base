# Intermission: Decerebrate Cats

## Core Idea
An organisation that has stopped attending to some kinds of information looks alive and functional but has lost the ability to act purposively — it survives only while its environment stays unchanged, and collapses at the first real shock.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Decerebrate Organisation Test**: Sever a cat's cerebellum from the rest of its brain and it still walks, rights itself, eats and grooms — but it can no longer generate its own responses to change, and needs sheltering from the everyday world. Organisations do the same when systems 4 and 5 are hollowed out.
  - When to use: Assessing whether an employer, client or division is quietly losing its higher functions.
  - How: Check what the organisation still tracks versus what it has stopped tracking. Warning sign: attention runs only to immediate surroundings — this quarter's revenue, current staffing level — and planning capacity has disappeared.
  - Why it works / failure mode: It works because it separates apparent liveness from viability: normal operations are no evidence of System 4/5 health. The failure mode is a shock arriving before the missing functions are re-established — at which point the organisation must find resources from somewhere to rebuild them, or simply collapse.

## Key Concepts
- **Decerebrate organisation**: One that still runs its operations competently but has lost the intelligence and identity functions, so it cannot plan or respond to novelty.
- **Purposive action**: Behaviour generated from an internal model of a changing future, not just reaction to present stimulus — the thing lost first.
- **Active decerebration**: An organisation in the process of stripping out its own higher functions, usually as a cost saving.
- **Environmental compatibility**: The decerebrate system survives exactly as long as nothing major changes around it.
- **Startling metaphor as teaching device**: Beer's habit of dropping a shocking image into a lecture to make a structural point land.

## Mental Models
- Think of a company that only reports on this quarter as a cat that can walk and eat but not decide where to go.
- Use "is it decerebrate or just calm?" when an organisation looks stable — stability may only mean the environment hasn't moved yet.
- Think of a restructuring that deletes planning, research and horizon-scanning roles as self-decerebration in progress.

## Anti-patterns
- **Cutting the higher functions to save overhead**: Systems 4 and 5 produce no directly measurable output, so they are the easiest to cut and the ones whose absence is invisible until a crisis.
- **Reading operational competence as health**: The decerebrate cat grooms itself; day-to-day performance says nothing about adaptive capacity.

## Worked Example
Beer's stock example of a decerebrate organisation was a university: operations run smoothly — teaching happens, terms begin and end, the accounts balance — while the capacity to decide what the institution is for and where it is going has quietly gone. Such a body coasts indefinitely in a stable environment. When a genuine shock arrives (funding regime change, a collapse in demand), it has no function capable of generating a response, so it enters crisis. From there only two outcomes exist: it finds resources from somewhere to re-establish the missing intelligence and identity functions, or it collapses. Davies adds the practitioner's corollary from personal experience: when you can see a company actively decerebrating itself, update your resume and start calling recruiters.

## Key Takeaways
1. Judge organisational health by what it attends to, not by whether operations are running.
2. Treat the disappearance of planning and horizon-scanning as an early warning, not an efficiency gain.
3. A decerebrate organisation's crisis is always deferred, never avoided.
4. If you can see the decerebration happening from inside, leave.
