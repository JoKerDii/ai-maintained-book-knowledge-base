# Chapter 10: What Is to Be Done?

## Core Idea
Quieten the signal that drowns out all the others — make anyone taking control of an operating company guarantee its debts — and corporate decision-making can return to its natural equilibrium, which is viability rather than maximisation; then stop letting a maximising discipline sit at the top of the world's governance, and build real channels for the decided-upon to signal intolerability.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Restoring the polarity**: Return to the pre-Friedman arrangement in which the corporate sector provides a stabilising function, insuring the working class against fluctuations in the business cycle rather than expecting them to soak up the volatility.
  - When to use: Whenever you are deciding which party in a system should carry variance.
  - How: Recover the models lost in the mathematical revolution — Galbraith's "technostructure" (a complex category spanning business, government, military and media, working to maintain overall stability, where executives' main priority is handling uncertainty and advertising is simply demand-side planning), and Herbert Simon's behavioural theory of the firm (profits are not the goal to be maximised but a constraint on behaviour; managers do just enough to keep investors happy, then spend the rest of their effort on new things they can be proud of).
  - Why it works / failure mode: Galbraith and Simon were not writing polemics about how firms ought to behave — they were describing what was in front of them. Without the Friedman doctrine and very great re-engineering of corporate finance, the industrial economy might simply have developed into a technostructure. If that is right, the fix is easier than it looks: change the terms of the battle between capital and the managerial class.
- **Guarantee the debts (the single private-equity reform)**: Of Elizabeth Warren's eight-part plan to reform private equity, only one point is needed cybernetically — any entity taking control of an operating company should have to guarantee its debts.
  - When to use: As the model case of fixing a system by closing the accountability sink rather than by writing rules.
  - How: Do not try to regulate "excessive" borrowing or distinguish companies by their owners — the variety of financial situations a company can be in far exceeds anything writable into a rule, and the law of requisite variety tells you that project is doomed. Instead find where feedback is amplified, attenuated or removed. Limited liability is the accountability sink that lets buyers take on debt and extract cash knowing bad news cannot travel upstream to the entity that decided. Close that and most of the attraction of buying a company with its own money disappears. A rule on exceptional dividends may be added but probably isn't necessary.
  - Why it works / failure mode: Limited liability is a resource bargain, not a principle of natural justice — introduced for US joint-stock corporations in the 1820s to reach a larger pool of savings for industrialisation — and bargains get reviewed when they cause trouble. Keep its original purpose, letting small non-controlling investors help buy industrial capital; there is no need to allow further levels of limited liability on top.
- **Viability over maximisation**: Corporations are decision-making systems, not intelligences; they have no motives. What looks like a profit motive is an imbalance between the two key higher functions, here-and-now versus there-and-then — the long-term planning and intelligence function cannot be heard because short-term planning operates under the constraints of the financial market disciplinary system.
  - When to use: Whenever you are tempted to attribute monomania or malice to an organisation.
  - How: Do not attribute motivation — that is assuming things about the internal workings of the black box, the original intellectual sin of cybernetic analysis. Use POSIWID instead: the purpose of the system is what it does. Then locate the amplified signal — either a survival condition based on a monthly interest bill, or the implicit threat of takeover — and remove it. Viable systems fundamentally seek stability, not maximisation.
- **The three-step argument against economists in charge**: (1) Any system set up to maximise a single objective has the potential to go bonkers. (2) Every decision-making system set up as a maximiser needs a higher-level system watching over it. (3) Therefore you can't have the economists in charge, not in the way they currently are.
  - When to use: Any time you hand responsibility to an algorithm — literal, or a part-human system instructed to maximise something.
  - How: Part of the resource bargain must include making oversight capability available, and providing a red handle to pull — a way for the decided-upon to indicate intolerability. Since the top level of an autonomously operating system cannot itself be a maximiser, the governing philosophy of the economy cannot rest on constrained optimisation.

## Key Concepts
- **Technostructure**: Galbraith's professional and managerial class spanning business, government, military and media, keeping control of productive resources by showing up to do a job nobody else wants, and maintaining stability by communicating with one another.
- **Red-handle alert**: A channel by which those subject to a decision can signal that an outcome is unbearable, triggering urgent research and question-asking from the top.
- **Generative art as a purposive non-maximiser**: Eno's *77 Million Paintings* recombines a few elements under a small set of rules; it makes decisions, progresses and has a purpose, but its purposiveness consists in exploring possibilities rather than maximising anything.
- **Ultrastability**: The key cybernetic property of being able to find a steady state in response to conditions the designers did not anticipate.
- **POSIWID**: The purpose of a system is what it does — the only defensible reason to say an oil company's purpose is destroying life on Earth.
- **Requisite-variety doomed-project test**: Checking whether Ashby's law has been respected is a great way of spotting a doomed project — and it distinguishes failure through insufficient resources from failure because the thing is impossible.
- **Resource bargain**: The fundamental unit of governance for a viable system — the means by which a subsystem is granted decision-making autonomy so long as its decisions don't affect broader systemic stability.
- **Only cybernetic problems are soluble**: If a problem is organisational, its complexity comes from the humans in the organisation, so those humans necessarily have enough variety to match it — the problem can't be bigger than the people whose misdirected efforts created it. Only non-cybernetic problems can genuinely be insoluble.
- **Economics as dentistry**: Keynes' benchmark — economists could count their discipline a success when regarded as useful and competent technicians.
- **Transformer network as variety amplifier**: A modern AI system can compress a large text into a summary and expand a short instruction into a long explanation — practically designed for two-way communication between a mass audience and a smaller decision-making system.

## Mental Models
- Think of businesses as artists, not paperclip maximisers — an artist doesn't succeed by maximising their art, but by repeatedly producing work they're proud of.
- Use "if you can hear the problem, you can hear the solution" with caution: naming the broken channel is real progress, but there is no slider for adding capacity, and organisation is not a scalar quantity, so you must talk about specific changes.
- Use the viable system model to check a proposal, not to generate one — it tells you what to think about, whether you're addressing the right problem, and whether the system can survive the change; it won't have the idea for you.
- Think of the debt burden as a shrieking klaxon: quieten it and you change the corporation's information environment, and managers — who already spend far more time talking to customers and employees than to investors — can act on what they hear.

## Anti-patterns
- **Regulating excessive leverage by rule**: The variety of financial situations exceeds anything writable into a regulation; requisite variety says the project is doomed before you start.
- **Attributing motives to organisations**: Assuming things about the inside of the black box; corporations have homeostatic forces and reorganisation capacity, not cravings.
- **Leaving a maximiser unsupervised**: Define an objective function and you throw away all other information; the environment will change and something no longer in the information set will destroy the system.
- **Demanding explainability as the price of automation**: We cannot afford the luxury of insisting an identifiable human is always available to blame; important decisions will be made by actual robots, and that is a consequence of complexity, not a choice.
- **Using new AI to make existing structures run faster**: A generational shame — like bringing back Shakespeare, Machiavelli and Napoleon and setting them to design tax forms.
- **Using electronic communication as better shouting**: Social media could carry grassroots information into decision-making, but a big change in complexity requires an equally significant reorganisation, and so far we've done none of it.

## Worked Example
Take the private equity fix end to end. You cannot ban buying a company you think you could run better, and you cannot stop a company borrowing — taking on debt, even risking bankruptcy, is often correct and necessary. Rule-writing fails the requisite-variety test. So instead ask where feedback is amplified, attenuated or removed. It is removed at limited liability: the buyer takes on debt, extracts cash, and if things go badly the information cannot travel upstream to the entity that made the decision — the little people absorb it. Impose one requirement: any entity taking control of an operating company guarantees that company's debts. Now a bad deal reaches the investor. The point of buying a company with its own debt-servicing capacity evaporates. Limited liability survives in its original 1820s form, letting small non-controlling investors help finance industrial capital — which is where its real benefit always was.

## Key Takeaways
1. Fix systems by closing the accountability sink, not by writing rules that must out-vary the situations they govern.
2. Make any entity taking control of an operating company guarantee its debts — one change, and most of Friedmanism loses its enforcement mechanism.
3. Set organisations a survival condition, not a maximisation target; viable systems seek stability, and their natural equilibrium is likely less hostile to human life.
4. Pair every maximiser you deploy with a higher-level supervisor and a red handle for the decided-upon; keep the top level of any autonomous system a non-maximiser.
5. Demote economics from governing philosophy to competent technical trade — its optimisation toolkit stays useful; its constrained-optimisation dogma cannot sit at the top, and its own non-maximising traditions (Galbraith, Simon) are already there to draw on.
6. Use AI as a variety amplifier for two-way communication between populations and decision-makers — summarising up, expanding down — rather than as an accelerator of existing structures.
7. Accept that robots will make important decisions and stop demanding a human to blame; repair the feedback link instead, since people are far less angry when they feel listened to. "I blame the system" will have to be said literally.
