# Intermission: Meanwhile, in Chile

## Core Idea
Cybersyn was not an attempt to run a command economy by computer — it was a large-scale "management by exceptions" system built on the viable system model, and it was so badly out of scale to the Chilean economy that it could never have been a fair test.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Cybersyn (three components)**: A national management-by-exceptions architecture: Cybernet (a network of several hundred repurposed telex machines carrying production data), Cyberstride (statistical software that judged whether incoming data fell in the normal range of variability and escalated departures), and the Operations Room (an ergonomic command centre for about a dozen managers with wall displays, from which instructions went back to factories). Checo, a Chilean economic simulator, was planned but never delivered.
  - When to use: When a central body must supervise far more operating units than it can attend to individually.
  - How: Instrument every unit with a cheap, reliable, record-generating data channel; run all incoming data through a statistical filter; escalate to human debate only the exceptions; have the small human forum negotiate resource bargains rather than issue every instruction.
  - Why it works / failure mode: It works because the filter matches the variety of the incoming data to the limited attention of the room — the automated, expanded version of Beer's hand-drawn steelworks charts. It fails when total channel bandwidth is grossly mismatched to the environment being regulated, and when the act of installing the system provokes resistance from the very operations it needs cooperation from.
- **Cybersyn mapped onto the viable system model**: Operations Room = System 3 (negotiating and renegotiating the bargains with operations); Cybernet = partial System 2 (preventing clashes, coordinating resources); Cyberstride = variety engineering matching Operations Room capacity to data inflow; Checo = intended System 4 (future and outside shocks entering the debate).
  - When to use: As a template for auditing whether a proposed control system has all the functions it needs.
  - How: Name which component performs each System role; a missing role is a predicted failure point.

## Key Concepts
- **Management by exceptions**: Escalating only statistically abnormal signals to human deliberation, leaving normal variation unremarked.
- **Cyberstride**: The statistical filter that distinguished a temporary blip from a change in trend from a new level of normality, and escalated accordingly.
- **Cybernet**: The telex network that turned out to be the most practically useful part of the system — during a trucking strike it let the government monitor deliveries and secure essential supplies.
- **Variety amplifier (violence as)**: Violence is a horribly effective variety amplifier — one guard with a gun controls a prison; a state overloaded by its political environment and lacking consensual legitimacy is highly likely to reach for atrocity as the only remaining route to stability.
- **Interventors**: Officials placed inside nationalised factories passing on central government orders — unpopular, and creating an impression of central technocratic control rather than devolved autonomy.
- **Out of scale**: A control system whose channel capacity plainly cannot match the variety of what it regulates — a few hundred telex machines against an industrial economy does not pass the laugh test.
- **Provoked reaction**: Building a regulator changes the behaviour of the system it is meant to regulate.

## Mental Models
- Think of Cyberstride as an automated, national-scale version of a trellis of hand-drawn control charts: same function, more inputs.
- Use the requisite-variety laugh test when someone proposes a control system: compare its channel capacity to the variety it claims to regulate before evaluating anything else.
- Think of the biggest constraint on a new control system as cooperation from the field, not technology.

## Anti-patterns
- **Reading Cybersyn as computerised central planning**: Both neoliberals and socialists use this myth. Management cybernetics already shows a centrally planned economy cannot respect the law of requisite variety or get the information it needs.
- **Installing central data feeds while also installing central order-givers**: The interventors undercut the devolved-autonomy logic the model depended on.
- **Scaling a control system faster than management capacity**: Chile nationalised faster than it could manage, without an overall industrial plan.

## Worked Example
Production data left each factory by telex over Cybernet — machines found lying idle in a government building, far more reliable than phoning numbers through and automatically leaving a record. Cyberstride ingested that stream and asked, statistically, whether each figure sat inside the normal range of variability; where it did not, it classified the departure as blip, trend change, or new normal and escalated accordingly. Only escalated items reached the Operations Room, where roughly a dozen managers debated with charts on the walls (hand-drawn on slides and projected — Beer claimed CRT flicker was distracting) and sent instructions back. During a trucking strike the Cybernet layer alone let the government track deliveries and keep essential supplies moving. Then the Pinochet coup ended the experiment; unlike Soviet cybernetics, it did not even get the chance to fail informatively.

## Key Takeaways
1. Build the exception filter before the command centre — the room is only useful if something else decides what it looks at.
2. Judge any control proposal first on channel capacity versus environmental variety; scale mismatch invalidates everything downstream.
3. Expect the regulated system to react to being regulated; budget for winning cooperation from the field.
4. The cheapest, most boring component (a telex network) may deliver the most real value; instrumentation beats the war room.
5. A system killed before it could be tested teaches nothing — design experiments that can fail legibly.
