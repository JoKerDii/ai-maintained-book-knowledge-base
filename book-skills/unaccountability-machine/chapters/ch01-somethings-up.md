# Chapter 1: 'Something's Up'

## Core Idea
The polycrisis of the twenty-first century is at root a crisis of accountability: organisations have systematically built **accountability sinks** — combinations of a human shield plus an unappealable policy — that absorb negative feedback, and the resulting inability to learn from unanticipated situations guarantees periodic catastrophic failure.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The accountability sink**: A constructed arrangement in which there is nobody to blame, because a decision is delegated to a rule book and the human presenting it cannot change it.
  - When to use: Whenever a bad outcome has no owner, or when you are designing a policy that will produce unpopular decisions.
  - How: An effective sink requires a *combination*: (1) a person (often hourly paid) who conveys the decision but has no authority over it; plus (2) a policy that there is no route of appeal to a higher level of management. The policy exists precisely so the CEO never has to make the decision personally. The diagnostic test: is there any information from the affected person that could change the outcome? If not, it's a sink.
  - Why it works / failure mode: It works because it breaks the feedback link, which is the whole point — the decision must be fully determined by policy, so it cannot be altered by information that wasn't anticipated. That is exactly its failure mode: every rule is a model of the world, and no model covers the whole world, so when an unanticipated input arrives, the mismatch produces gruesome or absurd outcomes with nobody able to intervene.
- **The fundamental law of accountability**: The extent to which you are able to change a decision is precisely the extent to which you can be accountable for it, and vice versa.
  - When to use: Assessing whether a person or role is genuinely accountable; auditing any "accountability" reform.
  - How: Ask what the person can actually change. If they can override, they're accountable. If someone *can* overrule a policy heading for a disgusting outcome, that person is potentially accountable for the outcome — which is why sinks remove that power.
  - Why it works: It makes accountability an information/authority property rather than a moral posture, so it can be checked structurally rather than asserted.
- **The principle of diminishing accountability** (offered, then deliberately qualified): Unless conscious steps are taken to prevent it, any organisation in a modern industrial society will tend to restructure itself so as to reduce the personal responsibility attributable to its actions — and will do so until crisis results.
  - When to use: Forecasting institutional drift over decades.
  - How: Expect anomalies and inconsistencies to accumulate in inflexible rules, then a catastrophic handback — "like a self-driving car handing back control to the owner at the worst possible moment."
- **Good vs. bad accountability sinks (the common-law test)**: Not all sinks are pathological; judge them by whether the feedback link is completely broken.
  - When to use: Before "fixing" a sink by restoring managerial discretion.
  - How: The common law is a sink — you can't argue with a judge — but it develops by feedback: precedent weighted by the seniority and reputation of the judges who set it, and judicial promotion takes account of the decisions made over time. A sink is *bad* when there is no such slow reputational feedback channel at all. Fundamentalism (and strict constructionism) is the attempt to remove that channel and treat a law book as algorithmic judgement.

## Key Concepts
- **Accountability sink**: An arrangement whose defining property is that there is nobody to blame, achieved by combining a powerless human interface with an unappealable policy.
- **Polycrisis** (Adam Tooze): Umbrella term for the structurally similar crises of the twenty-first century; its defining characteristic is a loss of control by the previously existing hierarchy, often preceded by a high-profile mistake by the professional-managerial class into which populists and merchants of rage step.
- **Criminogenic organisation** (Bill Black): An institution whose incentives and management systems are structurally designed to ensure crimes will be committed.
- **Control fraud** (Bill Black): A bank taken over by a criminal who installs incentives ensuring fraudulent loans and declared profits, with all the actual cash transfers legitimate — dividends, salaries, options.
- **Self-organising control fraud** (the author's coinage): A system so criminogenic that banks organise themselves into fraudulent behaviour with no connection between bosses and low-level crooks — executives need only set unrealistic targets and underinvest in compliance.
- **Parapolitics** (Peter Dale Scott): The part of the government system in which the possibility of accountability is intentionally diminished.
- **Deep state**: Originally Turkish shorthand for the army/media/industry network; adopted for the part of the state that operates by removing itself from accountability.
- **Kings vs. priests**: Two ancient authority types — a king's orders can be argued against and changed; a priest interprets the Word, so his decisions are far harder to reverse.
- **Crumple zone (of policy)**: The function of a sink in shielding an individual manager from a disastrous decision, as a car's crumple zone shields a driver.

## Mental Models
- Think of every rule as a model of the world — a relationship between causes and effects, inputs and outputs — which necessarily leaves out detail and therefore encodes assumptions about which situations can arise.
- Use "who can override this?" as the single question that locates accountability in any organisation.
- Think of the criminogenic organisation as a special case of the structurally bad decision-making organisation.
- Use the king/priest distinction when diagnosing public rage: discontent grows when structures designed for king-like leaders are handed to managers who behave like priests.

## Anti-patterns
- **Setting a general policy without an exceptions mechanism**: Policies exist to cut the effort of deciding case by case; but unless you build a system for making exceptions *and ensure it is used*, you must be confident every enforced outcome is acceptable — and you won't be.
- **Relying on frontline disobedience as the only safeguard**: It is neither psychologically plausible nor managerially realistic to expect someone to follow orders 99 per cent of the time and then act independently on the hundredth.
- **Importing a placeholder number as a binding constraint**: Junior officials' round numbers become continental policy because reopening them would require someone to risk being blamed for a decision.
- **Scapegoating an external body (the IMF pattern)**: Clever in a journal, indefensible to a hungry crowd — the absence of accountability is as intolerable to the decided-upon as accountability is to deciders.
- **Naive de-sinking**: Restoring middle-manager discretion reintroduces caprice, unfairness and racism — many policies exist precisely to prevent those.

## Worked Example
In April 1999 a cargo of 440 ground squirrels landed at Schiphol on a KLM flight from Beijing without import papers. They could not go on to Athens and nobody could send them back. Staff put all 440 through an industrial shredder built for disposing of worthless male chicks; over previous months the same machine had taken 200 more squirrels, dozens of turtles and a flock of parakeets. Trace the structure: the Dutch agriculture ministry held a sensible-looking policy — no veterinary paperwork means return or euthanasia at the importer's expense — covering the overwhelming majority of cases. KLM had delegated the decision to that ministry. Everyone had implicitly accepted a low baseline of destruction, which is why the shredder was bought at all, on the assumption it would function mostly as a threat. The pet-squirrel craze was the unanticipated input. No channel existed to feed the results back to the people who could change the policy, so decisions kept getting worse until the story broke — and then there was nobody to blame. KLM's employees had acted "formally correctly"; their only alleged fault was an "assessment mistake," i.e. failing to disobey.

## Key Takeaways
1. Test any process for accountability by asking what the frontline person can change; if nothing, you have a sink regardless of the org chart.
2. When writing a policy, write down the assumptions about the world it encodes, then ask what happens when each fails.
3. Build an explicit exceptions route and monitor that it is actually used — an unused escalation path is not a safeguard.
4. Build a slow reputational feedback channel (the common-law model) into any sink you must keep, so precedent can evolve.
5. Expect crises on a schedule: inflexible rules accumulate anomalies during calm periods, then fail catastrophically — the Minsky-shaped dynamic.
6. Recognise legitimate reasons sinks exist — legal liability shields, insurance underwriting requirements, and the avoidance of corrosive interpersonal conflict — before proposing removal.
7. Watch for numbers with no analysis behind them (the 3 per cent deficit rule; the 2 per cent inflation target) becoming fundamental constraints.
