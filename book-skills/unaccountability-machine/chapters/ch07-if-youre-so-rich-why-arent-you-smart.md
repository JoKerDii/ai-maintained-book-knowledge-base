# Chapter 7: If You're So Rich, Why Aren't You Smart?

## Core Idea
Managers are the System 1 of a structure whose higher functions are supplied by economists — but the channel between them is broken, because economists don't speak accounting, the language of management. Inside firms, financial reporting numbers built for one purpose get used for decisions they were never designed for, so companies begin to hallucinate.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Management Accounting vs Financial Reporting**: Management accounting measures process efficiency and allocates costs to specific decisions; financial reporting summarises the whole enterprise for the people who finance it, on standard periods and consistent conventions.
  - When to use: Before any make-or-buy, product-line, or outsourcing decision.
  - How: Ask what decision each number was collected for. Financial reporting attenuates: it forces costs into fixed calendar periods rather than the control horizon of the decision, matches costs to units of output for inventory valuation, and allocates overheads by convention (marketing, interest and the CEO's salary as "period costs"; direct labour and materials as unit costs). Never let those conventions drive a management decision.
  - Why it works / failure mode: "Generally accepted accounting principles" is a phrase that should immediately signal an accountability sink. The failure mode is a company making real decisions on numbers whose allocation rules were chosen for consistency and audit, not truth.
- **The Attributability Test for Cost Allocation** (the accountants' version of the fundamental law of accountability): The extent to which a cost can be attributed to an output is exactly the extent to which the cost could be avoided if you weren't producing it.
  - When to use: Setting up cost allocation, or contesting an allocation imposed on you.
  - How: For each cost ask "would this disappear if we stopped making this?" Then ask whether the manager charged with it is actually accountable for that part of the resource bargain — allocating costs a manager cannot influence is pure harm.
  - Why it works / failure mode: It ties allocation to the resource bargain rather than to convention. But whether a cost is fixed or variable is itself a strategic decision — almost any cost is variable if you'll rearrange the business — so disagreements about allocation numbers are really disagreements about the production process.
- **How Ricardians Win Arguments — They Collect the Numbers**: Whoever designs the data collection decides what is measured, and therefore what environmental variety is ignored; they also decide how it is classified and presented.
  - When to use: Any argument where one side has "the numbers" and the other has case studies.
  - How: Note that a single death can be encoded as liver failure (medical), mercury poisoning (causal) or murder (criminological). Ask who commissioned the data and for what. Look for data collected for a different purpose, or for an internal inconsistency in the model — these are the only two reliable ways to win.

## Key Concepts
- **Accounting as the language of management**: The system that must negotiate Beer's blurry line between System 2 (making coordinated activity possible) and System 3 (deciding what the organisation does).
- **Camouflaging a decision as a necessity**: The attraction of the System 2/System 3 ambiguity — and a great accountability sink.
- **Period costs vs unit costs**: The convention that marketing, interest and executive pay attach to a time period while labour and materials attach to output and sit on the balance sheet in inventory.
- **Business hallucination**: A firm acting on a picture of itself generated by reporting conventions rather than by its actual production process.
- **Einstein reads the phone book**: Beer's joke about hiring humanity's greatest geniuses to memorise a directory — what companies did in the 1960s by computerising their existing paper bookkeeping instead of rethinking it.
- **The decision nobody made**: When 1960s computing arrived, no one chose to keep financial reporting as the basis of control; it simply happened, with huge consequences.
- **Management consultants as accountability sinks**: Professional scapegoats hired when management must do something that would destroy their ability to manage afterwards.
- **Brains for hire**: The consultancy pitch that you can economise on management capacity by renting it — which fails because reproducing the middle managers' expertise costs the same hours at higher rates.
- **The Chandler pattern**: Every classic of management science describes a way of adapting structure to a more complicated world, then becomes obsolete as complexity outruns it again.
- **Machiavelli's constraint**: A prince who is not wise cannot be well advised — a manager without spare analytical capacity cannot tell when subordinates' signals have gone wrong.

## Mental Models
- Think of environmental variety as growing faster than any structure's management variety: the crossover point is when you must change architecture, not work harder.
- Use "learn the numbers by looking at the business, never the business by looking at the numbers" as the test of a competent manager.
- Think of middle managers as the translation and error-correction layer between a distorted reporting channel and reality — and of cutting them as cutting translation capacity.
- Use "what did the last generation's management fad actually mean?" — management by objectives, focus on core competences and the rest all say the same thing: stop generating complexity faster than you can manage it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Deciding on financial-reporting numbers**: They were collected to summarise for financiers; the decision is what turns data into information, so numbers gathered for another purpose carry different information.
- **Allocating overheads in proportion to direct labour or floorspace**: It systematically penalises the manufacturing or bulk operation and flatters the marketing-heavy one.
- **Measuring the wrong efficiency**: A fast-food chain judged managers on "chicken efficiency" — the proportion of each bird sold. Managers stopped frying an hour before closing, lost heaps of sales, and hit 100 per cent.
- **Renting brains instead of employing them**: Consultants tackling a problem nobody inside understands must rebuild the expertise you cut, in billable hours.
- **Telling the client what they want to hear**: Better for repeat business — the problem persists and the buyer still likes you — which is why consultancy can never supply the managerial class its identity function.
- **Trusting an accounting system as objective**: It is an almost perfect accountability sink; even the people who built it don't necessarily understand what they're doing.

## Worked Example
A factory makes two skincare lines from essentially the same cream: bulk barrels for construction workers, and small tubs sold to the beauty market at ten times the price. Under generally accepted principles the luxury line looks far more profitable per unit — most of its cost is marketing and advertising, treated as period costs — while the bulk line absorbs a disproportionate share of fixed packaging-line cost, allocated by direct labour. So an investor asks why the company bothers with the low-margin construction business. Consultants are brought in and compare in-house manufacturing against an external supplier — whose overheads are genuinely low because it does little marketing or R&D. In-house manufacturing, carrying the beauty division's overheads, loses the comparison. Manufacturing is sold. The marketing and research costs, of course, don't vanish; new quality-control and logistics problems appear. Two years later, consultants are hired to explain why the star product's profitability slumped.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ask what decision every number was collected for before letting it drive a different decision.
2. Allocate a cost to a manager only if the cost is avoidable by stopping the output *and* the manager controls that part of the resource bargain.
3. Treat disagreements over cost allocation as substantive disagreements about the production process, not spreadsheet quibbles.
4. When the other side has the data, look for data collected for a different purpose or for a flaw inside their model — arguing without either makes you look like a fact-denier.
5. Budget deliberately for excess management capacity; without it you cannot detect that your own information environment has degraded.
6. When complexity outgrows the structure, change the architecture so variety is matched at each level — don't add supervision.
7. Expect a consultant's real product to be a repaired feedback loop; being told your own employees' answer is the service, not the fraud.
