# Chapter 8: Enter Friedman

## Core Idea
The Friedman doctrine solved a real crisis — corporations at the end of the 1960s had lost any sense of purpose amid an explosion of environmental variety — by supplying a single maximising goal and a personal accountability sink, at the price of denying that the organisation exists as a decision-making system at all.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Friedman doctrine**: The corporation is an "artificial person" with only "artificial responsibilities", so the only possible locus of responsibility is the individual corporate manager, whose job is to increase profits for shareholders; social responsibility by a company is the moral equivalent of taxation, because it means spending other people's money and stepping from the "market mechanism" into the "political mechanism".
  - When to use: As a diagnostic lens whenever an organisation's stated purpose collapses into one financial number.
  - How: Watch for the three moves — (1) look through the corporation to individuals; (2) replace the messy actual shareholders with an abstract "representative shareholder" black box that cares only about profits; (3) invite the executive to split his moral self into judgements "as a person in his own right" and decisions "in his capacity as a businessman".
  - Why it works / failure mode: It works because it is an accountability sink at the personal level — it lets managers attribute bad consequences and lost independence to a separate work-self bound by one simple principle. It fails because it is a lie in both directions: executives are told to ignore the reality of the company and act as if hired by human beings, then to ignore the reality of human beings and act as if employed by a theoretical construct. Real shareholders number in the thousands, hold competing firms, include the workers' own pension funds, and change daily.
- **Debt as a technology of information and control**: Debt is an extremely efficient variety attenuator for the lender, who need answer only two questions — is the interest rate attractive compensation for time and risk, and will I be paid back? — and can therefore make far more investments than they could supervise.
  - When to use: When analysing why an organisation cannot attend to anything but cash.
  - How: Trace the debt load against the survival set. Small debt is one constraint among many. Heavy debt makes the payment signal swamp all other sources of information. Extreme debt — where no action can generate enough cashflow — converts debt into an instrument of control: the creditor may break the company up or extend another loan, and can exercise or withhold that power in return for concessions.
  - Why it works / failure mode: The attenuation is the point; the failure mode is that a system loaded with debt becomes incapable of paying attention to anything that does not directly produce cash for the next payment.
- **The leveraged buyout as management discipline**: Put up roughly 10 per cent of your own money, borrow the other 90 to buy the company, then cause the acquired company to borrow and use the proceeds to repay your own creditors — ending with no liabilities and an indebted company as your asset.
  - When to use: To read any situation where an owner's apparent risk is transferred onto workers, suppliers, customers and managers.
  - How: The debt burden rewrites management incentives by ultimatum rather than by appeal to duty: concentrate on profits or the company goes bust and you never get an equivalent job again. Limited liability caps the buyer's loss at the initial 10 per cent; the buyer can profit personally even from a deal that ends in bankruptcy by prioritising dividends over debt reduction.
- **Jensen's translation (managerial behaviour / agency costs / ownership structure)**: Michael Jensen (with William Meckling) put the Friedman doctrine on a rigorous mathematical footing — "managerial behaviour" names slacking and pursuing social goals with shareholders' money; "agency costs" names shareholders' difficulty checking up on managers; "ownership structure" names the fix, using debt and stock options to make managers act as if the corporation didn't exist and a personal owner were looking over their shoulder.

## Key Concepts
- **Neoliberalism (working definition)**: Shareholder primacy in corporate governance combined with deregulation, privatisation and low taxes in public policy.
- **"Fiduciary duty to maximise shareholder value"**: Legal jargon used outside its legal context; courts have never found a duty to run a company one way rather than another, let alone to maximise one class of its securities — the phrase dresses a moral exhortation in sonorous tones.
- **Semantic drift (increase → maximise, profits → shareholder value)**: The wording shifted to match economics' maximising language and to escape awkward questions about which accounting standard, what period, what risk, what trade-off between present return and future growth.
- **Financialisation**: A misnomer for a parasitic separate system — it is the growing importance of the financial aspects of the overall economic system, since finance is how resource bargains are struck, commitment to plans is made, and information is transferred.
- **Share price as verdict machine**: A real-time computing machine delivering a positive or negative judgement on management strategy, sparing managers the tricky questions about what "profits" mean.
- **Efficient market hypothesis (as ideology)**: Jensen's claim that the share price summarises all available information about a company's financial future — in effect, that the stock market literally is a computing fabric.
- **Economic Value Added**: Joel Stern's model setting a financial benchmark for investment returns to determine what should be done and what cut — letting consultants perform buyout-style management on companies unsuited to actual raiding.
- **Private equity's System 5**: The buyout industry's strong identity and ideology let it integrate information gathering with operational management under one clearly defined goal — making it a much faster, more aggressive predator than other non-human decision-making systems.
- **Capitalism versus managerialism**: The great unremarked class struggle of the 1970s and 1980s; the managers lost comprehensively.

## Mental Models
- Think of the Friedman doctrine as the system talking, not the man — the capitalist world had reached a stage where it needed Milton Friedman, and if he hadn't written the essay someone else would have.
- Use "the greatest fear of the manager is management" when explaining why a disciplinary doctrine lands: the persuasive move was not calling do-gooders thieves but forcing managers to face what they were already afraid of.
- Think of the market as the brain of the capitalist system and the debt relationship as its nerves and muscles.
- Use business schools, consultancies and journals as the internal communication system of management — that is where a cadre aligns on shared language and assumptions.

## Anti-patterns
- **Substituting the representative shareholder**: Replacing thousands of real, conflicted, rotating owners with a black box that only cares about profits, then pretending you can have the same relationships with the box.
- **Quoting "fiduciary duty to maximise shareholder value" as if from Friedman**: People cite the phrase to his essay without checking what he wrote — a reliable tell that ideology is doing the work.
- **Managing to the share price**: Pointing managers at a price rather than answering what "maximise profits" actually means; it abolishes the questions rather than resolving them.
- **Ideological drift after the target-rich phase ends**: Once the genuinely wasteful companies were bought, buyout firms expanded the definition of a valid target — unused debt tax shields, merely stable cashflow, a temporary market dip — rather than winding up.
- **Pre-emptive self-buyout**: Companies started behaving as if already under a leveraged buyout, to avoid becoming targets — spreading the pathology without the transaction.
- **Demanding only what is quantifiable**: History, community, customer trust and technology stopped mattering unless they fitted an accounting system and the right spreadsheet.

## Worked Example
A buyout firm sees a company where executives take corporate jets, country club memberships and lavish expense accounts, tolerate obsolete product lines, and lean on cosy relationships with equally sclerotic large customers. The firm puts up 10 per cent, borrows 90, buys the company, then makes the company borrow to repay the acquisition debt. Lenders were cheap precisely because they knew this repayment was coming. Interest is tax-deductible where dividends are not, converting a decade of taxable income into a long-term capital gain. Now the apparent disadvantage becomes the mechanism: every priority is subordinated to generating enough cash to survive, because bankruptcy ends the executives' careers. The perks stop; the hard decisions get made. The residual risk sits with workers, suppliers, customers and managers — the little people — while limited liability caps the buyer's downside at the original 10 per cent.

## Key Takeaways
1. When an organisation loses its sense of purpose amid rising environmental variety, it will accept whatever simple identity is on offer — supply a better one before someone supplies a worse one.
2. Diagnose any "single maximising end" doctrine by asking what information it licenses you to throw away.
3. Read a heavy debt load as an information problem first: it swamps every signal that isn't cash.
4. Check whether an appeal to duty is really a legal claim; "fiduciary duty" outside its legal context is exhortation in costume.
5. Look for the personal accountability sink inside a corporate doctrine — the invitation to attribute your decisions to a separate work-self.
6. Notice that structures beat individuals: neither Friedman nor Welch is the villain; the system that replicated them is.
7. When a disciplinary practice runs out of legitimate targets, expect definition creep rather than self-liquidation.
