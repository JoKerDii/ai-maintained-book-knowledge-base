## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The accountability sink (Ch 1, 3) — the book's central object.** An arrangement whose defining property is that *there is nobody to blame*. The test is precise and not moral: **is there any information the affected person could supply that would change the outcome?** If no, it's a sink. Use it whenever you're tempted to argue with a call-centre operator or a compliance officer — they are not the decision-maker, and the decision has been made unappealable by design. Sinks are not automatically bad: they are **variety-amplification devices**, "a solution as much as a problem". Good ones retain slow reputational feedback and can be revised; bad ones are unappealable *and* unrevisable.

**The fundamental law of accountability (Ch 1).** Your ability to change a decision equals your accountability for it. Corollary — the **principle of diminishing accountability**: the more decision-making is industrialised, the less accountability survives. Prefer this to moral language: if nobody could have changed the outcome, blaming individuals is a category error.

**Ashby's Law / requisite variety (Ch 4).** Only variety can absorb variety — a regulator needs at least as many states as the system it regulates. **Variety** is the number of states a system can be in, and is *not intrinsic*: it's a property of how you choose to describe the system. **Variety engineering** = attenuating incoming variety and amplifying regulatory variety until the law is satisfied. Use this before resourcing debates: a variety mismatch is a hard constraint, and no funding, effort, or better data fixes an impossibility in principle. Davies flags this comparison — *is regulator variety greater or less than input variety?* — as the most useful advice in the book.

**The black box (Ch 2, 3).** A system defined by its behaviour, not its composition, because its internals can't safely be known. Beer's move: there is nothing to be gained by opening it — observe behaviour, iterate on feedback, refuse assumptions. Prefer black-box treatment over mechanism-hunting whenever the system is **complex** (treatable only as a whole) rather than merely **complicated** (traceable by maths).

**POSIWID — the purpose of a system is what it does (Ch 2, 5, 10).** Not cynicism: a description of how System 5 preserves viability. Use it to dissolve the conspiracy-versus-cock-up question — replace "who intended this?" with "**what structure made this the systematic output?**" Systems don't have hidden motivations, and systems don't make mistakes.

**The Viable System Model (Ch 5) — the diagnostic spine.** Any organisation capable of surviving indefinitely contains five *functions* (not org-chart boxes):
- **System 1 — Operations.** Makes change in the real world; could in principle exist independently; absorbs most environmental variety itself.
- **System 2 — Regulation.** Stops operations clashing: timetables, scheduling, the quartermaster. The management everyone agrees is necessary.
- **System 3 — Integration/optimisation.** Coordinates the System 1s toward a purpose; strikes and polices **resource bargains**; handles **residual variety** by exception. The management people complain about — and System 3 managers routinely disguise themselves as System 2.
- **System 4 — Intelligence.** The "**there and then**": attends to the parts of the environment not yet in contact with operations. Forecasting is *not* System 4 — extrapolating the current model is here-and-now extended.
- **System 5 — Philosophy/identity.** Balances 3 against 4 by fixing what kind of organisation this is, which attenuates variety by making most options unthinkable.

**Recursion and embedding (Ch 5).** Every viable system contains all five and is itself a System 1 inside a larger one. Always diagnose in *both* directions — the system this unit is embedded in, and the organisation inside its operations. A division that bounces crisis to crisis is usually an unrecognised System 1 denied its own internal 2/3/4/5.

**Management by exception + the resource bargain (Ch 5).** Push variety-handling to the lowest level that can absorb it; size System 3 to the residual variety, not the total. State what a unit gets and what it must deliver — autonomy granted up to the point of jeopardising the wider system. The two failure modes are **micromanagement** (System 3 fights System 1's variety single-handed and drowns) and **excessive delegation** (no bargains, so operations settle conflicts by politics and hoarding).

**Algedonic signals — "red handles" (Ch 5, 10).** A channel letting any level signal directly to the top, across intervening levels, that something threatens viability. Model on a train's emergency brake: physical effect *plus* an organisational message. Resource the channel **and** the translation capacity at the receiving end.

**Information vs data; translation capacity (Ch 4, 5, 9).** Variety counts as **information** only if it has a causal role in decision-making — arriving in time and in a form the receiver can act on. Otherwise it's data on a disk. Most "we had warnings" failures are translation failures, not collection failures. Before any restructure or outsourcing, trace one real signal across every boundary it crosses.

**The Ricardian Vice (Ch 6).** Proving something in a stripped-down model, then acting as if it were proved about the world. Before trusting an optimisation model, confirm all four conditions: commodity good, observable marginal cost curve, spot markets independent of later periods, negligible strategic interaction. Separate assumptions made for *tractability* from assumptions equivalent to *assuming the conclusion*.

**Accounting as variety attenuation (Ch 7).** Management accounting is tied to specific decisions; financial reporting is a periodic convention-based summary for financiers. **Business hallucination** = acting on the picture the conventions generate rather than the real process. Apply the **attributability test**: a cost is attributable exactly to the extent it would be avoided by not producing that output. Rule: learn the numbers by looking at the business, never the business by looking at the numbers.

**Reversing / restoring the polarity (Ch 8–10).** The Friedman doctrine, shareholder value, and debt-as-control-technology (the LBO) removed the corporate sector's System 4 and made the working class insure the corporate sector against the business cycle, instead of the reverse. Davies' prescription: **viability over maximisation**; require any entity taking control of an operating company to guarantee its debts; pair every maximising system with a higher-level supervisor and a red handle — the top level of an autonomous system must never itself be a maximiser.
