# Chapter 7: Interrupting Interruption and the Art of Refusal

## Core Idea
An interruption is anything that prevents start-to-finish completion of a critical task. Attack the **three sources** of interruption with **batching**, strict access limits, and a polished art of saying no. Limit access to your time; force others to define requests before they get it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The three interruption offenders**:
  1. **Time consumers** — repetitive tasks/requests that must be handled but can be grouped (email, calls, reports, errands). **Solution: batching.**
  2. **Empowerment failures** — someone needs your approval for something small before acting. **Solution: give if-then rules** so they act autonomously (e.g., "fix any problem costing < $X without asking").
  3. **Self-generated / work-for-work interruptions** — you create busywork to feel productive. **Solution: recognize and resist the impulse.**
- **Batching**: because every task has fixed setup time (up to a 45-min "switching of gears" to resume interrupted work), group like tasks and do them on a schedule (email 1–3×/day, then weekly; laundry, bills, calls, etc.).
  - How: pick a batch frequency; measure what problems the delay costs vs. hours saved; batch as far apart as the cost allows. See Worked Example.
- **The Puppy Dog Close**: to win permanent change, propose a small, reversible "let's just try it once" trial. Like taking the puppy home — the return rarely happens. Used to sell no-meeting policies, remote work, and autonomy.

## Key Concepts
- **The art of refusal**: default response to any invader — *"I'm right in the middle of something. How can I help you?"* If unclear in 30 seconds, ask them to email; give a time limit ("I've got 2 minutes before a call").
- **Autoresponders + FAQs**: deflect email so you answer only 10–20 messages/week.
- **Meetings**: only agree to meetings/calls with a clear agenda and a defined end time; propose email or a decision instead.
- **Voicemail → email**: ask people to reply by email to train concision.

## Mental Models
- **"Replace 'How are you?' with 'How can I help you?'"** — get specific, no stories, focus on the immediate action.
- **Setup cost is fixed; do many at once.** One trip, one session, one batch.
- **The interruption impulse is human — learn to recognize and fight it in yourself.**

## Anti-patterns
- **Checking email first thing / last thing / constantly** — it resets your whole day.
- **Accepting open-ended meetings** with no agenda or end time.
- **Answering questions a FAQ or autoresponder could handle.**
- **Automating or delegating an interruption you could eliminate** (see ch08).

## Worked Example — The Batching Cost/Benefit Test (email)
- Baseline: checking email daily consumes ~10 hrs/week you value at $20/hr = **$200/week of time**.
- Switch to **once per week**. Suppose the delay causes ~2 lost sales = **$80/week lost profit**.
- Net: $200 saved − $80 lost = **+$120/week**, plus the enormous value of the deep work those 10 hours now enable. → Keep the weekly batch.
- Rule: if delay costs *less* than the time saved, batch even further apart; if it costs *more*, step back to the next-less-frequent schedule (weekly → twice-weekly, **not** daily) and fix the system to return to weekly.
Ferriss checked email as rarely as once every 1–4 weeks from 2004–2007; "nothing was irreparable, and nothing cost more than $300 to fix."

## Key Takeaways
1. Classify every interruption as time-consumer, empowerment-failure, or self-generated — each has its own fix.
2. Batch like tasks; setup cost is fixed, so do them in bulk on a schedule.
3. Kill empowerment failures with written if-then rules and spending limits.
4. Use the Puppy Dog Close to win reversible trials of new policies.
5. Master the refusal script: "I'm in the middle of something — how can I help?"
