# Patterns & Techniques — Think Like a Rocket Scientist

Concrete, reusable methods Varol draws from rocket science for work and life.

## Name Your Fear (Worst-Case / Best-Case on Paper)
**When to use**: Before a scary, paralyzing leap into the unknown.
**How**: In writing, ask "What's the worst that can happen, and how likely given what I know?" AND "What's the best that can happen?" Turn unknown unknowns into known unknowns.
**Trade-offs**: The naming defangs fear (usually worse than the fact), but forces you to confront things you'd rather not see. Pair with redundancies and margins of safety for irreversible one-way decisions.

## First-Principles Teardown
**When to use**: A high-leverage problem where the conventional answer is expensive, stuck, or inherited without reason.
**How**: Break the thing to its fundamental components/physics ("What is a rocket made of?"), find the true cost/constraint of those fundamentals, then rebuild up from there instead of copying a template.
**Trade-offs**: Effortful; deploy where it matters most, not on every daily decision. Reason by analogy for the trivial.

## Question Your Assumptions ("What if this weren't true?")
**When to use**: To expose invisible rules governing your work or life.
**How**: Spend a day questioning each commitment, budget line, and process: "Why am I doing it this way? Can I get rid of this or replace it?" The best way to expose a rule is to violate it. Be suspicious when you list *multiple* reasons to keep something.
**Trade-offs**: Not everything should be questioned constantly — routines free you from decision fatigue.

## Kill-the-Company / Red Teaming
**When to use**: To surface blind spots invisible from the inside; before a big strategic bet.
**How**: Play the competitor trying to put you out of business; generate the attacks; then switch back and defend the weak points. Personal version: "Why is this employer justified in *not* hiring me?"
**Trade-offs**: Emotionally uncomfortable; requires genuinely inhabiting the adversary rather than humblebragging.

## Thought Experiments ("What if?")
**When to use**: Testing an idea, disproving an assumption, or finding a path before any physical experiment.
**How**: Construct a fictional world where things work differently, run it in imagination, follow the logic to its extreme to surface contradictions. The word *experiment* lowers the stakes — if it fails, nothing bad happens.
**Trade-offs**: The goal is to ignite inquiry, not to be immediately right; some experiments lead nowhere but still shake you off autopilot.

## Deliberate Boredom / Incubation
**When to use**: When a hard problem won't yield to direct effort.
**How**: Schedule distraction-free unstructured time — walks, showers, a "Think Week," a daydreaming chair. Switch the brain from focused to diffuse mode and let the subconscious connect ideas.
**Trade-offs**: Feels unproductive and underwhelming in an instant-gratification culture; creativity arrives as a whisper, not a bang.

## Combinatory Play (Compare Apples and Oranges)
**When to use**: A creative jam or the need for a genuinely novel solution.
**How**: Collect diverse inputs across disciplines, then connect the dissimilar. Ask "What other industry has faced this before?" Recruit cognitively diverse people (the Medici effect); cycle between solitude and collaboration.
**Trade-offs**: The fit is rarely perfect, but the act of comparing sparks new lines of thought.

## Moonshot Framing (10x not 10%)
**When to use**: When you want a breakthrough, not a marginal gain.
**How**: Set a goal so large the status quo can't reach it (eliminate all accidents, not improve safety). The bigness forces first-principles solutions and clears the crowded center where competitors chase mice.
**Trade-offs**: Requires tolerating a portfolio where most bets fail; must be paired with pragmatic execution.

## Diverge Then Converge (Fly, Then Bee)
**When to use**: Any idea-generation problem.
**How**: First be a "fly" — generate wildly, "This could be done if…," ask "What *could* you do?" and "What would a science-fiction solution look like?" Only then switch to the "bee": rapid evaluation, kill metrics, business plans. Never let judgment kill ideas before generation finishes.
**Trade-offs**: Order matters — converging first ("we've tried that, no budget") strangles originality.

## Backcasting & Monkey First
**When to use**: Planning toward an audacious goal.
**How**: Work backward from the imagined future (or happy customer — Amazon's pre-written press release) to the steps. Then tackle the hardest, riskiest part first (train the monkey before building the pedestal) and set kill metrics up front.
**Trade-offs**: Don't treat the plan as a bible ("detailed dreams blind you to new means"); the pedestal is satisfying busywork that postpones the real risk.

## Reframe the Question (Strategy vs. Tactics, Function vs. Form, Inversion)
**When to use**: When stuck, or before committing to solve any problem.
**How**: Spend more time in problem definition. Ask "What problem is this tactic here to solve?" to find the strategy. Describe tools function-free to defeat functional fixedness. Ask "What if I did the reverse?" to beat stronger competitors.
**Trade-offs**: Feels slower than jumping to answers, but the framing of the question determines the answer.

## Prove Yourself Wrong (Falsification)
**When to use**: Testing any belief before you commit.
**How**: Hold working hypotheses, not opinions (change your vocabulary). Breed multiple conflicting hypotheses. Generate cases that would *break* your rule (like the 2-4-6 game), not confirm it. Ask "What's missing? What fact would change my mind?" Seek dissent and steel-man opponents.
**Trade-offs**: Self-falsification is genuinely unpleasant (no dopamine hit), but every "no" carries more information than a "yes."

## Test as You Fly, Fly as You Test
**When to use**: Before any launch, speech, interview, or high-stakes performance.
**How**: Recreate real conditions (jittery, in an unfamiliar room, with a stranger throwing curveballs). Test to fail — hunt the breaking point and exaggerate flaws. Test the assembled system, not just parts. Observe real behavior instead of self-reports; diversify testing instruments and calibration targets.
**Trade-offs**: Harder and less comfortable than a friendly dress rehearsal, but any gap between test and flight can be catastrophic.

## Celebrate Intelligent Failure & Near-Miss Reporting
**When to use**: Building a team that must take risks and surface problems.
**How**: Reward excellent failures, punish mediocre successes. Leaders advertise their own failures repeatedly to build psychological safety. Focus on inputs, not outputs. Keep a "black box" and a "Flight Rules" catalog so lessons endure. Mine near misses — frequent, cheap, predictive.
**Trade-offs**: Requires distinguishing intelligent from sloppy failure; hold people accountable for failing to *learn*, not for failing.

## Guard Against the Success Trap
**When to use**: After success and hot streaks.
**How**: Treat everything as a permanent work in progress; drop "routine." Run postmortems after wins ("What went wrong with this success? What role did luck play?"). Run premortems before acting (assume failure, list causes, assign probabilities). Fix the cause behind the cause. Distrust the safety net (risk homeostasis) and protect dissent.
**Trade-offs**: Wanting *less* uninterrupted success feels irrational, but regularity signals hidden fragility.
