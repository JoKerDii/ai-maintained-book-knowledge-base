# Chapter 5: What If We Sent Two Rovers Instead of One?

## Core Idea
Breakthroughs begin with a smart *question*, not a smart answer. Most people rush to solve the first problem they see; the leverage is in reframing — finding the right problem, separating strategy from tactics, and asking "What if we did the reverse?"

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Problem finding (reframe before you solve)**: Resist the initial framing; search for a better question instead of a better answer. "A problem well defined is a problem half solved."
  - When to use: Whenever you're stuck, or before committing to solve any problem.
  - How: Ask "Am I asking the right question? If I changed my perspective, how would the problem change?" Zoom the "camera lens" wide or tight. Spend more time in problem definition (the most creative people do).
- **Strategy vs. tactics**: A strategy is the objective; tactics are the actions serving it. Ask of any tool, "What problem is this tactic here to solve?"
  - When to use: When you've fallen in love with a solution and defined the problem as its absence.
  - How: Abandon the *what*/*how* for the *why*. Landing safely (strategy) vs. a three-legged lander (tactic); warmth (strategy) vs. an incubator (tactic). Focusing on strategy makes you malleable with tactics.
- **Function → form (defeat functional fixedness)**: See an object by its form, not its habitual function, to unlock new uses.
  - When to use: Repurposing a resource, product, or skill.
  - How: Describe things function-free ("a thin rectangular piece of metal," not "a plug prong"); block the obvious use; apply Occam's razor to your assumptions about the tool. (Amazon reframing its infrastructure as AWS, and Whole Foods stores as distribution centers.)
- **Inversion ("What if we did the reverse?")**: Flip the question on its head — "Invert, always invert" (Jacobi).
  - When to use: Beating a stronger competitor or escaping a stuck best practice.
  - How: Do the opposite of the incumbent's expectation (Faraday reversing Ørsted; GPS reversing Sputnik trajectory math; Patagonia's "Don't buy this jacket"; the Fosbury flop). Even if you don't execute, it jolts your assumptions.

## Key Concepts
- **Einstellung effect**: A fixed mental set — once we know a familiar solution, we stop seeing better alternatives (expert chess players' eyes locked on the known 5-move mate, missing the 3-move one).
- **Sentence before the verdict**: Rushing to conclusions/answers before understanding the problem ("Sentence first, verdict afterwards" — the Queen of Hearts).
- **Functional fixedness**: A mental block against using an object in a new way (Duncker's candle problem; the box as platform, not container).
- **The five-dollar challenge**: The obvious resource ($5) is a distraction; the real asset was the 3-minute pitch slot sold to a recruiter for $650 — reframe "what's the most valuable resource?"
- **Hammer/nail fixation**: 85% of executives say their firms are bad at defining problems; they seek an advertising solution to any "advertising problem," excluding alternatives.
- **Doppler → inversion**: The same signal analysis run forward (Sputnik's trajectory) and reversed (a sub's unknown location) became GPS.

## Mental Models
- Ask **"What problem is this tactic here to solve?"** to surface the strategy hiding behind a favored tool.
- **"When you see a good move, don't make it immediately. Look for a better one."**
- Reframe from **what/how to why** — fall in love with the problem, not your solution.
- **Function-free description** to dissolve functional fixedness; bring in outsiders who aren't "distracted by the hammer in front of you."
- **"Invert, always invert"** — ask the reverse and do the opposite of what an incumbent expects.

## Anti-patterns
- **Answer mode**: Leaping to bold conclusions and the first cause that comes to mind (doctors anchoring on a familiar diagnosis; executives racing to look decisive).
- **Solving the wrong problem**: Defining the problem as "we lack more/better [our favorite solution]" (more incubators, a better lander) rather than the underlying need.
- **Tactics mistaken for strategy**: Pursuing technology for its own sake — "tools can be the subtlest of traps."
- **Functional fixedness**: Seeing only a tool's known use; letting the familiar solution on the board capture your attention.
- **Copying a stronger competitor**: The low-hanging fruit is already picked; imitation can't beat them, inversion can.

## Worked Example
**Saving the Mars Exploration Rovers with two reframes.** After the 1999 Mars Polar Lander crashed, Varol's team suffered the Einstellung effect — asking "How do we design a *better three-legged lander*?" Engineer Mark Adler reframed it: the problem wasn't the lander, it was *gravity* — "How do we land safely on Mars?" That opened the door to Pathfinder-style airbags (crude, ugly, but proven). Then NASA's Dan Goldin asked a second reframing question no one had posed: "Can you build two?" — send two rovers as a redundancy, doubling both reliability and science for ~50% more cost. Spirit and Opportunity landed at two very different sites; Opportunity's happened to face exposed bedrock ("Holy smokes"). Designed for 90 days, Opportunity roamed 28 miles over 14+ years. Both breakthroughs came from smarter questions, not smarter answers. Paired case: Stanford's Embrace infant warmer ($25 sleeping bag) reframing "not enough incubators" into "premature babies need warmth."

## Key Takeaways
1. Try problem *finding* before problem solving — the framing of the question determines the answer.
2. Beware the Einstellung effect: when a familiar solution is visible, deliberately look for a better one.
3. Separate strategy from tactics; ask "What problem is this tactic here to solve?" and fall in love with the problem.
4. Defeat functional fixedness by seeing form over function and describing tools function-free.
5. Ask "What if we did the reverse?" — inversion beats imitation, especially against stronger rivals.
6. Reframe your resources: the obvious asset is often a distraction from the valuable one.
