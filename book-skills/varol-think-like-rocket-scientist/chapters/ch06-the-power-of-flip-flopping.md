# Chapter 6: The Power of Flip-Flopping

## Core Idea
Facts don't change minds — confirmation bias makes us see what we want to see. The rocket scientist's fix is to switch the default from proving yourself *right* to proving yourself *wrong*: hold working hypotheses (not opinions), breed multiple conflicting ones, hunt for what's missing, and invite dissent. Goal: find what's right, not to be right.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Working hypothesis (not opinion)**: Treat ideas as testable, disposable works-in-progress rather than identity-defining positions.
  - When to use: The outset of any investigation or decision.
  - How: Change your vocabulary ("this paper hypothesizes…" not "I argue…") to separate ideas from identity. Opinions are defended; working hypotheses are tested "for the sake of the facts."
- **A family of multiple (conflicting) hypotheses**: Generate several competing explanations so you can't fall in love with one.
  - When to use: Whenever a single answer is anchoring you.
  - How: Be "the parent of a family of hypotheses"; ideally make them conflict. "The test of a first-rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in mind at the same time and still function."
- **Falsification (try to prove yourself wrong)**: A hypothesis has value only if it can be disproven — then attack it.
  - When to use: Testing any belief before you commit.
  - How: Like the 2-4-6 numbers game, generate strings that would *break* your rule, not confirm it. "Every no brings us one step closer to the truth." Ask "What fact would change my mind?" and write down disconfirming facts immediately (Darwin's golden rule).
- **Steel man (not straw man)**: Argue the strongest version of the opposing view before you reject it.
  - When to use: Evaluating any argument you disagree with.
  - How: "You're not entitled to a view until you can argue against it better than the smartest person who holds it" (Munger). Seek dissent; build a mental model of your sharpest adversary.

## Key Concepts
- **Confirmation bias**: Overvaluing evidence that confirms, undervaluing what contradicts; ~2/3 of people refuse cash to avoid hearing the other side (Wason coined the term).
- **"I wouldn't have seen it if I hadn't believed it"**: Expectation manufactures perception (Stanford's false Mars Polar Lander signal; more moved by affirmatives than negatives — Bacon).
- **Facts don't change minds / backfire effect**: Fact-based vaccine campaigns didn't work and sometimes backfired — "the best response to false beliefs is not necessarily providing correct information."
- **Blind spot / "What's missing?"**: We register presence more easily than absence (the invisible gorilla; Holmes's dog that didn't bark; Spielberg hiding the shark in Jaws).
- **Falsifiability**: What separates science from pseudoscience (Popper); the simulation hypothesis is unfalsifiable and thus worthless as science.
- **Echo chamber**: Internet tribalism amplifies confirmation bias; "the road to self-insight runs through other people."
- **Flip-flopping as virtue**: Changing your mind when facts change is a strength ("I'd rather be right than consistent" — Justice Harlan; Kahneman disproving his own theories).

## Mental Models
- Ask **"What are my preconceptions? Do I *want* this hypothesis to be true?"** — if yes, be extra careful.
- Ask **"What's missing? What fact should be present but isn't? What else?"** — check your blind spot like a driver.
- **"Make me a liar"** — actively ask others to prove you wrong; find your Einstein-to-your-Bohr.
- Pretend your idea is **someone else's** to critique it (Stephen King's shelved drafts; you're more critical of "others'" ideas).
- Ask **"Who will disagree with me?"** before a big decision; if you know no one, go find them.
- **Steel-man** the opposition; avoid the straw man.

## Anti-patterns
- **Fact-drowning**: Assuming more data will persuade — the mind doesn't follow the facts.
- **Single-hypothesis lock-in**: Running with the first idea, which becomes "master" and blinds you to alternatives (the Mars Climate Orbiter navigators ignoring a 70 km discrepancy — "It will sort itself out").
- **Confirmation-seeking tests**: Generating only cases that prove your hunch (2-4-6); "I'm open to being wrong" as a platitude while never self-falsifying.
- **Unfalsifiable claims**: "This argument is irrefutable" — a virtue in politics, a vice in science.
- **Emotional attachment to a conclusion**: Wanting the lander alive so badly you "see" its signal.
- **Straw-manning** and **echo chambers**: Caricaturing opponents and surrounding yourself only with agreement.

## Worked Example
**The Mars Climate Orbiter ($193M, 1999).** The orbiter was meant to enter Mars orbit at a safe 150 km but hit the atmosphere at 57 km and was destroyed. Root cause: Lockheed Martin used English pound-force, JPL assumed metric newtons — measurements off by 4.45×, because both teams omitted units. But the deeper failure was cognitive: during the fourth trajectory-correction maneuver, data showed the craft drifting steadily downward, with predictions off by up to 70 km — which "should have had people screaming down the halls." Instead navigators assumed the *software* was wrong, not the trajectory ("Something funny's going on"… "It will sort itself out"), declined to add a fifth correction, and saw what they wanted to see. No one asked "What's missing? Are we wrong?" Contrast the *Contact* scene, where Ellie Arroway yells "Make me a liar, Fish!" and the team tries to falsify the signal (AWACS? NORAD? the shuttle?) before going global for independent verification.

## Key Takeaways
1. Facts alone don't persuade — expect confirmation bias in yourself and others.
2. Hold working hypotheses, not opinions; change your vocabulary to detach ideas from identity.
3. Breed multiple conflicting hypotheses so you can't fall in love with one.
4. Try to falsify your ideas — every "no" carries more information than a "yes."
5. Ask "What's missing?" and check your blind spot before announcing a conclusion.
6. Seek dissent and steel-man opponents; the road to self-insight runs through other people.
7. Changing your mind when the facts change is a strength, not flip-flopping.
