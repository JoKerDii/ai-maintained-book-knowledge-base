# Chapter 9: Nothing Fails Like Success

## Core Idea
Success is a lousy teacher and a wolf in sheep's clothing: it breeds complacency, conceals luck and near misses, and normalizes creeping risk until catastrophe. The antidote is to treat everything as a permanent work in progress, run postmortems after triumph too, mine near misses, and pursue the deeper causes — not just the first-order ones.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Permanent work in progress (drop "routine")**: Never declare an activity finished or routine.
  - When to use: Especially after success and hot streaks.
  - How: Assume you're "slightly behind" the villain (Avis vs. Hertz); train like you're #2 (Maurice Greene); ship a product while naming how the next version improves. NASA lost crews in flight only *after* spaceflight became "routine."
- **Postmortem after success, not just failure**: Investigate wins with the same rigor as losses.
  - When to use: After any outcome, good or bad.
  - How: Ask "What went wrong with this success? What role did luck play? What can I learn?" (the Patriots treating drafting Tom Brady 199th as a *scouting failure*, not genius).
- **Premortem (outcome-blind foresight)**: Before acting, assume the project failed and work backward to why.
  - When to use: Before firing the rockets, closing the deal, taking the job.
  - How: The opposite of backcasting — travel forward, assume failure, list causes, assign probabilities. Boosts ability to identify failure reasons by ~30%; quantifying uncertainty also reveals luck's role and softens failure's sting. Post premortems where anyone can add worries (X).
- **Attack the cause behind the cause**: Fix second- and third-order causes, not just the visible first-order one.
  - When to use: Any complex-system failure.
  - How: Beyond the O-rings, address the *conformity* and *normalization of deviance*. Otherwise you play "cosmic Whac-A-Mole" and the cancer returns (Challenger's cultural flaws re-emerged in Columbia).

## Key Concepts
- **Success is a lousy teacher**: It "seduces smart people into thinking they can't lose" (Gates); a "We can do anything" attitude at post-Apollo NASA. You can do things wrong and still succeed (dumb luck).
- **Normalization of deviance**: Repeated success with a known flaw makes an anomaly become the norm ("acceptable risk"; foam-shedding as an "in-family" event) — "Russian roulette" (Feynman).
- **Near miss**: An incident that could have been a hit; masquerades as success because the outcome held, but only luck intervened. Near misses breed complacency and riskier decisions, not urgency.
- **Resulting / hindsight in postmortems**: Knowing the outcome makes us assume good outcomes came from good decisions — the Carter Racing case (90% vote to race = "you just launched Challenger").
- **Risk homeostasis / the unsafety of safety**: Safety measures get offset by riskier behavior (ABS-equipped Munich cabs drove more recklessly; redundant O-rings bred invincibility). "Pretend the crosswalk isn't marked."
- **Disrupt yourself**: "You have to disrupt yourself or others will do it for you" (Forbes); Netflix reframing DVD-delivery (tactic) as movie-delivery (strategy), leaping to streaming then original content — Hastings even wanted a *higher* cancel rate.
- **Groupthink / burden-shifting**: Success stifles dissent; engineers were forced to prove the shuttle *unsafe* rather than management prove it safe (Boisjoly and Rocha punished/ignored).

## Mental Models
- Assume you're **slightly behind** — being 1 point down at halftime boosts effort and win probability.
- Run a **postmortem after wins**: "What went wrong with this success? What role did luck play?"
- Run a **premortem** before acting; assign probabilities to failure modes.
- Pretend the **safety net isn't there** — act as if the secondary O-ring, the ABS, the deadline extension doesn't exist.
- Chase the **cause behind the cause**; a symptom fixed is not a cause cured.
- **Attend to near misses** — cheaper and far more frequent than accidents, a rich data source.

## Anti-patterns
- **Celebrating success**: Popping champagne, ignoring luck, upping the ante on a hot streak ("just because you're on a hot streak doesn't mean you'll beat the house").
- **Normalizing deviance**: Treating a recurring flaw as acceptable because "we got away with it last time" (O-rings, foam).
- **Ignoring near misses / small failures**: Only total failures teach institutions; partial failures go unanalyzed and compound (Kodak, Blockbuster, subprime 2008).
- **Postmortem only after death**: Waiting for a catastrophe before investigating; chronic small failures go unnoticed.
- **First-order-cause fixation**: Slapping on a third O-ring while leaving the conformity culture intact (Whac-A-Mole).
- **Trusting the safety net**: Letting redundancies and margins breed recklessness (risk homeostasis).
- **Suppressing dissent**: Shooting the messenger; shifting the burden onto engineers to prove danger.

## Worked Example
**Challenger (1986) and Columbia (2003) — the same cultural cause.** Engineer Roger Boisjoly warned in a 1985 memo that cold made the solid-rocket-booster O-rings brittle and risked "a catastrophe of the highest order, loss of human life," and pushed to delay the freezing-morning January 1986 launch. Management overruled him; Challenger disintegrated 73 seconds in, killing all seven aboard. Over prior flights, O-ring erosion had been repeatedly survived and thus reclassified as "acceptable risk" — normalization of deviance, "Russian roulette." Seventeen years later, Columbia broke apart on reentry after launch foam ("about the size of a beer cooler") holed its wing — a flaw likewise downgraded to an "in-family," "accepted flight risk." Engineer Rodney Rocha begged for spy-satellite imagery of the damage; management rebuffed him, and he saved but never sent his dissenting email, deferring to the chain of command. Sally Ride, on both investigation boards, saw the echo: the *technical* causes differed, but the *cultural* cause — conformity, suppressed dissent, success-bred complacency — had been left unaddressed after Challenger, so it killed again.

## Key Takeaways
1. Success breeds complacency and hides luck — treat every project as a permanent work in progress; drop "routine."
2. Watch for normalization of deviance: don't let "we got away with it" turn an anomaly into the norm.
3. Run postmortems after success too — "What went wrong with this success? What role did luck play?"
4. Run premortems before acting; assume failure, list causes, assign probabilities.
5. Mine near misses and small failures — they're frequent, cheap, and predictive of the big one.
6. Fix the cause behind the cause; a first-order fix that ignores culture is Whac-A-Mole.
7. Distrust the safety net (risk homeostasis) and protect dissent, especially when you're winning.
