# Chapter 8: Nothing Succeeds Like Failure

## Core Idea
Failure is neither to be demonized nor celebrated. Aim to *learn* fast, not fail fast: treat intelligent failures as data, focus on the inputs you control rather than outcomes, and build a psychologically safe culture where failures are surfaced and mined rather than hidden.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Learn fast, not fail fast**: The goal isn't failure — it's the lesson. Celebrate the learning, not the crash.
  - When to use: After any setback; whenever "fail fast" tempts you to move on without reckoning.
  - How: Refuse to conceal, distort, or externalize the failure. Ask "What went wrong — and what went *right*?" (Squyres's 10 years of rejected Mars proposals: "The early ones weren't good enough… they didn't deserve to be selected").
- **Intelligent failure vs. sloppy failure**: Failures from exploring the edges are investments; repeating the same mistake or failing through carelessness is not.
  - When to use: Deciding what to reward and what to sanction.
  - How: "Reward excellent failures, punish mediocre successes." Hold people accountable not for failing intelligently but for failing to *learn*. (X gives bonuses for killing nonviable projects; Amazon rewards good inputs on the failed Fire phone.)
- **Inputs over outputs (avoid "resulting")**: Judge decisions by their quality, not by outcomes you don't fully control.
  - When to use: Post-mortems, performance reviews, personal setbacks.
  - How: Fix bad inputs; *keep* good-quality decisions even when they produced a bad outcome. Ask "What do I love doing so much that failure and success become irrelevant?" (Gilbert). The input becomes its own reward.
- **Psychological safety**: A climate where "no one will be punished or humiliated for errors, questions, or requests for help."
  - When to use: Building any team that must take risks and surface problems.
  - How: Leaders advertise their own failures repeatedly ("I need to hear from you because I'm likely to miss things"); reward intelligent risk; a "black box" and shared "Flight Rules" catalog mistakes so lessons endure.

## Key Concepts
- **Failure IS an option**: "If things are not failing, you are not innovating enough" (Musk) — a moratorium on failure is a moratorium on progress. Dyson: 5,126 prototypes.
- **The problem with "fail fast"**: Failing fast without learning teaches nothing — repeat-failed founders succeed no more than first-timers; surgeons who botch a procedure do *worse* next time (they hid it and reinforced bad habits).
- **Resulting**: Equating decision quality with outcome quality (Annie Duke); good decisions can yield bad outcomes and vice versa.
- **First-order negative, second-order positive**: The advantage of delaying gratification — enduring short-term failure for long-term payoff; "lead bullets, not silver bullets."
- **"How fascinating!"**: Curiosity strips the drama out of failure and creates emotional distance (Mike Nichols calmly watching his flops).
- **Beautiful mess effect**: Owning your vulnerability makes you more relatable — *after* you've established competence.
- **Failure deprivation / exposure therapy**: Treat regular intelligent failure like a vaccine that builds resilience (Blakely's father: "What have you failed at this week?").

## Mental Models
- Ask **"What went wrong AND what went right?"** — retain good inputs from a failed effort (Amazon reusing Fire phone tech; Viagra from failed angina trials; Google Glass repivoted to business).
- **Zoom out**: a single failure is not final defeat; breakthroughs are evolutionary ("ugly babies" — Catmull). "Solve one problem, then the next" (The Martian).
- **Reward excellent failures, punish mediocre successes.**
- **Advertise your own failures** as a leader, repeatedly, to build psychological safety.
- **Fail gracefully**: use thought experiments, redundancies, tests, and small pilots (a single division / subset of customers) to contain damage.

## Anti-patterns
- **Demonizing failure**: Playing it safe, avoiding healthy risk — "behind every book unwritten is the fear of failure."
- **Celebrating failure ("fail fast" / FailCon)**: Toasting failure so the champagne mutes the feedback; failing fast without learning fast.
- **Externalizing blame**: Attributing failure to luck, regulators, or others so you never change course.
- **Punishing the messenger**: Rewarding success and punishing failure so employees underreport failures and overreport successes (the vicious cycle in low-safety hospital teams making — actually reporting — fewer errors).
- **Flying blind**: Omitting the "black box" (the Mars Polar Lander lacked descent telemetry, so its $120M lesson was lost).
- **Resulting**: Firing people or reorganizing after a one-point loss (NFL coaches) while ignoring input quality.

## Worked Example
**SpaceX's Falcon 1: three failures, then orbit.** Musk funded three launches with ~$100M of his own money. Flight 1 (2006) lasted 30 seconds — a fuel leak from a corroded aluminum nut (fixed with cheaper, more reliable stainless steel). Flight 2 (2007) flew 7.5 minutes but didn't reach orbit — most objectives met, anomaly diagnosed and fixed. Flight 3 (2008, Musk's worst year — Tesla near bankruptcy, divorce, financial crisis) failed when the spent first stage re-contacted the second: a test-as-you-fly gap, since the residual thrust was below ground-test ambient pressure but kicked hard in vacuum. Facing strike three, Musk didn't celebrate or quit — he announced funding for two more launches, and the team fixed it by "changing one number" (a longer stage-separation delay). Flight 4 (Sept 28, 2008) reached orbit — the first privately built craft to do so — and three months later NASA awarded a $1.6B resupply contract. A single failure is not final defeat; each crash was learned from, not merely survived.

## Key Takeaways
1. Failure is an option — a moratorium on failure is a moratorium on progress.
2. Learn fast, not fail fast; celebrate the lesson, never the crash.
3. Distinguish intelligent failures (edge-exploring) from sloppy ones; reward the former, sanction carelessness and failure-to-learn.
4. Focus on inputs, not outputs — avoid "resulting"; keep good decisions even when outcomes disappoint.
5. Build psychological safety: leaders advertise their own failures so others surface theirs; catalog lessons in a "black box."
6. Fail gracefully with thought experiments, redundancies, tests, and contained pilots; treat regular failure as resilience-building vaccination.
