# Glossary — Think Like a Rocket Scientist

**Antelopes vs. mice** — Go after the big prize, not the sure small one; a lion burns more energy catching a mouse than it yields, while an antelope feeds it for days (Ch 4).

**Backcasting** — Starting from an imagined future and working backward to the steps, letting vision drive resources instead of forecasting forward from current resources (Ch 4).

**Beginner's mind (shoshin)** — Approaching a problem as an outsider with no stake in the status quo; "in the beginner's mind there are many possibilities; in the expert's mind there are few" (Ch 3).

**Breaking point (test to fail)** — Designing tests for the worst case to find where a thing breaks, before reality breaks it; exaggerate a flaw rather than dismiss it as an outlier (Ch 7).

**Certainty fetish / certainty trap** — The compulsion to make ambiguous things appear definite, leading us to "look for keys under the street lamp" (Ch 1).

**Combinatory play** — Recombining ideas from unrelated fields ("compare apples and oranges"); "to create is to recombine" (Ch 3).

**Confirmation bias** — Overvaluing evidence that confirms our beliefs and undervaluing what contradicts them; coined by Peter Wason (Ch 6).

**Connoisseur of uncertainty** — Someone exhilarated rather than frightened at the edge of the known; they "dance" in the dark room (Ch 1).

**Convergent thinking (the bee)** — Narrowing to the seemingly obvious solution; useful for evaluation but deadly if used first (the bees die bumping the light) (Ch 4).

**Dance with uncertainty** — Treating the unknown as friend not foe; "first chaos, then breakthrough" (Ch 1).

**Divergent thinking (the fly)** — Free, open-ended idea generation without judging constraints; the flies find the exit by fluttering (Ch 4).

**Einstellung effect** — A fixed mental set: once a familiar solution is known, we stop seeing better alternatives (Ch 5).

**Falsifiability** — A hypothesis has value only if it can be proven wrong (Popper); separates science from pseudoscience (Ch 6).

**Family of hypotheses** — Generating multiple, ideally conflicting, working hypotheses so you can't fall in love with one (Ch 6).

**First-principles thinking** — Stripping a problem to its irreducible fundamentals and reasoning up from there, instead of by analogy (Ch 2).

**Flip-flopping (as a virtue)** — Changing your mind when the facts change; "I'd rather be right than consistent" (Ch 6).

**Frankenstein's monster / Frankenstate** — Individually benign components turning catastrophic in combination; why you must test the assembled system ("understand the 'and'") (Ch 7).

**Functional fixedness** — A mental block against using an object in a new way (Duncker's candle-box problem); dissolved by seeing form over function (Ch 5).

**How fascinating!** — A curiosity ritual that strips the drama out of a failure and creates emotional distance (Ch 8).

**Intelligent failure** — A failure from exploring the edges and solving unsolved problems; an investment and teacher, unlike sloppy failure (Ch 8).

**Inputs over outputs** — Judging decisions by their quality, not by outcomes you don't fully control; avoids "resulting" (Ch 8).

**Invisible rules** — Habits that have rigidified into rules without anyone deciding they should ("tie the cat to the pole") (Ch 2).

**Inversion ("What if we did the reverse?")** — Flipping a question or best practice on its head; "invert, always invert" (Ch 5).

**It remains Day 1** — Treating every success as a fresh start; "Day 2 is stasis… followed by decline" (Bezos) (Epilogue).

**Kill metrics** — Predefined go/no-go criteria set while clearheaded, so you can quit a moonshot before sunk cost distorts judgment (Ch 4).

**Kill-the-company / red teaming** — Playing the antagonist trying to destroy you, then switching back to defend the weak points (Ch 2).

**Learn fast, not fail fast** — The goal is the lesson, not the crash; celebrate the learning, never failure itself (Ch 8).

**Margins of safety** — Building stronger/thicker than the minimum required, sized to the stakes and the irreversibility of the decision (Ch 1).

**Medici effect** — The creative explosion when people from different disciplines are combined (Ch 3).

**Monkey first** — Tackling the hardest, riskiest part of a project before the easy "pedestal" work (Ch 4).

**Moonshot (10x not 10%)** — An audacious goal so big the status quo has to go, forcing first-principles solutions (Ch 4).

**Near miss** — An incident that could have been a hit; masquerades as success because luck held, and breeds complacency rather than urgency (Ch 9).

**Normalization of deviance** — Repeated success with a known flaw turning an anomaly into an "acceptable risk" (O-rings, foam-shedding) (Ch 9).

**Occam's razor** — Given two hypotheses that explain the data equally well, prefer the simpler — a guiding principle, not an absolute rule ("seek simplicity and distrust it") (Ch 2).

**Observer effect** — Being watched changes behavior; mitigate with double-blind tests and real-world observation (Ch 7).

**Path dependence** — What we've done before shapes what we do next (the shuttle's engine width traced to Roman roads) (Ch 2).

**Permanent work in progress** — Never declaring a project or yourself finished or "routine"; train like you're #2 (Ch 9).

**Premortem** — Before acting, assuming the project failed and working backward to why; the inverse of backcasting; assign probabilities (Ch 9).

**Problem finding (reframe the question)** — Searching for a better question instead of a better answer; "a problem well defined is a problem half solved" (Ch 5).

**Psychological safety** — A climate where "no one will be punished or humiliated for errors, questions, or requests for help" (Edmondson) (Ch 8).

**Redundancy** — A backup that prevents a single point of failure from killing the mission; must be independent to work ("fail without failing") (Ch 1).

**Resulting** — Equating the quality of a decision with the quality of its outcome (Annie Duke) (Ch 8, Ch 9).

**Risk homeostasis (the unsafety of safety)** — Safety measures offset by riskier behavior; "pretend the crosswalk isn't marked" (Ch 9).

**Risking your significance** — Abandoning the identity story that constrains you (Steve Martin quitting stand-up at his peak) (Ch 2).

**Steel man** — Articulating the strongest version of an opposing argument before rejecting it; the opposite of a straw man (Ch 6).

**Strategy vs. tactics** — Strategy is the objective; tactics are the actions serving it — "What problem is this tactic here to solve?" (Ch 5).

**Success is a lousy teacher** — Success seduces smart people into thinking they can't lose; a wolf in sheep's clothing (Ch 9).

**Test as you fly, fly as you test** — Making tests mirror real "flight" conditions, then operating as you tested (Ch 7).

**Thought experiment (Gedankenexperiment)** — Running a fictional scenario in "the laboratory of the mind" to disprove or illuminate (Ch 3).

**Two-way vs. one-way doors** — Reversible vs. irreversible decisions; keep the door two-way when you can (Ch 1).

**Unknown knowns** — Self-delusion: thinking we know what we know but don't; the land of false confidence (Ch 1).

**Working hypothesis** — A testable, disposable work-in-progress belief, held "for the sake of the facts" — the opposite of an opinion (Ch 6).
