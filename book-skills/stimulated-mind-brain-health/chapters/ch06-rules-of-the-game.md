# Chapter 6: The Rules of the Game

## Core Idea
The prize in the brain game is **headroom** — a neurobiological buffer that lets you function well under stress, illness, and age. How you *approach* health information matters as much as the information itself, so Wood sets five rules that govern every tactic in Part Two.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Five Rules of the Brain Game**: The governing constitution for everything in Part Two.
  1. **Prepare for your own race** — Identify the areas where *you* are most likely to see benefit, and match them to actions you can consistently and reliably perform. Everything else is a distraction. What you focus on can and should change over time.
  2. **Never give up** — Not a call to overcome laziness at any cost; a reminder to keep your feet moving. Over decades, skipped workouts, a few bad nights, or an entire cake in one sitting make very little difference as long as the steps keep accumulating.
  3. **Don't go it alone** — Ask for help; many brain-health actions are easier or actively enhanced when done with others. Also: find your *why*, which for almost everyone involves other people.
  4. **Everything counts** — Small changes have real impact, and you should let yourself celebrate them.
  5. **Be kind to yourself (and others)** — Take rest and recovery; be realistic about what to attempt now; make no value judgments about how you or others act on health information.
  - When to use: Every time you're deciding what to add, and every time you catch yourself cataloguing failures.
  - How: Rules 1–3 define the approach to *action*; rules 4–5 structure the necessary *rest* afterwards. They are complementary opposites, deliberately.
  - Why it works: The tension between what you think you should be doing and what you're actually doing is itself physiologically harmful — so a rule set that closes that gap is an intervention, not just encouragement.

- **Stack the Deck in Your Favor**: Reframe brain health from guarantee-seeking to odds-shifting.
  - When to use: When someone objects "my relative exercised every day and still got dementia."
  - How: Accept you're working with probabilities, not certainties. At 80, ~20% of those with 4+ protective factors were still diagnosed — but that's about half the risk of those with 0–1. Also consider that the exercising relative may have had better day-to-day function, milder symptoms, or a diagnosis years later than they otherwise would have.
  - Failure mode: Treating a single counterexample as refutation. No single variable guarantees any outcome.

- **Aim for Progress, Not Perfection**: Reframe what counts as "enough."
  - When to use: When a target (8 hours' sleep, 4 hours/week of aerobic exercise) is so far from your baseline that you're about to do nothing at all.
  - How: Set lower goals with a broader definition of what counts as exercise — this makes you more likely to stick with it *and* improves how you feel about your health. Sometimes it doesn't even need to be good; it just needs to be *something*.

## Key Concepts
- **Headroom**: Extra cognitive capacity and robustness held in reserve — you don't need it every day, but you want it available when you do.
- **Complementary opposites**: Stimulus and challenge drive adaptation; rest and recovery are what allow the adaptation to occur. Both are required.
- **Psychological friction**: The chronic stress of holding a mental list of health tasks you aren't addressing (per Gloria Mark's work) — which itself damages health.
- **Perception as physiology**: Your beliefs about your own health behavior change your biology and outcomes (Ellen Langer's line of work).
- **The 5K vs. the Ironman**: Small, regular, recoverable challenges vs. an endless self-inflicted beatdown with no reprieve.
- **Your why**: The purpose behind the pursuit — almost always other people — which supplies the motivation to keep accumulating.

## Mental Models
- Think of brain health as **preparing for an athletic event, not clearing game levels** — play to strengths, address limiters, expect measurable gains, and accept that the outcome partly depends on factors outside your control (2012 Olympic 100m final: second, third, and fourth all ran personal bests; four more set national records — they just ran behind Usain Bolt).
- Use **"the storm"** as the label for uncontrollables — socioeconomic status, stress, genetics, air and water quality. Weather them; don't let them decide whether you keep moving.
- Think of a fifteen-item brain-health listicle as **a stressor, not a resource** — when faced with an impossible list, most people do nothing at all, which is the worst of all worlds.
- Use **"the quitting point"** as a mental image: in Wood's off-road Ironman, most competitors quit at the warm indoor rest station, and fewer than half of starters finished. The failure was rarely fitness.

## Anti-patterns
- **Letting perfect be the enemy of good**: If you sleep six hours and the expert says eight, the "reasonable" response is to abandon sleep improvement entirely. That's the trap.
- **Chronic self-deficit framing**: In a study of 60,000+ US adults, those who *believed* they exercised less than peers had the highest risk of death — after adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, employment, income, marital status, multiple health measures, smoking, and *actual physical activity levels*. A parallel study found the same for cognitive function. Believing you're not doing enough may itself be harmful.
- **Assuming exercise must be vigorous to count**: People think they need to add a lot of vigorous activity before it registers as change, despite lower levels also being beneficial.
- **Blaming yourself or a family member for a dementia diagnosis**: Dementia is common, rarely the result of any single factor, and definitely not anybody's fault.
- **Trying to control your entire life**: The goal is a consistent resolve to engage and challenge yourself — not perfect optimization, and not operating at 100% without interruption.

## Worked Example
Wood's 2012 off-road Ironman is the rules applied end to end. The race: a ~2-mile sea swim, 112 miles of overnight mountain biking, then a marathon trail run, inside a 24-hour limit. It was his first year working as a doctor, so training time was minimal. **Rule 1 (prepare for your own race)**: he defined success as simply finishing, then diagnosed his real limiters — not fitness, but the high probability of getting lost or quitting. So he spent his scarce time riding and running the entire course beforehand and training immediately after hospital night shifts to prove he could keep going without sleep. Any good coach would call that a poor training plan; a "perfect" plan would have failed while costing him his work and sleep for months. **The storm** arrived on cue — the third-wettest UK summer since records began in 1836, twelve hours of mud in complete darkness. **Rule 2 (never give up)**: he considered quitting several times and got back on the bike. **Rule 3 (don't go it alone)**: a fellow competitor named Glenn, met on the hillsides and never seen again, walked back out of the rest station with him and talked him through the final miles. Fewer than half the starters finished. He was not a better athlete or navigator than those who quit — he just focused on what was most likely to hold him back and controlled it with the time he had.

## Key Takeaways
1. Pick your focus by intersecting *where you'll benefit most* with *what you can actually do consistently*.
2. Accumulate; don't restart. A missed week is noise across a decades-long game.
3. Stop discounting what you already do — believing you're not doing enough may be independently harmful to your health and cognition.
4. Lower the bar and broaden the definition of what counts; you'll adhere better and feel better.
5. Pair every challenge with recovery — adaptation happens in the rest, not the stimulus.
6. Recruit someone. Social connection is both a rule of the game and a tactic within it.
7. Anchor to a *why* involving people you love; purpose itself is protective.
