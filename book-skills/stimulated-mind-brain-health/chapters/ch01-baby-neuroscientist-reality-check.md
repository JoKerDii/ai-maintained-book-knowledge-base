# Chapter 1: Baby Neuroscientist's First Reality Check

## Core Idea
Brain health is personal and time-extended: a healthy brain performs the cognitive functions *you* want, *when* you want them, and stays future-proof for decades. Cognitive decline is not inevitable — it is largely the consequence of mismatch between what our brains evolved to receive and what modern life actually gives them.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Headroom** (borrowed from economist Art De Vany): the total amount of function available to you when you really need it — the gap between your day-to-day demand and your true maximum capacity.
  - When to use: whenever deciding whether an activity is "worth it" for long-term brain health. Ask "does this widen the gap between my daily load and my ceiling?"
  - How: build capacity deliberately above daily demand (the strength analogy: if you need 25 lb per hand for groceries but can deadlift 100 lb, groceries feel light and surprise loads don't injure you). Cognitive equivalent: education, skill learning, physical activity, sustained engagement.
  - Why it works: aging removes some function regardless. A larger starting buffer means you cross the impairment threshold later, or never. Failure mode: treating headroom as something built only in youth — it is buildable at any age.
- **The Three R's of Headroom — Reserve, Resilience, Resolve**: the components that make up cognitive headroom.
  - **Cognitive reserve** = total cognitive capacity, and how much function you can lose before significant impairment or a dementia diagnosis. The *software* of the brain.
  - **Brain reserve** (a.k.a. brain maintenance) = the structure itself, how much brain is in your skull; more is usually better, preserve size with age. The *hardware*.
  - **Cognitive resilience** = ability to tolerate accumulated damage without function being affected. The *redundancy* in the system.
  - **Resolve** = Wood's third R: shedding the expectation of decline and committing to lifelong engagement in activities that produce reserve and resilience.
  - When to use: when choosing between interventions. Because the same lifestyle inputs improve all three, you rarely need to optimize them separately.
- **Mismatch** (biological term): the gap between how the human brain evolved to function and how we currently live.
  - When to use: as a diagnostic lens on any modern habit — food, activity, sleep, social environment, technology, retirement.
  - How: identify the ancestral input that has been removed or distorted, then restore a version of it. Reducing mismatch reduces dementia risk and improves daily focus, function, and mood.

## Key Concepts
- **Brain health (Wood's definition)**: a brain that performs the cognitive functions you want, at the time you want it to perform them — plus being as future-proof as possible.
- **Future-proofing**: minimizing aging effects and preserving the ability to adapt to unknown future situations for decades.
- **Cognitive reserve**: total cognitive capacity and tolerable loss before impairment.
- **Brain reserve / brain maintenance**: structural brain volume and its preservation.
- **Cognitive resilience**: functional tolerance to accumulated brain damage.
- **Mismatch**: evolutionary-environment discrepancy driving modern brain problems.
- **Retirement mismatch**: removing work's cognitive and social stimulus; retirement is often the point at which cognitive decline accelerates.
- **The expectation cycle of decline**: expecting less → using the brain less / offloading to tech or people → brain downregulates capacity → function drops → expectation drops further.

## Mental Models
- Use **headroom** when evaluating any brain-health activity: the question is never "is my brain fine today?" but "how much buffer do I have for the day it isn't?"
- Think of the **brain as a muscle**: the more it is exercised the stronger it gets, regardless of age; the body will not invest energy into capacity that isn't being used.
- Use **mismatch** as the default explanation before reaching for pathology — ask what input was removed, not what protein accumulated.
- Think of **older adulthood as designed-for, not leftover**: humans are one of the few species living decades past reproduction, likely because healthy grandparents as teachers and providers improved survival of kin.

## Anti-patterns
- **Accepting the Osler premise**: Sir William Osler's 1905 Johns Hopkins valedictory claim that workers were "useless" after age sixty made retirement socially acceptable and still shapes belief a century later — despite U.S. life expectancy then being around fifty. It contradicts our evolutionary history.
- **Fatalism from statistics**: treating dementia numbers as inevitability rather than a call to action.
- **Focusing health effort below the neck**: assuming the mirror reflects overall health, which is often not true at all, while ignoring the brain because it's hard to measure.
- **Offloading cognitive work to technology or other people because you assume decline**: this manufactures the decline it fears.

## Worked Example
The Harvard hunter-gatherer activity analysis: researchers examined data from modern hunter-gatherer groups and found that grandparents were at least as physically active as parents, frequently hunting and gathering several hours per day. Adults in their seventies and eighties in those societies were still three times more physically active every day than the average U.S. adult at *any* age. Read alongside the observation that cognitive decline often accelerates at retirement, the reasoning chain runs: biology expects sustained bodily and social engagement as the signal to maintain function → industrialized life removes that signal at a fixed age → function declines on schedule → we misattribute the decline to age itself rather than to the removed input.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define brain health personally — name the specific functions you want your brain to deliver, then train those.
2. Build headroom deliberately; the buffer, not today's performance, is what protects you at 80.
3. Don't try to optimize reserve, resilience, and brain maintenance separately — the same inputs move all three.
4. Add resolve: explicitly reject the expectation of decline, because expectation drives behavior which drives biology.
5. Hunt for mismatches in your own life (food, movement, sleep, social contact, technology) and close them.
6. Treat retirement as a stimulus problem to solve, not a reward to passively consume; replace work's cognitive and social load with something else.
7. It is never too late to start — the brain can increase function and slow the rate of change at any age and from any starting trajectory.
