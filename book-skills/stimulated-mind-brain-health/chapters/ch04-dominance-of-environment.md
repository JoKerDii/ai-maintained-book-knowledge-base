# Chapter 4: Dominance of the Environment

## Core Idea
The human brain's defining feature is that it is built to be shaped by its environment — the regions that evolved most recently are the least developed at birth, take ~30 years to mature, retain the greatest neuroplasticity, and are also the most vulnerable to aging. Therefore the inputs you supply (learning, movement, social engagement, expectation) dominate drugs and dominate biology in determining how your brain functions across life.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Dominance of the environment**: when it comes to brain growth, development, and long-term function, the impact of drug treatments pales in comparison to the impact of the environment — meaning the world around us and the way we engage with it.
  - When to use: whenever weighing a pharmaceutical or supplement hope against a lifestyle input.
  - How: identify which environmental inputs your brain is currently receiving versus which built it in the first place, then restore the missing ones.
  - Why it works: the parts of the brain that make us distinctly human are effectively "waiting to see what world they're going to exist in" before developing accordingly. Failure mode: waiting for the breakthrough drug — the varied, interacting nature of cognitive decline means any meaningful approach must be multipronged.
- **Train the functions you want to keep**: your ability to perform complex skills is much more related to how you use your brain than to how old your brain is.
  - When to use: when choosing what to practice, and when interpreting your own age-related slowdowns.
  - How: distinguish (a) nonspecific capacities like processing speed, which decline with age and are hard to train directly, from (b) trained specific skills, which hold up with recent deliberate practice. Then deliberately maintain practice volume on the skills you refuse to lose.
  - Why it works: in expert pianists, practice — especially total practice in the last ten years — was consistently the most important factor, most pronounced in the older experts. Failure mode: attributing a practice deficit to age.
- **Last-in first-out hypothesis (and Wood's amendment)**: the brain regions that evolved and develop most recently are those that decline most rapidly with aging.
  - Wood's correction: it doesn't quite fit, because aging also strongly hits the medial parietal cortex, which combines with parts of prefrontal cortex to form the default mode network (DMN). So it's "last-in first-out, **plus the bits of the brain that those bits are connected to**."
- **Stereotype-embodiment theory** (Langer): people age by following their underlying stereotypes about older people — including expectations about cognitive decline and dementia. If you expect to lose function, you see greater decline, partly through subconscious expectation effects and partly through the activities you consequently choose not to do.
- **Decreasing engagement with age as the ultimate mismatch**: modern adult life systematically removes the exact inputs that built the brain — we stop learning, stop moving, stop sleeping enough, and socialize less with good friends.
  - How to use: audit which developmental input you have dropped (novel effortful learning, physical activity, social complexity, sleep), and treat the drop as the intervention point.

## Key Concepts
- **Neuroplasticity**: the brain's capacity to be adaptable and changeable — present even in old age, given the right inputs and tools; several researchers suggest dementia and Alzheimer's are fundamentally driven by *loss* of neuroplasticity.
- **Default mode network (DMN)**: medial parietal cortex plus certain prefrontal regions; activity increases when not focused on a task, and when thinking about oneself and planning for the future; some of its functions likely uniquely human.
- **Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex**: the "deliberative" prefrontal cortex; the part that stops you doing or saying something stupid at the last moment.
- **Crystallized intelligence**: the components of cognition that combine to form wisdom — does not automatically decline and may peak in the sixth or seventh decade; older adults are less impulsive and better at integrating multiple strands of information.
- **Digit symbol substitution test (DSST)**: a measure of brain processing speed — filling numbered boxes with corresponding symbols against the clock; a perfect score (e.g., 100 symbols in 90 seconds) requires more than one symbol per second. Scores generally decrease with age but are also influenced by short-term factors like sleep.
- **Synaptic pruning as development**: cortical thickness peaks in toddlerhood and total gray matter peaks around first grade; subsequent development is *selective removal* of unneeded neurons and synapses, directed by environmental input.
- **Aging as continued development**: one theory holds aging is just the decades-long continuation of the processes that drive development — so decline at retirement is the brain responding normally to the removal of an input.
- **The Knowledge of London**: the black cab licensing exam requiring memorization of 25,000 streets within a six-mile radius of Charing Cross; takes three to four years to learn.

## Mental Models
- Use **"impossible vs. merely challenging"** whenever you catch yourself saying you can't learn something now: thinking the brain cannot change with age is a claim about biology; finding time to prioritize learning is a matter of resources, accessibility, and belief. The biology probably isn't holding you back as much as you think.
- Think of **human infant ineptitude as the feature**: a newborn chimp clings to its mother immediately; a newborn human does little but sleep, eat, and cry — the slow maturation buys decades for the environment to optimize the brain.
- Use the **older elite athlete as the template**: young athletes can eat anything and train anyhow and still improve; successful older athletes are targeted, know *why* they do what they do, and therefore have more time for rest and recovery.
- Think of **engagement, not age, as the variable** — as with the pianists, engagement in the task is what drives performance.

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting for the breakthrough drug**: one of the two ideas Wood held that turned out to be wrong. No single drug or drug type is likely to cure all of Alzheimer's.
- **Believing the adult brain is fixed and resistant to change**: his second wrong idea; adaptation as an adult is challenging, not slow-and-impossible.
- **Letting routine substitute for learning**: after formal education ends, routine takes over; specialization plus busyness sends far weaker maintenance signals than youth did. Taking multiple years to learn a large body of new information as an adult, as prospective cab drivers do, is surprisingly rare.
- **Blaming decline on age when the real change is practice volume**: the pianist study's authors noted older experts' competing life goals cut practice time — it was those who practiced less, not the oldest, whose performance suffered.
- **Accepting delivered care over exercised control**: nursing home residents told staff would do everything for them fared worse on health, activity, and social measures than those given control.

## Worked Example
The London taxi driver evidence chain, end to end.
1. Prospective black cab drivers in their thirties and forties, plus a control group, were brain-scanned before and after three to four years of studying the Knowledge. Those who *passed* showed structural changes in the posterior hippocampus — the region critical to spatial memory. Those who failed showed no change, and neither did controls. So the change tracked successful learning, not merely time or intent.
2. A separate study found this same region is larger in London taxi drivers than in bus drivers, who follow fixed routes and rely less on navigation. So the effect tracks navigational demand, not driving.
3. The hippocampus is usually affected in Alzheimer's disease. In a large U.S. study of dementia rates, taxi drivers and ambulance drivers — two professions requiring frequent navigation — had a lower risk of death due to Alzheimer's than all other occupations, even after accounting for age and socioeconomic status.
4. Wood's own caveat: nobody can randomize people into a taxi-driving career, so this is not proof. But it shows how the brain responds to environmental demand *in adulthood*, and the long-term effect that can have.

## Key Takeaways
1. Prioritize environmental inputs over pharmacological hope — the environment dominates drug effects for long-term brain function.
2. To keep a skill, keep practicing it; recent practice volume, not age, predicts expert-level performance into the sixties.
3. Take on genuinely hard, multi-year learning as an adult — the kind where you risk failing — because that is what built the brain originally.
4. Support the untrainable base layer with physical health: in ~1,500 adults aged 60–85, higher leg strength and physical activity tracked faster processing speed, while higher blood sugar, higher blood pressure, raised inflammation, and B vitamin or iron deficiency tracked slower processing speed. Strength, nutrient status, physical health, and sleep replicated in a second NHANES cohort.
5. Use exercise as the broadest lever available: it is the only intervention that seems to either slow or reverse *every* biochemical hallmark of aging, and it measurably improves neuroplasticity.
6. Manage expectation actively — beliefs change physiology, as Langer's hotel cleaners showed: cleaners simply told their work counted as exercise saw significant improvements in body composition and blood pressure.
7. Preserve and exercise personal control and responsibility, especially in later-life care settings.
8. Lean into crystallized intelligence: adapt the game rather than repeat the young version of it (Ronaldo runs less but still scores; LeBron maximizes younger teammates).
9. Learning new skills or creative activities — art, languages, music, even video games — can slow or reverse changes in the brain networks susceptible to aging.
