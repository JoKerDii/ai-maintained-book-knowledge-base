# Chapter 8: Nourish

## Core Idea
There is no single best brain diet; almost any dietary pattern works if it delivers the nutrients the brain needs and matches energy intake to your actual needs. Two rules only: **maximize nutrients** and **fuel for your own needs** — implemented through a *pattern*, not a branded diet.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Components of Brain Nutrition — Nutrients, Energy, Pattern**: nutrients supply the machinery, energy must land in the middle of a bell curve, pattern is the sustainable logistics that deliver both.
  - When to use: evaluating any diet, protocol, or supplement claim for brain health.
  - How: (1) fill the plate with nutrient-dense whole foods; (2) test whether you're over- or under-fueled using symptoms, measurements, and labs; (3) choose the pattern you can actually sustain.
  - Why it works: nutrients act in cascades, not in isolation, so food beats single-nutrient reductionism; energy availability has a bell-shaped relationship with brain volume, so both deficit and excess shrink the brain.
- **Triage Theory** (Bruce Ames): when nutrients or calories are scarce, the body allocates them to immediate survival and defunds long-term repair and reserve-building.
  - When to use: explaining why "not deficient" is not the same as "optimal," and why chronic under-fueling erodes brain headroom.
  - Failure mode: warning signs of under-fueling are low hormone levels, gut symptoms, loss of muscle/strength/fitness, poor recovery, fatigue, poor sleep.
- **The Brain's Nutrient Cascade**: trace one nerve signal — B vitamins for mitochondrial energy → vitamins C/E and polyphenols to control oxidative stress → sodium/potassium/calcium to propagate → iron for white matter → omega-3s + choline for membranes → vitamin D to make serotonin from tryptophan → DHA at the synapse, attached to a choline/ethanolamine headgroup via methylation (riboflavin, B6, folate, B12) → magnesium/zinc modulating receptor binding.
  - When to use: rebutting single-nutrient trials that "failed."
- **Thirds of a plate** (Wood's recommendation; he does not brand it): divide a 10-inch plate — 1/3 lean protein, 1/3 nutrient-dense veg/fruit, final 1/3 flexed to energy needs (starch if you need fuel; more veg/fruit if in energy excess).
  - How: always place the protein first — it is the easiest thing to miss.
- **WOOP** (Gabriele Oettingen): Wish → Outcome → Obstacle → Plan; name every obstacle and pre-plan an action for each.
- **Implementation Intentions**: replace vague goals with a specific if-then plan, scheduled in the calendar; works best with one goal at a time.
- **Self-Determination Theory** (Ryan & Deci): any dietary change sticks only if it satisfies autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

## Key Concepts
- **Longevity vitamins** (Ames): non-essential compounds that support long-term health — ergothioneine (mushrooms), carotenoids, astaxanthin.
- **Low energy availability**: fatigue, poor focus, distractibility, gut symptoms, suppressed thyroid/testosterone/estrogen from under-fueling.
- **Excess energy availability**: chronic overfueling driving inflammation, oxidative stress, atherosclerosis, and smaller brains.
- **Nutrient density vs energy density**: nutrients per bite vs calories per bite — brain-healthy patterns raise the first and lower the second.
- **Homocysteine**: blood marker of methylation/B-vitamin status; elevated levels flag the people B vitamins help.
- **Hyperpalatable**: calorie-dense, easy-to-overeat, low in protein and fiber — the actual problem, not "ultraprocessed" per se.
- **Satiety per Calorie** (Ted Naiman): framing food choice by fullness delivered per calorie consumed.
- **Naturalistic fallacy**: assuming traditional/ancestral diets are automatically best.
- **Nutrient synergy**: B vitamins only help cognition when omega-3 status is adequate, and vice versa.

## Mental Models
- Think of your body as a household budget under triage — when income drops it cancels the long-term investments (repair, reproduction, brain reserve) first.
- Use the F1 fuel-load analogy when deciding intake: too much fuel is sluggish and wears the tires, too little and you don't finish.
- Think "a brownie is still a brownie" whenever a food is defended by its label (vegan, keto, Mediterranean, protein-added) rather than its composition.
- Use "add, don't subtract" when a change feels restrictive — adding a serving of vegetables raises nutrients and lowers calories without a sense of loss.

## Worked Example
**The SMILES trial pattern** (first RCT showing diet improves depression symptoms) — daily/weekly targets, with participants told to eat as much as they wanted: vegetables 6 servings/day; fruit 3 servings/day; legumes 3–4 servings/week; low-fat unsweetened dairy 2–3 servings/day; raw unsalted nuts 1 serving/day; fish ≥2 servings/week; lean red meat 3–4 servings/week; chicken 2–3 servings/week; eggs up to 6/week; olive oil 3 tablespoons/day. Reduce sweets, refined cereals, fried food, fast food, processed meats, and sugary drinks to no more than 3 total per week. Practical use: adopt one or two lines at a time rather than the whole list.

**The BROAD trial** shows *why* it held: people with obesity plus metabolic disease switched to unprocessed whole plant foods but could eat as much as they wanted (autonomy), were taught to source and cook it (competence), and had twice-weekly meetings with social events and potlucks for three months (relatedness). Nine months after the intervention ended, all improvements — including weight loss — were maintained.

## Key Takeaways
1. Judge any diet by two rules only: does it maximize nutrients, and does it fuel your actual needs?
2. Never evaluate a nutrient alone — B vitamins only helped cognition in VITACOG-style trials when omega-3 status was adequate.
3. Test where you sit on the energy curve before changing anything: waist >35 in, waist-to-height >0.55, fasting glucose >100 mg/dL, HbA1c >5.6%.
4. Eat more protein than the RDI: 0.55 g/lb minimum, 0.7–0.8 g/lb ideally, and more as you age — paired with resistance training.
5. Pick the energy strategy (caloric restriction, IF, or TRE) purely on sustainability; their benefit runs through reduced intake, not fasting magic, and rodent fasting data doesn't transfer to humans.
6. Build plates, not diets: 1/3 protein first, 1/3 veg/fruit, final 1/3 flexed to energy needs.
7. Supplement only for deficiency links or positive asymmetry — omega-3s, methylation B vitamins, vitamin D, magnesium, creatine. You cannot supplement your way out of a bad diet.
8. Composition beats the "ultraprocessed" label: calorie-dense, hyperpalatable, low-protein, low-fiber, low-chew is the real risk profile.
