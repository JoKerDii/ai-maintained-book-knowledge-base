# Chapter 5: The Game of Brain Health

## Core Idea
Dementia is not predetermined — at least 45% of cases are preventable (Lancet Commission 2024), and possibly over 70% (UK Biobank, ~350,000 people). The winning strategy is to "play the game" of brain health at the level of lifestyle and environment, rather than trying to hack the brain's biochemical "source code."

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Play the Game (Team One vs. Team Two)**: Borrowed from neurologist Josh Turknett. Aliens find a phone with a game on it. Team One just plays the game for hours and beats it; Team Two dissects the source code and can't clear a single level. Team One wins without understanding any of the mechanism.
  - When to use: Whenever you're tempted to wait for a mechanistic explanation before acting on brain health.
  - How: Intervene at the level of lifestyle and environment (exercise, diet, cognitive activity, sleep, connection), not at the level of amyloid and microglia. Accept that you cannot and need not isolate which component did the work.
  - Why it works: Cognitive function is the output of a lifetime of interacting exposures that trained an algorithm unique to each person — it is literally impossible to understand the whole by isolating components. Exercise is the exemplar: we still don't know exactly how it works, and that has never been a reason not to prescribe it.
  - Failure mode: Reductionism is the right tool for single-component problems (a broken limb, an infection, a single-gene disease) — "the biologist's approach to fixing a broken radio." It fails for multi-causal, network-level conditions like dementia.

- **The Five Healthy Lifestyle Factors (Chicago Health and Aging Project)**: Diet, exercise, cognitive activity, smoking, alcohol.
  - When to use: As the baseline scorecard for personal dementia risk.
  - How: Count how many of the five you meet. 4–5 factors → half the risk of dementia diagnosis at any age vs. 0–1 factors. 2–3 sits in the middle.
  - Note: Observational (~2,500 adults 65+), so it shows association, not proof of causation — which is why FINGER matters.

- **The FINGER trial as proof of concept**: Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability. 1,200+ adults aged 60–77 at high dementia risk, randomized to control or a 2-year multidomain intervention: dietary change + aerobic and resistance training + cognitive training + active monitoring/treatment of heart disease risk factors.
  - Result: Significant gains vs. control in overall cognitive function, processing speed, and decision-making.
  - Why it matters: Don't demand to know which component did the work. They all matter, they act synergistically, and the effects can never be fully measured.

## Key Concepts
- **Headroom**: The extra neurobiological capacity and robustness that buffers against aging and stress — the book's central prize and the thing every intervention is building.
- **Mismatch**: The gap between what our brains evolved to thrive on and what the modern environment actually delivers.
- **Age-specific dementia incidence**: The likelihood of being diagnosed at a *given* age — this has been *decreasing* across UK, French, Swedish, Icelandic, Dutch, and US cohorts (Alzheimer Cohorts Consortium), even as total lifetime risk rises because people live longer.
- **The Tsimané**: ~17,000 Indigenous Bolivian forest dwellers with the lowest estimated dementia rates in the world and slower brain aging; attributed to lifelong non-motorized physical labor into their 70s–80s, a diet of vegetables/fish/lean meat, and near-absent hypertension and diabetes.
- **Vascular contribution to Alzheimer's**: Most individuals with Alzheimer's have diseased brain blood vessels, mirroring cardiac disease; stiffened vessels can no longer deliver the dynamic blood-flow changes the brain needs.
- **Reductionism**: The approach of breaking a system into components to understand it — productive for single-cause disease, unproductive for cognitive decline.

## Reference Tables

Lancet Commission 2024 — at least 45% of dementia preventable; roughly half of that 45% is explained by factors under significant individual control (physical activity, smoking, alcohol). Selected attributions:

Wood's caveat: these are almost certainly underestimates. The Livingston report omits later-life cognitive activity, nutrient status, and sleep as protective factors despite good evidence for all three.

## Mental Models
- Think of brain health as **a game you can win by playing, not by decoding** — you don't need the source code to beat the level.
- Use the **Tsimané comparison** when you want evidence that dementia is environmental rather than inevitable.
- Think of falls as **the dominant head-trauma pathway in adults over 65** — so strength and balance training is dementia prevention, not just orthopedic prevention.
- Treat heart disease prevention as **dementia prevention by proxy** — the declining age-specific dementia rate likely rides on decades of public-health work on smoking, blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol.

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting for the mechanism before acting**: We still can't fully dissect how exercise works; that has never justified not exercising. Applying the same standard to brain health means indefinite inaction.
- **Demanding to know which intervention in a multidomain program mattered most**: The components act synergistically across innumerable pathways. Insisting on isolating them destroys the intervention.
- **Reading rising dementia projections as fate**: The projected rise is driven mainly by increasing life expectancy — a success story. Age-specific incidence is falling.
- **Assuming a fixed dementia risk factor is unmodifiable**: Even "childhood education" (7%) is partly modifiable, because learning at any age enhances brain health regardless of years of formal schooling.

## Worked Example
Consider two 65-year-olds followed to age 100 in the Chicago Health and Aging Project framing. Person A has 0–1 healthy lifestyle factors; Person B has 4–5 (good diet, regular exercise, cognitive activity, non-smoker, moderate or no alcohol). At every age on the curve, Person B's cumulative dementia diagnosis rate is roughly half Person A's. Critically, this is not zero — at age 80, around 20% of the 4–5 factor group had still been diagnosed. That is the honest shape of the bet: you are not buying immunity, you are halving your odds. Wood pairs this observational finding with FINGER's randomized result — where a 2-year multidomain program actually raised cognitive function in high-risk 60- to 77-year-olds — to argue that the association is causal enough to act on now.

## Key Takeaways
1. Assume at least 45% and plausibly 70%+ of your dementia risk is modifiable — act accordingly.
2. Score yourself on the five factors (diet, exercise, cognitive activity, smoking, alcohol); moving from 0–1 to 4–5 is associated with halving dementia risk at any age.
3. Intervene on lifestyle and environment, never on the "source code" — the reductionist route has produced far more failure than success in dementia.
4. Stop waiting for mechanistic certainty. There is no such thing as proof in biology.
5. Multidomain beats single-domain: FINGER combined diet, aerobic + resistance exercise, cognitive training, and cardiovascular risk management, and it worked.
6. Protect against falls with strength and balance work — falls are the most common cause of head trauma in adults over 65.
7. Your cardiovascular risk factors are your brain's risk factors; treating blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol is brain work.
