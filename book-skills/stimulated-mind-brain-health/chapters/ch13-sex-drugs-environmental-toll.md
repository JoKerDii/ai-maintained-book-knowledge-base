# Chapter 13: Sex, Drugs, and Environmental Toll

## Core Idea
After movement, nourishment, stimulation, connection, and recovery, the remaining dementia risk factors — alcohol, smoking, cardiovascular disease, medications, hormones, frailty, infection, oral and gut health, pollutants, and social disadvantage — matter mainly through how they gate your engagement with stimulus or your physiology's ability to adapt and recover. Cardiovascular risk and dementia risk travel together.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 3-F Framework — Feel, Feed, Find (Rangan Chatterjee)**: for loosening a daily habit (nightly wine, ice cream, skipped gym).
  - When to use: when a behavior has become daily rather than occasional.
  - How: at the urge, ask (1) *What am I feeling?* (the most important question — it inserts a pause between thought and action), (2) *How does this habit feed that feeling?*, (3) *Can I find an alternative for when I feel this way?* Then **restructure the environment** — remove the trigger or add friction (one patient moved alcohol to a shed in the yard).
  - Why it works: "Understanding why you have a behavior immediately changes your relationship with it." Not stopping the behavior — building self-awareness first.
- **Anti-Frailty Tool Kit (Deborah Gordon)**: for prefrail/frail older adults.
  - **Jump** — restores fast-twitch fibers, the ones that catch a stumble. Women in their 60s: side-to-side box jumps, **3–4 sets of 15–20 seconds, 2x/week** (<5 min/week) improved leg strength and function; men in their 60s–70s: single-leg hops, **5 sets of 10 reps daily for 6 months**, improved muscle mass and strength. Practical target: ~**50 jumps in 5 minutes**.
  - **Blood-Flow Restriction (BFR / Kaatsu, Yoshiaki Sato, 1960s)** — cuffs at **30–50% above systolic blood pressure**; **3–5 sets of 20–30 reps**, **30–60 s rest**, cuffs stay inflated until all sets are done, ~5 minutes total. Safe and extensively used in frail, elderly, and rehab populations; check with your doctor.
  - **Protein hacks** — **0.55–0.7 g/lb/day** improved strength and function vs. standard guidelines (**0.35–0.45 g/lb/day**), where the control group lost muscle and strength over 12 weeks (type 2 diabetes + sarcopenia trial).
  - **Vitamins and omega-3** — see reference table.
- **Symptom-Guided Hormone Therapy**: MHT and testosterone therapy should be guided by symptoms and quality of life, not prescribed for dementia prevention.
  - When to use: perimenopausal/menopausal symptoms (hot flashes, insomnia, mood, sexual function) that block brain-supporting behaviors; or men with low testosterone plus depression, low libido, or erectile dysfunction.
  - Why: guidelines do not recommend MHT for dementia prevention; but symptom relief protects the behaviors that build headroom. A nerve block that controlled hot flashes (no hormonal action) improved cognition proportionally to symptom relief.

## Key Concepts
- **Endothelial dysfunction**: blood vessel lining stops responding to changes in blood-flow demand; stiffening vessels also lose the pulse waves that drive glymphatic clearance.
- **Neurofilament light chain (NfL)**: blood biomarker of neuronal injury; lower in those with better cardiovascular health (Chicago Health and Aging Project).
- **ApoB**: apolipoprotein B–containing particles driving atherosclerotic plaque; more strongly tied to dementia risk than LDL-C, with elevated risk above **118 mg/dL** (UK Biobank, >450,000 people).
- **Beers Criteria**: geriatric list (Mark Beers) of medications whose harms may outweigh benefits over age 65.
- **Frailty**: unintentional weight loss, slow walking speed, low grip strength, physical exhaustion, low physical activity — **1 criterion = prefrail, 3+ = frailty**, usually with elevated CRP.
- **Sarcopenic obesity**: low muscle mass with higher BMI; independently raises cognitive decline and dementia risk.
- **Periodontal disease**: gingivitis progressing to periodontitis, driven by overgrowth of *Porphyromonas gingivalis* — found in arterial plaques and inside amyloid plaques in Alzheimer's brains.
- **Gut-brain axis**: bidirectional communication; microbiome changes are largely downstream of host health, so improve the host, not the bugs.
- **Social Vulnerability Index**: education, income, housing, household structure — when matched on socioeconomic status, race no longer affected dementia risk in the Chicago Health and Aging Project.
- **Brain fog / subjective cognitive decline**: subjective concentration, memory, planning, decision-making, and language difficulties with normal test scores — a call to action, and reversible.

## Mental Models
- Think of dementia risk as cardiovascular risk with a longer lag — up to **90%** of dementia cases (Alzheimer's + vascular) involve vascular disease.
- Use symptoms, not hormone levels alone, to decide on MHT or testosterone therapy.
- Think of frailty as a vicious cycle: less muscle → insulin resistance and inflammation → falls, infection, bed rest → loss of stimulus → cognitive decline.
- Use "minimize time out of action" as the practical rule for illness, falls, and retirement — Deborah Gordon has seen function drop almost as soon as work's stimulation and connection disappear.
- Think of your microbiome as the one you have when you're healthy; 16S sequencing can tell you it's a dog, not whether it's a coyote or a labradoodle, and never what it's doing.

## Reference Tables

### Substances and thresholds
| Substance | Threshold / finding |
|---|---|
| Alcohol — heavy | >**2 drinks/day** raises cognitive decline, stroke, heart disease, several cancers, death |
| Alcohol — UK Biobank | ≥**2 UK units/day** (1 UK unit = 8 g ethanol ≈ half a US drink; US standard = **14 g** = 12 oz beer or 5 oz wine) → smaller gray and white matter, dose-dependent |
| Alcohol — author's position | little net benefit; **one or two drinks once or twice a week** probably won't harm brain health |
| Smoking | linear dose-response with dementia risk (meta-analysis, ~1,000,000 people); **former smokers not at increased risk** vs. never-smokers; heart/stroke risk halved within a few years, gone in **10–20 years** |
| Nicotine (patch/gum, non-smokers) | **1–4 mg** improves focus and working memory via acetylcholine receptors; neurotoxic at high doses, addictive |
| Caffeine | see Ch12 — last dose ~9 hours before bed |

### Cardiometabolic cutoffs
| Measure | Cutoff |
|---|---|
| Metabolic syndrome (South Korea, ~2,000,000 people) | waist ≥**35.5 in** men / **31.5 in** women; BP >**130/85 mmHg**; fasting glucose ≥**100 mg/dL**; triglycerides ≥**150 mg/dL**; HDL <**40 mg/dL** men / **50 mg/dL** women — all raised risk of pre-65 Alzheimer's and vascular dementia; glucose and BP largest |
| High blood pressure | above **130/80 mmHg**, dementia risk rises with BP |
| Antihypertensive benefit | reductions of **10 mmHg systolic / 4 mmHg diastolic** reduced later cognitive impairment and dementia (meta-analyses, one with ~100,000 people across 12 trials) |
| ApoB | risk above **118 mg/dL** |
| Hemoglobin | anemia <**12 g/dL** women / <**13 g/dL** men raises dementia risk; high >**15 g/dL** women / **17 g/dL** men often signals sleep apnea |
| Statin trials pending | STAREE, PREVENTABLE |

### Medications to watch
| Drug class | Concern |
|---|---|
| Antihypertensives | over-correction → dizziness, falls, head trauma; big BP swings raise dementia risk |
| Statins | myopathy limiting exercise; CoQ10 depletion — supplement **100–600 mg** for fatigue/muscle symptoms, or switch agent |
| Metformin | B12 deficiency — check levels regularly, supplement if needed |
| PPIs (omeprazole, esomeprazole) | altered magnesium and B12 absorption; dementia evidence mixed |
| Beers: first-generation antihistamines (chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine) | block acetylcholine → confusion, falls, dementia with extended use |
| Beers: antipsychotics (haloperidol, quetiapine, risperidone, olanzapine) | accelerate decline in people already diagnosed with dementia |
| Beers: all benzodiazepines | cognitive impairment in older adults |

### Supplements, oral, and environmental
| Item | Dose / action |
|---|---|
| Omega-3 (EPA/DHA) for sarcopenia | **3–4 servings fish/week** or **2–4 g/day** |
| Vitamin K2 (MK-4 or MK-7) | benefit from ≥**1 mg/day**, usually **40–50 mg/day**; food: natto, sauerkraut, eel, cheese, liver, chicken |
| Vitamin D, magnesium, calcium, protein, physical activity | all required together for bone health |
| Brushing / flossing | ≥**2 minutes twice daily** + interdental cleaning; dentist a couple of times a year |
| Xylitol gum | **2–3 g, 2–3x/day** (Malawi trial: gum twice daily in >10,000 pregnant women cut preterm births and babies ≤3 lb) |
| Shingles vaccine | recommended over age **50**; Wales natural experiment (eligibility cutoff birthdate Sept 1, 1933) and Australia 2016 both showed lower dementia rates (Zostavax) |
| Air pollution | second-highest global cause of death, below high blood pressure and above smoking; **N95** filters the **2.5-micron** particles; indoor air often several times worse than outdoor; air purifiers improved BP in hypertension trial; good **B vitamin** intake attenuated the pollution-dementia link |
| Water | filter for lead and **PFAS**; drink filtered tap over plastic-bottled |
| Microplastics | reduce via glass storage, wood/metal utensils, fresh foods; anthocyanins and **β-glucan** (oats, mushrooms) may help |

## Worked Example
Deborah Gordon, a primary care physician in her mid-seventies (competitive rower, lifts weights, grows her own food), applies the whole chapter to one prefrail patient archetype. She starts resistance training not with weights but with **jumping** — a few sets of hops to reawaken fast-twitch fibers and cut fall risk in under five minutes a week. She adds **BFR cuffs** (a set the author gave her) so the patient can train hard without heavy loads. Because frail patients undereat, she pushes protein to **0.55–0.7 g/lb/day**, using bars or powders when whole food falls short, plus omega-3s and K2 for muscle and bone. She vaccinates against flu, pneumonia, and shingles to prevent the multi-week illnesses that erase stimulus. She tells every patient to **floss daily**. And she plans to keep working into her eighties, having watched otherwise-healthy patients decline as soon as retirement removed their cognitive stimulation and social connection.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat cardiovascular risk as brain risk — address BP, glucose, ApoB/LDL-C with lifestyle first, medication when lifestyle isn't enough.
2. Alcohol: no strong case for abstinence, no real benefit either; keep it occasional and contextual (a good meal, good company), avoid daily drinking.
3. Smoking: no safe amount, linear risk — but quitting returns former smokers to never-smoker dementia risk.
4. Raise potassium (fruit, lentils, beans, vegetables, potatoes, dairy, fresh meat and fish) and nitrate (beets, leafy greens) while cutting processed high-sodium foods — most sodium comes pre-added, not from your shaker.
5. Audit medications: check B12 on metformin/PPIs, CoQ10 for statin myopathy, and apply Beers Criteria after 65.
6. Fight frailty with jumping, BFR, protein, and vaccination — and avoid the long gaps of no stimulus caused by illness, falls, or retirement.
7. Do the cheap environmental wins: floss and see the dentist, filter water, run an air purifier where you spend time, mask on wildfire days.
