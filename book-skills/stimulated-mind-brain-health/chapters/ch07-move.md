# Chapter 7: Move

## Core Idea
Movement is not an "intervention" but a canonical requirement of human physiology; the modern problem is the absence of movement, so any increase above your current level improves cognition and lowers dementia risk. Build activity as a funnel you progressively add to, not a prescription you must hit.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Movement Funnel**: five levels — Snack → Propel → Resist → Sprint → Coordinate. Enter at the widest part (snack) and add layers over time; lower levels have similar importance so similar width.
  - When to use: designing or auditing any physical activity practice for brain health, at any starting fitness level.
  - How: (1) Snack — break up sedentary time at least every hour, dynamic bursts 1–2× a day; (2) Propel — extended moderate movement, build to 30–40 min ≥3×/week; (3) Resist — whole-body resistance 1–3×/week; (4) Sprint — HIIT 1–3×/week, 20–30 min total sessions; (5) Coordinate — open-skill/play movement layered onto any level.
  - Why it works: it is inverted (funnel, not pyramid) because the highest-yield, lowest-barrier work is the widest entry point; every layer benefits the brain even without the ones below it.
- **Exercise Snacks**: brief bouts (seconds to a few minutes) of movement inserted to break prolonged sitting.
  - When to use: desk work, long sedentary stretches; the mandatory starting point if you do nothing else.
  - How: squat snack = stand up, sit back toward the chair without letting it take your weight, stand, ×15, every 20 minutes. Or stairs, push-ups, lunges, calf raises, skipping, jogging in place, hopping, jumping jacks.
  - Why it works: only *unbroken* sedentary periods raised dementia risk in 50,000 UK adults aged 60+; breaking them up offsets the risk.
- **Dan John's Five Basic Human Movements**: Push, Pull, Hinge, Squat, Carry — a gym-free template for building any resistance program with any equipment.
  - When to use: training at home, travelling, or without machines.
  - How: pick one loaded movement per category; 4 sets/week per body part, 6–15 reps, as a circuit if time-constrained.
- **Norwegian 4 × 4 Protocol**: 4 minutes hard, 3 minutes rest, repeated 4× (~25 min).
  - When to use: time-efficient fitness and hippocampal preservation, 1–3×/week.

## Key Concepts
- **Exerkines**: signalling compounds released during exercise by muscle, liver, pancreas, and bone (myokines are the muscle subset).
- **BDNF**: brain-derived neurotrophic factor; supports neurons and neuroplasticity — the "cognitive tonic" that builds headroom.
- **Lactate**: not waste; crosses into the brain via transporters and directly drives brain BDNF production — the mechanism making intensity matter.
- **Sarcopenia**: muscle's version of dementia — loss of muscle function severe enough to impair daily activities; directly tied to cognitive decline.
- **Open-skill vs closed-skill**: unpredictable, reactive movement (ball/racket/board sports) vs pure fitness/strength movement (running, cycling).
- **Relative strength**: strength adjusted for body size — the metric that predicted processing speed; "strong enough for you," not absolute load.
- **Medicalization of exercise** (Lieberman): treating activity as a prescribable pill, which makes sub-"ideal" movement feel worthless.
- **Primal Play** (Darryl Edwards): re-creating childhood games, crawling, chasing, climbing as the movement modality — mostly noncontact, low risk.
- **MET-minutes**: intensity (metabolic equivalents) × minutes — how total physical activity dose is quantified.

## Mental Models
- Think of movement as a funnel you pour into, not a pyramid you climb — you enter at the widest, easiest point and everything below is additive.
- Use "everything counts" when someone believes they have no time: 1–2 minutes of vigorous activity per day already shows benefit.
- Think of muscle and fitness as headroom savings — a buffer of strength and brain volume you draw down decades later.
- Use open-skill/coordinative movement when you want more brain per unit of intensity: matched-intensity trials favor the coordinative arm.

## Anti-patterns
- **"No pain, no gain"**: makes people opt out of exercise entirely; pain is optional and misaligned with the science.
- **Long unbroken sitting even in exercisers**: very long sedentary stretches may raise dementia risk despite regular training.
- **Chasing more intensity/volume**: after a hard session you should leave energized, not exhausted; more is not better without pro-athlete recovery.
- **Long warm-ups and static stretching before lifting**: not needed for the average resistance session; a couple of light sets suffices.
- **Avoiding resistance training for joint/back pain**: in many cases those with joint or back pain need *more* resistance, not less.

## Worked Example
**The hippocampus-reversal walking protocol.** Adults in their 60s–70s started at 10 minutes of walking, 3×/week, adding 5 minutes per week until they reached 40 minutes, then progressively raised intensity while still walking. Over one year, hippocampal volume rose ~2% instead of the expected 1–2% decline; those who gained the most fitness gained the most hippocampal volume, and gains tracked rises in BDNF. Translate: pick any activity that raises heart rate similarly (cycling, rowing, wheelchair propulsion), start at 10 min × 3/week, +5 min weekly to 40 min, then add intensity rather than more time.

**Contrast — the HIIT version.** Adults aged 65–85 randomized to no exercise vs 30 min moderate activity vs a 25-min Norwegian 4×4 (4 min running / 3 min rest × 4), 3×/week for 6 months. Moderate helped; HIIT produced much larger memory gains and preserved hippocampal size, sustained at least 4 years post-study.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start at "snack": break sitting at least hourly and add 1–2 dynamic bursts a day — this is free, equipment-free, and benefits even trained people.
2. Walk more, with no threshold to hit: benefit is linear to ~10,000 steps; 8,000–12,000 is the honest range.
3. Build to 30–40 minutes of brisk activity 3×/week, then raise intensity rather than duration.
4. Lift for the whole body 1–3×/week, 1–3 sets of 5–15 reps; minimum effective dose is 4 sets/week/body part. It never has to be heavy — only challenging in the last few reps.
5. Aim for top-third relative strength; a 2× body weight leg press is an excellent leg target.
6. Add sprints once a base exists: 10–60 s hard, rest 2–4× as long, ~10 min of work; the lactate → BDNF pathway is intensity-dependent.
7. Layer coordination (racket sports, dance, tai chi, yoga, Pilates, animal moves) onto any level — matched for intensity, coordinative exercise wins on cognition and BDNF.
