## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The central thesis
**The brain is a product of the environment** — and environments are changeable, so long-term cognitive function is malleable. Adult brains adapt at any age, but unlike young brains they need *a good reason to*. The founding parable: Stanford's barn owls in prism glasses. Adults fed dead mice never rewired; adults forced to **hunt live mice**, or **housed with other birds**, adapted fine. Challenge and social engagement are the two demonstrated triggers. A life where everything arrives without effort is "a perfect metaphor for modern human life."

Zoom **out**, not in. Amyloid explains only 20–25% of cognition and decline — meaning 75–80% sits elsewhere, in modifiable territory. At least 45% of dementia is preventable (Lancet 2024), plausibly over 70% (UK Biobank, ~350k people).

### The 3-S Model (Ch 14 — the book's capstone)
Brain function is a **network, not a checklist**. Three coupled nodes drive brain structure and function, which produce focus, performance, and lowered dementia risk:
1. **Stimulus** — cognitive demand through mental and social activity. The origin station; connects to everything.
2. **Supply** — oxygen, energy, nutrients. Requires cardiovascular health, good energy regulation, no insulin resistance.
3. **Support** — neuroplasticity, maintenance, repair during rest and sleep. Augmented by BDNF, IGF-1, hormones; impaired by allostatic load, alcohol, smoking, pollutants.

They're physically coupled: challenging the brain draws blood via **neurovascular coupling**; challenged cells then repair during sleep. Improved function permits wider stimuli, which improves function further — "just like lifting weights."

**Use it to decide where to act.** Busy demanding job → add supply/support. Retired or unrewarding work → add stimulus. Time-poor → set-and-forget (water filter, air purifier, multivitamin, sardines). Unsure → start with sleep; it greases everything. **Prefer activities hitting multiple nodes**: group/coordinative exercise, outdoor volunteering, in-person classes.

**Pick exactly one thing.** "When you only have the capacity to do one thing, focusing on two makes it likely that zero will happen."

### Headroom and the Three R's (Ch 1)
**Headroom** (from economist Art De Vany) is the prize: reserve capacity above daily demand, available when you need it. If you need 25 lb per hand for groceries but can deadlift 100, groceries feel light and surprise loads don't injure you. Composed of:
- **Cognitive reserve** — total capacity; how much you can lose before impairment. The *software*.
- **Brain reserve / maintenance** — structural volume. The *hardware*.
- **Cognitive resilience** — tolerance of accumulated damage. The *redundancy*.
- **Resolve** — Wood's addition: shedding the expectation of decline and committing to lifelong engagement.

The same lifestyle inputs improve all of them, so you rarely optimize them separately. **Headroom is buildable at any age.**

### The Five Rules of the Brain Game (Ch 6)
Rules 1–3 govern *action*; rules 4–5 structure the *rest* that lets adaptation happen. Deliberately complementary opposites.
1. **Prepare for your own race** — intersect where you'll benefit most with what you can do consistently. Everything else is distraction.
2. **Never give up** — accumulate. A skipped workout, bad nights, or a whole cake make very little difference across decades.
3. **Don't go it alone** — ask for help; many actions are easier or enhanced with others. Find your *why* (it almost always involves people).
4. **Everything counts** — small changes have real impact; let yourself celebrate them.
5. **Be kind to yourself (and others)** — no value judgments about how you or anyone acts on health information.

### Play the game, don't hack the source code (Ch 5)
Josh Turknett's parable: aliens find a phone with a game. Team One just plays and wins; Team Two dissects the source code and can't clear a level. Intervene at lifestyle and environment, never at amyloid and microglia. We still can't fully explain how exercise works — that has never justified not exercising. Reductionism is right for broken limbs and single-gene disease; it fails for network conditions like dementia.

**The five healthy lifestyle factors** (Chicago Health and Aging Project): diet, exercise, cognitive activity, smoking, alcohol. **4–5 factors → half the dementia risk** at any age vs. 0–1.

### Mismatch (Ch 1, 4)
The gap between what the brain evolved to receive and what modern life delivers. **Retirement is the archetype** — often the point cognitive decline accelerates, because work's cognitive and social stimulus was removed. Wood's diagnostic: audit which developmental input you've dropped (novel effortful learning, movement, social complexity, sleep) and treat the drop as the intervention point.

### Master mental models
- **Amyloid is rust; your brain is the car** — and **the brain is its own mechanic**. Activity signals where maintenance gets done, so *not* using the brain is actively pro-aging.
- **Stack the deck, don't buy immunity** — you're shifting probabilities. At 80, ~20% of the 4–5 factor group still got diagnosed — but that's half the risk.
- **Progress, not perfection** — setting lower goals with a broader definition of what counts improves adherence *and* how you feel. Believing you exercise less than peers is independently associated with higher mortality, even adjusting for actual activity.
- **Train the functions you want to keep** — expect **near transfer only**. Skill holds up with recent deliberate practice; don't blame age for a practice deficit.
- **Multidomain beats heroic** — FINGER, POINTER, Maintain Your Brain, and SMARRT all show that modest, personalized, combined interventions work where single levers don't.
- **You can't change just one thing** — raise physical activity and you also cut sedentary time and improve blood pressure, blood sugar, sleep, and possibly food preferences.
