## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**Audience as hero / presenter as mentor.** The audience is the star, not you — they decide whether the idea spreads or dies. Take the Yoda stance and deliver three things: a **special gift** (insight that improves their lives), a **"magical" tool** (a skill or mind-set serving their objectives *and* yours), and help getting **unstuck**. People came to see what you can do for them, not what they must do for you.

**The Big Idea.** The one key message, stated as a **complete sentence** with a subject (often "you") and a verb, carrying two required components: **your point of view** and **what's at stake**. "Software updates" is a topic. "Your department will struggle to meet key production deadlines until we update the workflow management software" is a big idea. It is also your only legitimate filter when cutting content.

**What is / what could be** (the sparkline structure). Persuasive communicators create conflict by juxtaposing **what is** with **what could be** — establishing the status quo until heads nod, opening a gap, then toggling back and forth so the present grows unappealing and the alternative alluring — finally arriving at the **new bliss**, the rewarded state reached by adopting the idea. Never announce the transition; make it felt. Never end on a task list.

**The three-level ending.** Describe future rewards as benefits **to them**, **to their sphere** (peers, reports, customers), and **to the world**.

**S.T.A.R. moment — Something They'll Always Remember.** One climactic moment per talk that drives the big idea home and is what people repeat afterward. Four types: **shocking statistics** (amplified, not glided over), **evocative visuals**, **memorable dramatization** (Bill Gates releasing mosquitoes at TED), **emotive anecdote**.

**The transformation arc.** Before writing anything, map "move audience **from** → move audience **to**" — the beliefs, behaviors, and emotions that must change. This is the most critical planning step, because the endpoint is the entire reason you're presenting.

**The five segmentation lenses.** Politics, demographics, psychographics, firmographics, ethnographics. Then act as **curator of content** for the single highest-influence subgroup, keeping enough general material not to alienate the rest.

**Resonance.** Tap the audience's **resonant frequency** — shared experiences, common goals, your qualifications — so people move because you touched something they already believe, not because you pushed.

**The three types of resistance.** **Logical** (read the opposing literature), **emotional** (a bias, dogma, or moral code your idea violates — honor the value at risk), **practical** (physically or financially hard — name the sacrifice and shoulder part of it). Every audience resists; pre-empt in the talk and pre-sell the loudest critics privately.

**Dynamic opposites.** Build tension from contrast pairs — past/future, need/fulfillment, stagnation/growth, sacrifice/reward, budget/quality — and generate plenty of material on *both* sides or momentum dies.

**Four calls to action.** Audiences can be **doers, suppliers, influencers, or innovators**. Pick one, break it into discrete tasks and deadlines, and state the benefit to them.

**Divergent → convergent.** Generate alone (mind mapping), then in a group (sticky notes, one idea each, no judgment), then alone again. Then converge: filtering is the hard part, and if you don't filter, the audience will and will resent you for it.

**The slide-title test.** Write points as clear, active, verb-carrying titles in outline mode before designing anything, then ask: "If people read only the titles, will they get what I'm saying?" They scan titles like headlines and decide from them whether to keep listening.

**The grandmother test.** Translate every specialized term central to your message. Unclear ideas don't get adopted — and obscurity limits careers.

**The glance test.** Every slide must be comprehensible in **three seconds** — think billboards. Audiences read *or* listen, never both: 40 text-heavy slides in a 40-minute talk costs the audience ~13 minutes of your talk. Start from a clean surface, limit and enlarge text, unify type and color, arrange deliberately.

**Storyboard one idea per slide.** Sketch on 1.5″ × 2″ sticky notes before building. Slides are free — give every idea its own moment onstage. A concept that won't fit the note is too complex.

**The 60% rule.** Plan content for 60% of your slot, keep cut slides in a **slide cemetery**, rehearse counting *up* while trimming and *down* once in range, and plant a **false ending** and a **real ending** so overrunning is survivable.

**Persuade beyond the stage.** The presentation starts when you commit to speak and continues through follow-up: reinforce **before** (agenda, prereading, benefit stated), **during** (handouts, props, posted assets), and **after** (notes, e-mail recapping the new bliss, accessibility, next-steps meetings).

**The backchannel.** Online chatter is constructive (enriches, archives, connects, extends reach) or destructive (distracts, derails, excludes, flattens nuance, turns snarky). Avoid a **backchannel revolt** by making people online feel *heard*: give a hashtag, assign a moderator, repeat and validate audience sentiment. Reciprocity is the currency.
