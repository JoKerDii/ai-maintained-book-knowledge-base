# Chapter 10: Section 6A — Rehearsal, Venue, Technology, Stage Fright, Tone, and Authenticity
*Nancy Duarte*

*(Groups six chapters from Section 6 — Delivery: Rehearse Your Material Well; Know the Venue and Schedule; Anticipate Technology Glitches; Manage Your Stage Fright; Set the Right Tone for Your Talk; Be Yourself.)*

## Core Idea
There's no such thing as overrehearsing — rehearsal, venue reconnaissance, and tech checks exist to free you to be *present* with the audience, and presence plus authenticity is what earns trust.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The five rehearsal activities** (know the material inside out; do *not* memorize it, or you'll come across stiff and struggle to connect):
  1. **Get honest feedback from a skilled presenter** — hand her a printout and have her note what you say well, what you don't, what's essential, what's distracting ("when you put it that way, people won't follow you"; "that term sounds derogatory"). You're too attached to your ideas to hear them as the audience will.
  2. **Prepare a short version** — technology fails, prior speakers run long, executives interrupt. Craft and rehearse a much shorter cut in advance.
  3. **Fiddle with your slides** — tweaking text and images up to the day of the talk *is* rehearsal; it makes slides feel integrated rather than tacked on.
  4. **Rehearse a few times in slide-show mode** — it hides the notes view, forcing familiarity and exposing choppy transitions, inconsistent graphics, and awkward builds.
  5. **Practice on camera** — a webcam or phone is enough. Address the lens as a person, then review for stage presence, eye contact, facial expressions, gestures, and ease of movement; fix wherever you don't look natural or in command.

- **The four venue variables**: **Floor and seating plan** (room arrangement and size — better for people to sit close and feed off each other's energy than to be lost in a cavernous space; stage or floor level; room to walk; where the lights hit — tape off dark pockets; obstructing poles; remove the podium unless you need it for notes, since it's a barrier). **Food plan** (near a mealtime? provide or budget time for it; speaking over cutlery needs amplification). **Show flow** (order of events, who introduces you, who speaks before and after and on what — reference others' points; keep it short if you're late in a long list; prepare to counter a preceding contrary view; check competing sessions). **Recording** (locate cameras and look at them to connect with remote viewers; state distribution restrictions to organizers explicitly).

- **The nine-item technology checklist** — arrange a walkthrough, or give yourself *at least* 30 minutes: get to know the AV person by name and treat him well; test all equipment (projector, clicker, audio); bring backups (your own projector, cables, clicker, audio — plus content backed up on drives and in the cloud and printed); prerecord your demos in case the connection fails; test the slide deck by clicking through every single slide in the room; try out the comfort monitors (confirm you can actually read them from the stage); play all media (confirm files transferred and file types work on the venue machine); confirm type of projection (aspect ratio 16:9 or 4:3, front- or rear-projected — tape the floor so you don't walk through the beam); find out if people will attend remotely (last-minute equipment swaps are where files get lost).

- **The five stage-fright techniques** (fight-or-flight is your brain reading the audience as a threat; mild nerves are useful, like a shot of adrenaline): **quiet your mind** (stop self-critical chatter; walk outside; soothing music); **breathe** (sit, breathe deeply and hold, take one more gasp to fill the lungs further, release very slowly — four times in a row calms the body in under a minute); **laugh** (releases tension, not just distraction); **visualize** (Nick Morgan: role-play in your mind a communication with your favorite person, forming a memory of what it feels like *physically* — your hands, your proximity — then use that behavior); **remember your audience's flaws** (studying their resistance also revealed how human and frail they are).

- **The four tone-setting levers**: **Precommunication** (a thoughtfully written agenda with a concise but telling subject line, explicit about what the audience gets — all pre-talk communication affects credibility). **Atmosphere** (cold and sparse for a chilling speech; bright lighting for casual, dimmer for formal; refreshments; music, props, projected images). **Appearance** (Guy Kawasaki: match your audience, or dress for a "tie" — underdress and you say "I don't respect you," overdress and you say "I'm better than you"; suit up for clients and investors, dress down to signal accessibility to new direct reports). **Disposition** (somber for a layoff announcement, not smiley; chat and shake hands before an upbeat talk; always available and sincere).

- **Authenticity beats polish**: transparency wins people over. Victoria Labalme: audiences will "forgive a stumble, an 'um,' or a section where you backtrack as long as they know that your heart is in the right place," and "authenticity means claiming who you are" — avoid corporate-spokesperson delivery, but don't perform false humility either; playing small and meek when you and the audience both know you're a giant wins no fans.

## Key Concepts
- **Comfort monitor** — presenter-facing teleprompter screen; verify legibility *from the stage*, not from the desk.
- **Short version** — a pre-rehearsed compressed cut held in reserve.
- **Muscle memory** — the goal of on-camera rehearsal; feel the gesture so it repeats reliably.
- **Backchannel of the room** — physical tone signals (lighting, refreshments, seating density) that prime the audience before you speak.

## Mental Models
- Think of rehearsal as buying presence: the better you know it, the more of you is available to the people in front of you.
- Audiences can tell when you wing it, and they read it as "you don't value my time."
- Use authenticity as a range, not a setting: Steve Ballmer's sweaty, over-the-top cheerleading and Susan Cain's quiet TED 2012 talk on introversion both worked because each matched the speaker and the subject matter.

## Worked Example
**The cost of assuming the room.** Invited to speak to 70 people at Google, the mental image was rows of chairs. The reality was a tiny conference room with 20 people crammed in and 50 webcam faces projected on the wall. With advance knowledge, the right move was to facilitate a conversation, not deliver a formal presentation; instead the talk required constant on-the-fly adjustments — where to stand, where to aim eye contact — that broke stride.

**The comfort monitor catch.** At a technical walkthrough the day before a talk that quoted lengthy excerpts from famous speeches, the comfort monitors turned out to be too small to read anything from the stage. Doubling the font size that night converted a certain embarrassment into a non-event — which is the entire argument for the walkthrough.

**Recording without restrictions.** Speaking very personally to 250 professional women about overcoming obstacles, on the belief that only attendees could access the password-protected recording. A local TV channel broadcast the entire presentation. State distribution limits to organizers *before* you get raw.

## Key Takeaways
1. Know the material cold; never memorize it word for word.
2. Always have a rehearsed short version — something usually goes wrong with time.
3. Click through every slide in the actual room on the actual machine.
4. Bring your own backups of anything critical, plus printouts.
5. Manage nerves physiologically (breath, laughter) and cognitively (visualization, audience fallibility).
6. Everything before the first word — invitation, room, clothing, demeanor — is already setting tone.
7. Be recognizably yourself; audiences forgive stumbles far more readily than they forgive performance.
