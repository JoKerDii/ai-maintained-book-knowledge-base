## Core Idea
Arrangement, data restraint, and diagram vocabulary are what let an audience process a slide fast enough to keep listening — and slide *count* matters far less than whether each slide earns its click.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The five arrangement principles**:
  - **Flow** — placement governs how the eye travels; people should cross the slide in one back-and-forth motion and be done.
  - **Contrast** — eyes go to what stands out; create it through size, shape, color, and proximity (e.g., blurred backgrounds throwing stark white illustrations into high relief).
  - **White space** — the open space around items of interest. Resist filling it: a healthy amount imparts a feeling of luxury (advertisers find it raises perceived value) and sharpens focus by isolating elements. It needn't literally be white — the design just needs to feel spacious.
  - **Hierarchy** — a clear order of importance so viewers instantly find the key element (e.g., picture → large percentage → supporting copy).
  - **Unity** — slides that look like one person made them, via consistent type styles, color, image treatment, and element placement.

- **Six rules for clarifying data**: audiences read a data slide in a multistep sequence — titles, then the shapes the data make, then the axes — and can't pull the chart closer or linger.
  1. **Highlight what's important** — ask "What would I like people to remember about the data?" and give that visual emphasis: the relevant bars in rich color, the rest gray; deemphasize gridlines, borders, axes, and labels because you supply that context by speaking.
  2. **Tell the truth** — omit 3-D effects when there's no z-axis (foreground pie pieces look deceptively large); never alter axis proportions to inflate or flatten a change; square gridlines keep data true.
  3. **Pick the right chart for the job** — pies, bars, matrices, and line graphs serve different purposes. Use a line graph over bars if the line's shape carries your point; use a matrix over multiple pies to show relationships between data points.
  4. **Sometimes the best chart is no chart** — if one number conveys the message, show just that number, huge.
  5. **Find the narrative in the data** — explain not just the *what* but the *why* and the *how*: what made the numbers move, what impact people had, how people will be affected.
  6. **Use concrete comparisons to express magnitude** — millions, billions, and trillions sound alike and aren't. To convey a billion square feet, hold up a 12″ × 12″ carpet square and say it would take a billion of them to cover Manhattan.

- **The five-category diagram taxonomy** (derived from sorting thousands of sketches from 20+ years of client work): **Network** (hub-and-spokes: stakeholders across departments converging on an initiative), **Segment** (a donut: separate products within a suite), **Join** (a hook: a supply-chain partner relationship), **Flow** (parallel arrows: two teams working in concert), **Stack** (vertical layers: fiscal-year goals as building blocks toward profitability).
  - How to convert bullets to a diagram: find a bullet list — the bullets already "feel" related, which is why you grouped them. Circle the verbs or nouns and ask *how* they're related. That relationship falls into one of the five categories; swap in a diagram from it. Repeat on other text slides.

- **Slide count variables** — count depends on audience, technology, setting, your pacing, and comfort with a clicker:
  - **No slides** — for a very personal connection or a short casual talk. Andrew Dlugan: no slides for a commencement speech, a eulogy, a wedding toast, or a layoff announcement. Unsure? Bring them plus printed notes and decide on arrival.
  - **Moderate count** — the common recommendation of 1–2 slides per minute (30–60 for an hour); typical of corporate decks, but most cram too much per slide. One idea per slide often yields more than 60.
  - **High count** — ~5 slides per minute; keeps audiences alert because they visually reengage on each click, but demands heavy rehearsal and careful pacing. Duarte typically uses 145 slides in a 40-minute talk (up to 300 clicks counting builds) — audiences guess she used 30 to 50.
  - **Social media count** — the most popular SlideShare decks run 75+ slides readable in 2–3 minutes, built like children's books: sentence, visual, sentence, visual.
  - Rule: "Don't worry about slide count. Just make your *slides* count."

- **The four tests of effective animation**: it **shows how things work** (control eye movement to reveal assembly, change, direction, or sequence — e.g., a drawer sliding out from behind a box to reveal detail); it **creates contrast** (show many unanimated slides so animation stands out when used); it **looks natural** (elements enter, interact, and leave like actors — controlled, not frenetic); it **does not annoy** (spinning, twitching, twirling clarifies nothing and wastes your time).

## Key Concepts
- **Glance-test debt** — visually complex data the audience has no time to decode before you click away.
- **Gray-out** — muting non-essential chart elements so the eye lands on the point.
- **Visual taxonomy** — the reusable vocabulary of relationship diagrams.
- **Build** — sequential reveal within a slide; counts toward clicks, not slide count.
- **Rhinestone problem** — adding every available effect until the audience is blinded by bling and can't pick anything out.

## Mental Models
- Think of white space as an asset with measurable perceived value, not as waste.
- Use a matrix when you want relationships and a line when you want a trajectory; the chart type is an argument, not a container.
- If the eye doesn't know where to begin, you have a hierarchy problem, not a content problem.
- Think of animation as punctuation: valuable because it's rare.

## Worked Example
**Pies that hide the point.** A slide breaks airline ticket sales into three channels — online, agents, direct — using one pie per airline. Little can be deduced, because the pies look visually similar. Reformatting the same numbers as a **matrix** makes it immediately obvious that total sales for Airline 3 are nearly double the others. Same data, different argument, because the chart type changed.

**Decluttering a revenue chart.** In the "before," gridlines and borders carry the same visual weight as the data, so the eye has no entry point. In the "after" — borderless, with muted axes and gridlines — viewers see at once that revenue leveled off after an early-year spike.

## Key Takeaways
1. Arrange for one back-and-forth eye path per slide; more than that and you've lost the listener.
2. Deemphasize everything you will say out loud; the chart doesn't need to carry context you're providing.
3. Never distort dimensions or add depth that isn't in the data.
4. Convert related bullet lists into Network/Segment/Join/Flow/Stack diagrams.
5. Slide count is a style choice; per-slide clarity is not.
6. Use animation to explain mechanism and create contrast — never as decoration.
