# Chapter 7: Section 4 — Media (Identify the Best Modes for Communicating Your Message)
*Nancy Duarte*

*(Groups the five chapters of Section 4: Choose the Right Vehicle for Your Message; Make the Most of Slide Software; Determine the Right Length for Your Presentation; Persuade Beyond the Stage; Share the Stage.)*

## Core Idea
A time slot is not a mandate for a slide deck: choose the vehicle from how the audience prefers to process information, keep slide software's three jobs separate, budget only ~60% of your slot, and treat the talk as a before/during/after campaign rather than a single event.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Choose the vehicle before the software**: audience size and level of interaction determine media. Options include props, handouts, sketches, tablets, videos, flipcharts, memos, and video — presentations can be broadcast, streamed, downloaded, and distributed.
  - When to use: Before opening presentation software, every time.
  - Why it works: A slick deck signals everything is final; sketching while people watch signals your thinking is formative and they can still weigh in. When two vendors pitched a digital storage system, the one that whiteboarded a full storage and network plan won over the one that ran an undeviating deck — the whiteboard felt collaborative, not canned.

- **The three separate jobs of slide software** — keep them apart to avoid the standard PowerPoint failures:
  1. **Create documents** — the default template is actually a document template. Great for dense, readable material. But *never project* a document; circulate it before or after.
  2. **Compose teleprompter notes** — bulleted slides are fine as prompts, but again *don't project them*. Write in Notes view, then Set Up Show → Presenter View, so notes appear on your comfort monitor and only visuals project. Bring printouts as backup. (Sheryl Sandberg's TEDWomen talk showed no slides to the room; her bulleted slides were on the comfort monitor only.)
  3. **Visualize ideas** — the only thing you should project: images, graphics, and phrases that move ideas along. Strip everything that exists to remind *you* what to say.
  - Failure mode: the **slideument** (Garr Reynolds' term) — a text-heavy hybrid projected as a read-along.

- **The 60% rule and five ways to tighten**: plan content for 60% of your slot (40 minutes of a 60-minute hour), leaving room for Q&A or discussion.
  1. **Plan for 60% of your time slot.**
  2. **Trim your slide deck** — cutting an hour to 40 minutes means cutting roughly a third of slides; drag them to a "slide cemetery" at the end of the file rather than deleting, in case you need one during Q&A.
  3. **Practice with the clock counting up** — while cutting, so you know *how much* you're over.
  4. **Practice with a timer counting down** — once in range, divide content into quarters and know the exact slide you should be on at each mark so you can trim on the fly.
  5. **Have two natural ending points** — a false ending (a summary) and a real ending (a rousing story). Running long, drop the second and still land the message.

- **Persuade beyond the stage — before / during / after**: the presentation starts the moment you commit to speak and continues through follow-up.
  - **Before**: e-mail a summary and rough point list, or a meeting request with a detailed agenda; for external talks post bio, talking points, and prereading links. Make the audience's benefit explicit.
  - **During**: bring surplus handouts and recruit volunteers to distribute them at the right moment; tape secret messages under chairs; use color-coded response cards; hand out a prop or prototype; post slides, video, and photos online to fuel coverage.
  - **After**: thank-you note, survey, or supplementary material — but never overtly solicit. Feedback requests must return value the audience cares about.

- **Share the stage**: audiences accustomed to rapid scene changes won't sit through a monologue. Reengage them by **bringing in other presenters** (experts, analysts, or teammates playing to strengths — let the quick-witted colleague run Q&A) and **mixing up your media** (posters, exhibits, tchotchkes, props unveiled by a helper, video for humor/testimonial/animated infographics, live product demos, flipchart or whiteboard drawing, or a hired graphic recorder producing a mural of the talk in real time).

## Key Concepts
- **Slideument** — a document masquerading as a slide deck; boring to project, inefficient to read aloud.
- **Comfort monitor / Presenter View** — the presenter-only screen for teleprompter notes.
- **Slide cemetery** — a holding area at the end of the file for cut slides.
- **False ending / real ending** — two rehearsed exits giving you a graceful bail-out.
- **30–40 minute tolerance** — the point past which audiences, conditioned by television, begin to squirm.
- **Graphic recorder** — a visual practitioner who synthesizes the talk onto butcher paper as it happens.

## Mental Models
- Think of the deck as *one* possible container, not the default one; analysts may prefer a memo, young professionals a video.
- Use low tech deliberately: sketching in front of people is a trust move, not a shortfall.
- Think of time discipline as respect — audiences never scold you for ending early, only for ending late.
- If your content can be distributed and understood without a presenter, you built a document. That's fine — just don't project it.

## Worked Example
**Converting a slideument you didn't mean to write.** You realize the deck is dense prose. Instead of forcing it onstage: divide the content into clear sections, add a linked table of contents, add page numbers, convert fragments and phrases into complete sentences, and distribute as PDF rather than a deck. Edelman's Nolan Haims goes further and sets slideuments up in *portrait* layout, so it's visually obvious to staff that these are documents in the making, not visual aids to be projected. Then build a separate, sparse visual deck for the room.

**The two endings, in practice.** At a TED event in India with a rehearsed 15-minute talk, a severe chest cold and medication threw off the pacing; the "time's up" light blinked before the material was done. Because a first, natural ending had been planted, the talk closed there with a salutation to India drawn from a famous Indian speech — and the audience never knew it wasn't the intended finale.

## Key Takeaways
1. Decide the medium from audience preference and venue, not from habit.
2. Never project documents or teleprompter notes; project only what helps the audience understand and retain.
3. Budget 60% of your slot for content; rehearse counting up while cutting, counting down once in range.
4. Keep cut slides in a cemetery — Q&A often resurrects them.
5. Plant two endings so overrunning is survivable.
6. Design contact points before, during, and after the talk; reinforcement across all three is what changes behavior.
7. Introduce a new voice or new medium every so often; continual novelty is what holds attention.
