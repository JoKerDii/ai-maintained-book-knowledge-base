# Chapter 12: Section 7 — Impact (Measure and Increase Your Presentation's Impact)
*Nancy Duarte*

*(Groups the four chapters of Section 7: Build Relationships Through Social Media; Spread Your Ideas with Social Media; Gauge Whether You've Connected with People; Follow Up After Your Talk.)*

## Core Idea
The talk is one moment in a relationship: engage the **backchannel** so the online audience feels heard, instrument the room and the network for feedback, and follow up in ways that help people actually implement your idea.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The backchannel** (Cliff Atkinson, *The Backchannel*) — the stream of chatter before, during, and after your talk.
  - **Constructive when it**: enriches your message as people take notes, comment, and suggest resources; provides a valuable archive to review afterward; connects people in the room into a community around the ideas; lets those who can't attend follow along and converse; increases your reach.
  - **Destructive when it**: distracts attendees so they attend to the channel rather than to you; steers conversation off topic; excludes people unaware of it or unable to join; limits nuance and context because posts are brief; injects a rude or snarky tone, since people tweet what they wouldn't say aloud.
  - Goal: avoid a **backchannel revolt**, where people rally each other to reject your message. The mechanism: make the folks online feel *heard*. They will talk about you with or without you, so participate.
  - How to build relationships there: **observe their behavior** (what else they comment on points you to LinkedIn groups, fan pages, and other hot spots where conversations start); **provide a channel** (create a hashtag, invite its use before/during/after, display it on an introductory slide — appropriate only for external talks with broad audiences, never confidential meetings or client sales calls); **ask for their input** (float a partially developed idea for refinement; ask your channel what's on the minds of people at an event or in an industry you don't know).

- **Six ways to spread ideas during the peak** (social activity spikes during the talk, with moderate chatter before and after): **stream your presentation** (the most direct extension — the full message travels, not just the chatter); **time-release messages and slides** (craft social-specific messages, auto-push them at key moments; 140-character phrases in the PowerPoint notes field can auto-tweet on slide advance); **select a moderator** (quick-witted, strong command of your material — tweets key phrases as you say them, raises questions, pulls the thread back on topic, and sends out links to your posted slides); **repeat audience sentiment** (the currency of social media is reciprocity: if you don't spread others' ideas, yours won't travel); **post photos of your talk** (a photographer working with your moderator posts during the talk for immediacy); **encourage blogging** (invite bloggers, journalists, and social media specialists to cover it).
  - Two findings from Dan Zarrella's research: **don't be too overt** — flagging sound bites with a little Twitter bird makes them get shared *less*, because people want to identify what's worth spreading themselves; **be novel** — close to 30% of respondents were more likely to tweet or blog about a presentation if it was novel or newsworthy.

- **Five ways to gauge connection**:
  1. **Watch the backchannel** — have a moderator text you criticisms worth addressing in Q&A (passing along tough-but-fair comments and filtering anything that would throw you). Or set your phone silent on the podium and glance at it, changing direction if the room revolts — and tell people you monitored their sentiment because you wanted to address their concerns.
  2. **Watch the live audience** — posture and facial expressions. Positive: leaning forward, nodding, smiling, taking notes. Negative: leaning back with arms crossed (resistance), tired, fidgeting, looking around, checking e-mail (boredom or apathy). Steve Jobs sustained anticipation across a 90-minute keynote partly by eliciting frequent physical reactions — in the 2007 iPhone launch the audience laughed 79 times and clapped 98 times, about one reaction every 30 seconds.
  3. **Survey your audience** — less immediate but more controlled and more thoughtful. Keep it short and direct, on paper, online, or by e-mail; explicitly ask for candor; project a closing slide inviting an immediate phone rating. Ask event organizers for their session results; at small talks, ask one or two people whose opinion you value for an honest read.
  4. **Analyze sentiment** — for high-stakes talks, examine how many blogged, how much traffic social drove to the press announcement, and whether coverage skewed positive or negative. Hire an analytics specialist; the analysis may surface a rival you didn't know about or a new key influencer driving buying behavior.
  5. **Analyze your reach** — how many spread the message, how many clicked shared links, and whether the people you *wanted* to hear it did.

- **Seven follow-up moves** — once you've won people over, help them implement: encourage them, bring new insights, remove roadblocks.
  - **Sending personal notes** — handwritten notes are rare and appreciated; send whenever you feel grateful (and "I'm sorry" notes work too). A human touch stands out in a digital world.
  - **E-mailing the audience** — thank them and, if appropriate, summarize your big idea, key points, call to action, and "new bliss." Organizers often share attendee addresses in lieu of a fee.
  - **Being accessible** — internally, host a lunch right after, or block your calendar for open-door questions; externally, follow up via blog and social posts and answer anyone who starts a thoughtful conversation.
  - **Sending materials** — deliver anything you promised immediately. Check before offering gifts; many people's contracts bar accepting them from vendors or influencers.
  - **Calling or meeting with individuals** — listen to each person's concerns after a demanding initiative; insights shape your next communication (e.g., if resource limits dominate, describe your plan to shore them up).
  - **Booking "next steps" meetings** — gather people to answer questions requiring research and to build a roadmap; facilitate collaboration concretely.
  - **Presenting again** — if you're selling, the purpose of the first presentation is usually to get a second one: face-to-face time with a decision maker.

## Key Concepts
- **Backchannel revolt** — coordinated online rejection of a speaker's message.
- **Reciprocity** — the currency of social media; spreading others' ideas is the price of yours spreading.
- **Moderator** — a designated person keeping the social thread constructive and on-topic.
- **Physical cues** — involuntary audience body language used as live feedback.
- **New bliss recap** — the summary you repeat in follow-up e-mail, mirroring the talk's ending.

## Mental Models
- Think of each interaction as one moment in a larger relationship with your audience — that's the mind-set persuasion actually requires.
- Use negative cues as a prompt to change course mid-talk, not as something to endure.
- Treat critical feedback as expensive information you already paid for.

## Worked Example
**Two revolts and a recovery.** At a higher-education conference in Milwaukee, attendees tweeted during the keynote: "Starting to see the OMG I AM TRAPPED looks on faces," "We need a drinking game for every time he says 'actually' and 'actionable,'" "We need a tshirt, 'I survived the keynote disaster of 09.'" Within hours someone had produced the shirt on CafePress and shared it with attendees. Contrast with a presenter who read his audience's body language, saw he was missing the mark, stopped, admitted he'd miscalculated in preparing, and asked whether he'd get a slot at the next conference if he promised to understand the group's needs better. He got a standing ovation and an invitation back.

**The feedback that redirected a career.** The first survey from a small annual conference said the speaker delivered a fire hose of valuable information but the audience felt no connection; the organizer said to add personal stories. Painful, true, and taken seriously — it launched a multi-year study of story principles and structure that became the basis for the storytelling method in this book.

## Key Takeaways
1. Participate in the conversation about you; the alternative is not silence, it's an unanswered one.
2. Give the online audience a hashtag and a reason to feel heard — but never for confidential or client-facing content.
3. Don't flag your own sound bites; overt prompting reduces sharing.
4. Read the room physically and be willing to change course mid-talk.
5. Combine real-time signals (backchannel, body language) with considered ones (surveys, analytics) — they yield different insights.
6. Follow-up isn't courtesy, it's implementation support: remove roadblocks, be reachable, book the next step.
7. Put audience needs first and be sincere, and people will generally want to help you succeed.
