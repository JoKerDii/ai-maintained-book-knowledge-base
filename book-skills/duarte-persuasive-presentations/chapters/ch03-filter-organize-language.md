# Chapter 3: Section 2B — Filtering, Organizing, Balancing Appeal, and Sharpening Language
*Nancy Duarte*

*(Groups five short chapters from Section 2 — Message: Choose Your Best Ideas; Organize Your Thoughts; Balance Analytical and Emotional Appeal; Lose the Jargon; Craft Sound Bites.)*

## Core Idea
The quality of a presentation depends as much on what you remove as on what you include: converge on the ideas that serve the big idea, arrange them as clear active slide titles, tune the analytical/emotional mix, strip the jargon, and plant repeatable sound bites.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Divergent vs. convergent thinking** (Tim Brown, IDEO: "In the divergent phase, new options emerge. In the convergent phase... it's time to eliminate options and make choices"): idea generation is divergent; sorting and filtering is convergent — and harder.
  - When to use: Immediately after brainstorming, before touching software.
  - How: Connect, analyze, sort, and filter every idea against the big idea. Cut anything that doesn't support it, however interesting.
  - Why it works: If you don't filter, the audience must — and they will resent the work.

- **The slide-title outline test**: enter each point as a clearly worded title in outline or slide-sorter mode, never straight into slide-creation mode. Then ask: *"If people read just the titles, will they get what I'm saying?"*
  - How: Make titles clear and active (include verbs), sequence them so they flow, and infuse personality so you sound like a person, not an automaton.
  - Why it works: Audiences scan titles like news headlines and decide from them whether to keep listening. Agonize over them the way copywriters agonize over ad copy — your "conversion rate" depends on it.

- **The "why" ladder for emotional appeal**: repeatedly ask "why do we need this?" until you reach the human beings whose lives are affected. That's your emotional hook; then use words carrying emotional weight.

- **The grandmother test**: if a specialized term is central to your message, translate it. Would your grandmother understand? Rework until it is *that* clear. (Carmine Gallo: speaking over people's heads can cost you a job or a promotion.)

- **Sound-bite rhetorical devices** (as used by Steve Jobs):
  - **Rhythmic repetition** — a repeated phrase at the beginning, middle, or end of sentences.
  - **Concrete comparison** — simile or metaphor.
  - **Slogan** — a concise, easily remembered statement.
  - How: Craft them deliberately, then deliver them *without* fanfare so they seem spontaneous and people want to repeat them.

## Key Concepts
- **Convergent thinking** — the elimination phase of design; choosing among generated options.
- **Two classes of emotion** — pain and pleasure; decide where in the talk you want people to feel happy, to cringe, to be inspired.
- **Emotional texture** — emotional content layered onto otherwise purely analytical material.
- **Jargon/lexicon problem** — the more a group innovates, the gnarlier its private vocabulary gets, and the less portable its message.
- **Sticky-note speaking notes** — filtered notes in a file folder are, by themselves, a presentable talk; slides are optional.

## Mental Models
- Use topic *and* audience together to set the analytical/emotional mix: layoffs and launches are inherently charged; science, engineering, and finance invite analysis — but no presentation should be devoid of emotion, however cerebral the room.
- Think of yourself as selling the big idea: the faster you grab attention, the higher the conversion.
- Cut mercilessly on the audience's behalf — a fascinating tangent is still a tangent.

## Worked Example
**Filtering an acquisition business case.** Brainstormed candidates: competencies gained; estimated ROI; lessons from the last acquisition; threat R&D might perceive; bringing in culture consultants; receivables are at net 45 days; need to retool the factory floor. All support the big idea except *receivables at net 45 days* — important, but a distraction here. Save it for another meeting.

**Titles rewritten.**

| Vague, passive | Clear, active |
|---|---|
| Market overview | We're neck-and-neck with an aggressive rival. |
| Productivity gains | Production time shrank from 21 days to 8. |

**The why ladder.** "Why do we need cloud storage?" → "To facilitate data sharing with remote colleagues." → "Why does that matter?" → "So those colleagues can coordinate disaster relief efforts and save lives." Now "save lives" is the phrase that carries the ask.

**Sound bites in the wild.** During the 2010 iPhone 4 antenna press conference, Jobs repeated variants of "we love our users" across slide after slide until it was the phrase the press carried away from a crisis communication. At MacWorld 2007 he called the Intel processor switch a "huge heart transplant" (concrete comparison) and said "reinvent the phone" repeatedly (slogan) — which landed in headlines the next day.

## Key Takeaways
1. Filtering, not generating, is the hard part — and the big idea is the only legitimate filter.
2. Write and sequence titles before you design anything; if the titles alone don't tell the story, the deck won't either.
3. Ask "why" until you hit human consequence — that's where emotional appeal lives.
4. Translate every central specialized term; unclear ideas don't get adopted, and obscurity limits your career.
5. Build one or two crafted, repeatable sound bites into every talk, and deliver them casually.
6. Purely factual delivery isn't neutral — it reads as cold when the topic touches people.
