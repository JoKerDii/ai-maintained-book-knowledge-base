# Chapter 6: Section 3C — Add Emotional Texture, Use Metaphors as Your Glue, Create Something They'll Always Remember
*Nancy Duarte*

*(Groups three chapters from Section 3 — Story.)*

## Core Idea
Visceral response, not analysis, pushes an audience off the status quo — so add stories with emotional texture, bind the talk together with a metaphor, and plant one **S.T.A.R. moment** (Something They'll Always Remember) that drives the big idea home.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **S.T.A.R. moment — Something They'll Always Remember**: a climactic moment placed to drive your big idea home; it's what the audience chats or tweets about afterward and how the message goes viral. Use it to make people uncomfortable with *what is* or to draw them toward *what could be*. Four types:
  1. **Shocking statistics** — don't glide over a startling number, amplify it.
  2. **Evocative visuals** — emotionally potent imagery, often paired in contrast.
  3. **Memorable dramatization** — bring the message physically to life.
  4. **Emotive anecdote** — a gripping personal story.
  - When to use: Once per talk, at the climax. More than one dilutes all of them.

- **The emotional-impact story template** (beginning / middle / end, adapted from Glenn Hughes): **When, who, where** → **Context** → **Conflict** → **Proposed resolution** → **Complication** → **Actual resolution**. Use it to convert a dry supporting point into a story.

- **Story placement by emotional target**: while describing *what is*, tell a story that makes people shudder, guffaw at the ridiculousness of their situation, or feel disappointment. While describing *what could be*, tell one that strikes awe or a little fear — something that inspires change.

- **Inventory of Personal Stories**: a reusable catalog built in one uninterrupted hour and added to over time. Prompts: important times in your life (childhood, adolescence, young adulthood, later years); relatives; authority figures; peers; subordinates; enemies; important places; things you cherish; things that have injured you. For each recalled event, jot down *how you felt*.

- **Metaphor as glue**: for each point, find a metaphor connecting minds to the concept — and consider weaving one throughout the whole talk. About 20% of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" was metaphorical; his bad-check image ("a check which has come back marked 'insufficient funds'") arrived three minutes into a sixteen-minute talk and drew the first roar from the crowd.
  - Failure mode: Over-relying on tired *visual* metaphors instead of powerful words. King's speech would not have been improved by slides of bad checks and piles of gold. Reject racecars and sporting events; mine your own life instead.

## Key Concepts
- **Emotional texture** — emotion layered onto a logical case built from data, case studies, and evidence; not a gushing plea.
- **Visceral response** — dilated eyes, racing heart, chills, laughter, leaning forward; largely involuntary, which is why it persuades.
- **Appropriate drama** — stories must be relevant and proportionate, or you read as manipulative or out of touch.
- **Vulnerability premium** — audiences feel more affection for presenters who reveal their own challenges than for those retelling others' stories.

## Mental Models
- Think of a story as the delivery vehicle for a data point, not a break from the data.
- Use a personal story only if its stakes match the room's stakes — a wedding-logistics anecdote fits a staff project update badly, but fits a case for remote-collaboration technology perfectly.
- Think of the S.T.A.R. moment as the thing you'd want quoted if only one sentence of your talk survived.

## Worked Example
**The template filled in — a story about organizational change.**

- *Beginning — when, who, where*: A few years ago the sales team tackled a cross-divisional problem with a steering committee.
- *Middle — context*: At the time, all sales groups were independent.
- *Conflict*: We were confusing customers with many different rules, processes, and formats.
- *Proposed resolution*: So we created a sales steering committee.
- *Complication*: Reaching agreement on anything was extremely hard.
- *End — actual resolution*: We met every two weeks to find common ground, standardized all our processes over the next year, learned from each other, and customers became much happier.

Point being made: every cross-divisional function could benefit from a steering committee.

**S.T.A.R. moments, one of each type.**
- *Shocking statistic*: Intel's Paul Otellini at CES 2010 — a 32-nanometer microprocessor is 5,000 times faster with transistors 100,000 times cheaper than the 4004; at that rate of innovation cars would go 470,000 mph, get 100,000 miles per gallon, and cost three cents.
- *Evocative visuals*: Conservation International contrasted glistening under-ocean images captioned "90% of our oxygen" with photos of rubbish washing ashore captioned "14 billion pounds of trash" — donors opened their wallets toward a $1.7M goal.
- *Memorable dramatization*: Bill Gates released a jar of (malaria-free) mosquitoes into a TED 2009 auditorium — "There is no reason only poor people should be infected" — and let the room squirm before reassuring it. Mirran Raphaely of Dr. Hauschka held up a glass of synthetic weed killer and a glass of horsetail extract, asked which the audience wanted on the crops they eat, and took a sip of the biodynamic one.
- *Emotive anecdote*: Symantec.cloud's Rowan Trollope told of hammering emergency pitons on a darkening ledge at 11,000 feet, deciding the first was "good enough," then re-tying a knot because something nagged at him — the first piton popped out and struck his helmet on the first weighted move. He brought the failed piton onstage as a prop and made "piton decisions" the metaphor for whether *good enough* is good enough at his company.

## Key Takeaways
1. Emotion is what moves people; analysis is what justifies the move afterward.
2. Match story emotion to structural position — discomfort on the what-is side, aspiration on the what-could-be side.
3. Personal, vulnerable stories outperform borrowed ones.
4. Build a personal story inventory once; harvest it for years.
5. Prefer verbal metaphor over literal visual metaphor, and reject the first (clichéd) image that occurs to you.
6. Plant exactly one S.T.A.R. moment and make it serve the big idea, not your own showmanship.
