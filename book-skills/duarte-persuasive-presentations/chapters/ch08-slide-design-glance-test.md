# Chapter 8: Section 5A — Think Like a Designer, the Glance Test, Slide Types, Storyboarding, and Visual Clichés
*Nancy Duarte*

## Core Idea
Audiences process one stream of information at a time, so every slide must pass the **glance test** — comprehensible in three seconds — which means storyboarding one idea per slide before you build anything and rejecting the first visual metaphor that comes to mind.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The glance test**: people should comprehend a slide in three seconds. Think of slides as billboards — the audience briefly takes its eyes off its main focus (you) to process them, then returns.
  - When to use: On every slide, as a pass/fail gate.
  - How, in four moves: (1) **Start with a clean surface** — turn off the master prompts, begin with a blank slide, and justify every element you add (does the logo on each slide help? does the blue swoosh add meaning?). (2) **Limit your text** — short, skimmable, type scaled as large as possible for the back row. (3) **Coordinate visual elements** — one typeface, two at most; a consistent palette of three complementary colors plus a couple of neutrals; photos that look like one photographer took them; illustrations in one style. (4) **Arrange elements with care** — align graphics and text blocks, size objects appropriately (larger reads as more important), remembering slides blow up many times larger than your laptop screen.
  - Why it works, arithmetically: the default "Click to Add Text" field holds about 80 words, and average reading speed is ~250 wpm. Forty text-heavy slides in a 40-minute talk means the audience misses roughly 13 minutes — a third of your talk — because they're reading instead of listening. Research also shows people learn better from multimedia stripped of extraneous words, graphics, animation, and sound; the extras *take away* meaning by overtaxing cognitive resources.

- **The eleven slide types** — every slide is one of these:
  1. **Walk-in slide** — up as people enter; sets first impression and tone.
  2. **Title slide** — title, and for external audiences your name, title, company; orients and focuses.
  3. **Navigation slide** — section titles or a highlighted agenda showing where you are.
  4. **Bullet slide** — clusters related ideas, revealed with a **build** (one bullet per click) so the audience can't run ahead and get bored. If the bullets needn't be associated, give each its own slide.
  5. **Big-word slide** — one word or short phrase in large type; can set up a visual surprise on the next slide.
  6. **Quote slide** — one quote at a time, under ~30 words, with quotation marks and a source line; optionally supplement with a recording or voice-over of the speaker.
  7. **Data slide** — used judiciously; emphasize the relevant part and render everything else gray.
  8. **Diagram slide** — translates abstract concepts into visible relationships and processes.
  9. **Conceptual image slide** — photo or illustration where showing beats telling.
  10. **Video slide** — a break from static slides; talking-head endorsements, animated infographics.
  11. **Walk-out slide** — a re-projected call to action, contact info, or branded slide with music.

- **Storyboarding, one idea per slide**: sketch before you build, as filmmakers do.
  - How: **Keep it simple** — draw small visual representations on 1.5″ × 2″ sticky notes; the constrained space forces simple, clear words and pictures as proof of concept. Doodles are fine as long as you understand them — and if you don't, the concept is probably too complex. **Limit yourself to one idea per slide** — slides are free; give each idea its own moment onstage.
  - Why it works: clunky or overcomplex concepts announce themselves immediately (you run out of room on the sticky), so you can eliminate and re-brainstorm them cheaply. Dan Roam: real problems are multidimensional, and there's no way to fully understand — thus to begin solving — them without at some point literally drawing them out.

- **Avoid visual clichés**: throw out the first visual concept that comes to mind — it occurs to everyone else too. Brainstorm several images per concept to reach fresh, surprising ones.

| Concept | Cliché | Unique |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Bull's-eye | Maze; threshold |
| Partnership | Handshake in front of globe | Reef ecosystem; Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers |
| Security | Lock and key | Doberman pinscher; pepper spray |

## Key Concepts
- **Build** — animating bullets to appear one at a time to control pacing.
- **Clean surface** — a blank slide with master prompts disabled as the starting point.
- **Cognitive resources** — the finite attention that decorative elements consume.
- **Ideation phase** — storyboarding, where rough sketches are the point.
- **Design thinking premium** — a portfolio of 63 design-driven British companies trounced the FTSE 100 over 13 years (Design Council study, cited in *Fast Company*, Oct 2007); design correlates with financial success, and presenters are in the design business.

## Mental Models
- Think of a slide as a billboard glimpsed from a moving car, not a document read at a desk.
- Use restraint as a design skill: presentation software offers many shiny, seductive elements, and there is beauty and clarity in refusing them.
- If your sketch won't fit on a sticky note, the idea is too complex for one slide — split it or rebuild it.
- Even with no images at all, nice big type on screen beats dense prose.

## Worked Example
**The big-word slide setting up a surprise.** Speaking at a high-tech company's annual sales meeting, the talk opened with a story about a first sales job — selling candy for the Camp Fire Girls as a kid — and a clipped newspaper photo of the troop holding a sales trophy. To keep the photo a surprise, a slide reading only **"Victory is sweet"** came first. The phrase both explained a real reason people sell and teed up the image of goofy fifth-grade smiles and skinny arms holding a trophy bigger than the girls were.

**Before/after on the glance test.** A text-packed "before" slide fails outright. Streamlining the text to a short phrase and adding one simple visual element lets the audience process the point in a glance and return their attention to the speaker — the same content, a third of the talk recovered.

## Key Takeaways
1. Audiences read *or* listen — never both. Every word on a slide is time taken from you.
2. Three seconds is the standard; if a slide needs more, it's a document.
3. Justify every element added to a blank slide, including your logo.
4. Storyboard on sticky notes before opening software; slides are free, so give each idea its own.
5. Never reveal a bullet list all at once — build it.
6. Reject your first image idea; it's everyone's first image idea.
7. Constrain type, color, and image style across the whole deck for unity.
