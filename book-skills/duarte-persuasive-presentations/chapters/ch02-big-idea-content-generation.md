## Core Idea
Every persuasive presentation is governed by one **big idea** — a complete sentence carrying your point of view *and* what's at stake — and everything else is content generated to support it, tension built to move it, and an explicit action to complete it.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **The Big Idea** (screenwriters call it the "controlling idea"): the one key message you must communicate, expressed in a **complete sentence** with a subject (often some version of "you") and a verb (to convey action and elicit emotion). Two required components:
  1. **Your point of view** — your perspective on the subject, not a topic label.
  2. **What's at stake** — why the audience should care and why the status quo is unacceptable.
  - When to use: First, before generating any content; and again as the primary filter when cutting.
  - How: Take your topic, add POV, then add stakes. Use words that push people away from pain and pull them toward pleasure.
  - Failure mode: A phrase like "Q4 financials" or "software updates" is a *topic*, not a big idea. If your deck can be replaced by e-mailing a spreadsheet, you don't have one.

- **The divergent/convergent brainstorm loop**: alone → group → alone again.
  - How: Change environment (new room, no e-mail or phone, music), use tactile tools (paper, whiteboards, sticky notes — one idea per note). Generate by (a) gathering existing content, (b) building on it — challenge it, view from a new angle, draw new connections, (c) creating new content. Solo **mind mapping**: write a keyword, riff, connect with lines until you have a messy web. Then a fast-clip group session with a facilitator capturing ideas so nobody self-censors. Then solo again to let latent ideas develop.
  - Why it works: Go for quantity, not quality — distinctive ideas usually show up after five, ten, twenty attempts.

- **The three types of resistance**: **Logical** (counter-arguments and contrary data — go read them), **Emotional** (bias, dogma, or moral code your idea violates — proceed carefully and acknowledge the value at risk), **Practical** (physically, geographically, or financially hard to do what you ask — acknowledge the sacrifice and shoulder part of it yourself).

- **Dynamic opposites / the tension of extremes**: build interest by juxtaposing contrasting elements and then resolving them. Canonical pairs: past-present/future, need/fulfillment, speed/endurance, ambition/humility, stagnation/growth, roadblocks/clear passage, sacrifice/reward, budget/quality.
  - How: Draft two columns of material and generate plenty on *both* sides. The familiar comforts; the new stimulates.

- **The four calls to action** (derived from classifying the calls to action in hundreds of speeches): the audience can be **doers**, **suppliers**, **influencers**, or **innovators**. Pick the role, then break it into discrete tasks with deadlines that feel manageable.

## Key Concepts
- **Controlling idea** — screenwriting's name for the big idea.
- **Mind mapping** — solo associative brainstorming producing a web of concepts and relationships.
- **Pressure-testing** — handing your big idea to someone else to attack, when you're too close to see obvious objections.
- **Pre-selling** — meeting tough critics in advance so their concerns don't become in-room roadblocks.
- **Stakes** — the consequence of not acting, stated explicitly.

## Mental Models
- Think of the big idea as the only filter that matters: everything you keep must support it.
- Use contrast whenever momentum flags — audiences are drawn to it because life is full of it (day/night, love/hate).
- Think of pre-empting an objection ("I was skeptical too until I looked at the data") as inviting an open mind in return.

## Worked Example
**Topic → Big Idea, twice.**

- Topic: "Software updates." → POV added: "Your department needs to update its workflow management software." (still no stakes) → Big idea: **"Your department will struggle to meet key production deadlines until we update the workflow management software."**
- Topic: "The Florida wetlands." → Big idea: **"We need to restrict commercial and residential development in Florida's wetlands, because we're destroying the fragile ecosystem there and killing off endangered species."**

Note the prodding verbs — *struggle*, *destroying*, *killing* — that make the status quo uncomfortable, paired with a lure (meeting deadlines; protecting endangered species).

**Contrast applied.** An airline maintenance division asking for analytics funding:

| Customer complaints (what is) | Customer satisfaction (what could be) |
|---|---|
| Low survey ratings from delays and missed connections caused by simple maintenance issues | What if we could schedule maintenance better by digging into our repair data? |
| We follow the manufacturer's recommended schedule and it isn't sufficient; planes sit at the gate for routine repairs | By tracking how often we actually perform each repair, we build a realistic schedule and prevent problems instead of fixing them |

**Call to action ("innovate").** An aging product needs reinvention, and ideas need not come from engineering: (1) identify enthusiastic brainstormers from all departments; (2) have engineers facilitate a cross-departmental session that week; (3) assign a note-taker; (4) filter ideas at the engineering summit the following week.

## Key Takeaways
1. If you can't state your message as one complete sentence with POV and stakes, you aren't ready to build content.
2. Don't open presentation software to brainstorm — it forces linear thinking. Use sticky notes, one idea each.
3. Every audience resists; classify the resistance as logical, emotional, or practical and prepare for each type separately.
4. Raise and address concerns *before* they harden into mental roadblocks.
5. Generate material on both sides of your contrast pair, or the tension collapses.
6. State explicitly what you want done, by when, and how it benefits the audience — presentations only move people who know what to do.
