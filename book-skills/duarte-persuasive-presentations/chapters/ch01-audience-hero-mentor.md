## Core Idea
The audience — not the presenter — is the hero of the presentation; you are the mentor who hands them a gift, a tool, and a way to get unstuck. Every content decision gets filtered through their desires, goals, and resistance.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Audience as hero / presenter as mentor**: You are not the star. The people listening decide whether your idea spreads or dies, so approach them with humility and use their goals as the filter for everything you present.
  - When to use: Before writing a single slide, and again whenever your draft starts sounding like a to-do list for the audience.
  - How: Adopt the Yoda posture and deliver three things: (1) **give the hero a special gift** — insight that improves their lives; (2) **teach the hero to use a "magical" tool** — a skill or mind-set that gets them to their objectives *and* yours; (3) **help the hero get "unstuck"** — a solution to a painful situation.
  - Why it works: People came to see what you can do for them, not what they must do for you. A mentor stance converts compliance ("I pay you, so do it") into willing propulsion of your idea.

- **The five segmentation lenses**: Segment any audience along Politics (power, influence, decision process), Demographics (age, education, ethnicity, gender, geography), Psychographics (personality, values, attitudes, interests, communities, lifestyle), Firmographics (headcount, revenue, industry, locations, HQ), Ethnographics (social and cultural needs).
  - When to use: Any mixed-role room; especially internal decision meetings.
  - How: Segment, then identify who most affects adoption of the idea, then act as a **curator of content for your most valuable and powerful stakeholders** — write as if to that one subgroup while keeping enough general content that you don't alienate the rest.
  - Failure mode: Treating the audience as a "homogenous, faceless clump" — you can't connect with a clump.

- **The seven audience questions**: What are they like? Why are they here? What keeps them up at night? How can you solve their problems? What do you want them to do? How might they resist? How can you best reach them?

- **The audience transformation arc**: Map where the audience starts and where you want them to end up — as a screenwriter maps a protagonist's arc.
  - How: Write a two-column "Move audience from → Move audience to." Ask what new beliefs they must adopt, how behavior must change, and what attitudes or emotions must shift *before* behavior can.
  - Why it works: The endpoint is the entire reason you're presenting; people won't get there on their own.

- **Resonance / resonant frequency**: Tap the audience's natural frequency and they move on their own — you're not pushing or pulling. Find it via **shared experiences**, **common goals**, and **qualifications** (why you're uniquely qualified to guide them).

## Key Concepts
- **Common ground tiers** — Lots (family, friends, congregation), Moderate (colleagues: mine the one or two shared interests), Minimal (broad mixed audiences: you must research and manufacture it).
- **Curator of content** — the role you take once you've picked the highest-leverage subgroup.
- **The 10% rule (executive summary slides)** — devote roughly 10% of deck length to front-loaded summary slides; the rest is appendix.
- **Executive appendix** — the body of your deck, pulled up on demand as executives drive the conversation.
- **Crash course** — a separate pre-brief for audience members who lack context, so you don't pace the main talk to the least-informed person.

## Mental Models
- Think of the audience as a line of individuals waiting to have a conversation with you, not a crowd.
- Use the "cut to 5 minutes" test when the audience is senior: build for your allotted 30, then rehearse as if you got 5 — it forces high-level findings, conclusions, recommendations, and call to action to the front.
- Think of knowing your audience as the cheapest way to sound conversational: people don't fall asleep during conversations, only during presentations.

## Anti-patterns
- **Self-focused structure** (what/how/when/budget, with "why it matters" as the last slide): screams "just do it" and forfeits buy-in.
- **The big reveal at the end with executives**: they interrupt, and you never land the point. Lead with the answer.
- **Going off the brief**: if invited to update them on the Indonesia plant flood, cover that first; they invited you for a specific missing piece.
- **Presenting to an audience you haven't researched**: bios, annual reports, press, competitor positioning, and event social feeds are all available before you walk in.

## Worked Example
Presenting a new product concept to an executive team, segmented in advance:

| Member | Qualities |
|---|---|
| Bert, CEO | Hierarchical, micromanager, dominant, fear-driven, needs to be liked |
| Carol, President Consumer | Visionary, creative, disruptive, scattered |
| Trent, President Enterprise | Entrepreneurial, design thinker, systematic |
| Martin, CMO | CEO's favorite, empirically minded, arrogant, sabotages projects |
| Marco, CTO | Political, risk-averse, analytical, introverted |

The team defers to Trent's instincts on new initiatives, so he is the target subgroup: lead with how exciting the new market is, appealing to his entrepreneurism. Keep hard data in your pocket for Marco's inevitable risk objection. Neutralize Martin in advance by asking his counsel on a marketing point *before* the meeting — pre-selling turns a saboteur into a co-owner.

Transformation arc for a university donor talk: **from** skepticism the school will use the money well **to** excitement about innovative faculty/student/alumni research and an impulse to give.

## Key Takeaways
1. Filter every element of the presentation through what the audience wants, not what you want to say.
2. Pick one high-influence subgroup and write mostly to them; general content is the remainder, not the core.
3. With senior executives: get to the point, stay on the topic you were invited for, set expectations up front, front-load summary slides, and rehearse with an honest coach.
4. Map the from→to transformation before you write anything; it is the most critical planning step.
5. Change requires sacrifice — show empathy for what people must give up to adopt your idea.
6. Research the room (bios, annual reports, press, social feeds) even when the audience feels unfamiliar or uninteresting to you.
