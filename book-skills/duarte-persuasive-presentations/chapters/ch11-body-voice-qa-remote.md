# Chapter 11: Section 6B — Body, Voice, Live Storytelling, Interpreters, Q&A, and Remote Audiences
*Nancy Duarte*

*(Groups seven chapters from Section 6 — Delivery: Communicate with Your Body; Communicate with Your Voice; Make Your Stories Come to Life; Work Effectively with Your Interpreter; Get the Most out of Your Q&A; Build Trust with a Remote Audience; Keep Remote Listeners Interested; Keep Your Remote Presentation Running Smoothly.)*

## Core Idea
Delivery is a trust mechanism: open body language and varied voice signal confidence, re-experienced stories and empathetic Q&A signal sincerity, and remote presenting requires deliberately manufacturing the human contact the technology strips out.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Five body techniques** (constricted, contrived gestures read as insecure; larger movement conveys confidence and openness): **project emotion with your face** (smile, laugh, open your mouth in disbelief; warm up by moving every facial muscle beforehand); **peel yourself away from your slides** (turning your back erects a barrier); **open up your posture** (no folded arms, crossed legs, hands in pockets, or hands clasped front or back — all signal discomfort); **exaggerate your movements** (fill the space; use conversational gestures made bigger and more deliberate; stretch arms wide and tall beforehand to open the chest cavity); **match gestures with content** (go big for a record sales year; bring everything in — a tiny gap between thumb and forefinger — for a narrowly missed target). Jill Bolte Taylor's 2008 TED talk is the model: arms thrown upward for the rush of euphoria as her brain's left side shut down, brought back down as she described surrendering her spirit.

- **The seven vocal registers** and their delivery: **Assertive** (firm, unyielding, significant, focused), **Cautious** (measured, enunciated, understated), **Critical** (harsh, angry, upset, pointed, caustic), **Humorous** (comedic, light, novel, irreverent), **Motivational** (uplifting, encouraging, friendly), **Sympathetic** (emotional, moving, personal, delicate), **Neutral** (casual, technical, dispassionate, informative) — achieved through **pitch, tone, volume, pacing, and enunciation**.
  - Failure mode: the default business dispassion, adopted in the belief it sounds objective or authoritative. Flat delivery bores. Create contrast through vocal variation, or by tag-teaming with a contrasting co-presenter.
  - **Verbal tics** ("um," "uh," "you know," "like," "anyway") fill silence that a **pause** would fill better, giving the audience a chance to reflect. Find yours on video; self-awareness alone lets you catch them mid-sentence.

- **Two techniques for making stories come to life** (Victoria Labalme): **Reexperience your stories** — Broadway actors relive material each performance to keep it fresh; narrate as if you're still in the moment, without melodrama, using evocative words enhanced by stance and gesture. **Use sensory details to set the scene** — invoke sight, sound, taste, smell, and touch. "I waited in a chilly, mildewed alcove the size of an elevator" says far more than "I waited in a small room," and grounding in specifics protects you from flowery greeting-card language.

- **Working with an interpreter** — three types: **Simultaneous** (soundproof booth, audience earphones, no disruption, most material covered, highest technical overhead), **Consecutive** (interpreter shares the stage; you pause after each point — for less formal settings or smaller budgets), **Whispering** (interpreter whispers translation *to you* when the audience comments; best if you mostly understand the language). Then, allowing up to a month: **test your chemistry** (someone who makes you laugh or calms you, never agitates); **call in reinforcements** (a professional interpreter plus a bilingual subject-matter expert to correct content in real time); **prepare half as much material** (an hour's slot means 30 minutes of content); **send your notes** a week ahead so she can practice; **work through idioms and metaphors** so she can flag untranslatables and suggest regional substitutes; **practice pacing** on arrival and let her coach your speed; **complete each thought** — each burst should be concise but complete, never leaving people hanging mid-phrase.

- **Six Q&A practices** (always allow time — trim the talk if necessary; unanswered burning questions mean unadopted ideas): **plan when you'll take questions** (hold to the end if you must build a case; take them after each point if you're making a series of points); **anticipate questions** from mundane to hostile and rehearse answers, while staying ready for curveballs; **listen empathetically for subtext** — answer directly but address the deeper question; **admit when you don't know** — never fake it, offer to research and follow up; **keep a tight rein on large or tough crowds** (a moderator who graciously reclaims the microphone after each question, or a stated one-question-per-person rule; acknowledge an angry questioner but look at *other* audience members while answering so you can move on; for charged topics or crises, a facilitator who mixes tough and lighter questions, omits off-topic or personal-agenda ones, and plants questions people are too intimidated to ask); **leave a strong final impression** — never end abruptly after Q&A; wrap with a brief summary recapping the new bliss.

- **Building trust remotely** (~80% of corporate presentations are delivered remotely; the technology that connects also isolates — a McLuhan-style trade-off): **With video streaming** — keep eyes on the camera, not notes or slides, or you look shifty; place the camera at eye level (looking down at it forces viewers to look up at you, a cinematographer's trick for showing superiority); stand rather than sit so you move naturally; make movements expansive for the room but keep gestures you want remote viewers to see close to your chest so they stay in frame; good recorder and good lighting make the audience feel valued. **Without video** — your voice is the instrument; still stand; vary volume, pitch, and tone without melodrama; use open-feeling slides with readable type and clean graphics, and let the audience download the deck.

- **Six ways to keep remote listeners interested** (a survey of ~400 recent webinar attendees found more of them checked e-mail than watched the webinar — the inbox is your biggest competitor): **break content into small bites** (move quickly, change slides about every 20 seconds); **make it interactive** (research-and-post-to-chat activities, surveys whose results interest them, rewards for attention); **enjoy your own material** (smile so your voice brightens; laugh at your own joke to invite laughter); **vary the voices** (co-host and banter like morning-show hosts); **pause strategically** (pauses cut through white noise, and sometimes make people fiddle with their computers, reengaging them); **picture your listeners** (imagine faces — or literally tape photos of colleagues above the monitor).

- **Remote smoothness checklist**: provide clear instructions (registration, login, technical requirements up front); plan for snafus (tech contact info, handouts e-mailed ahead, slides online as fallback); test your slides on the exact machine you'll present from (webinar software breaks animations, builds, and transitions; path-based animations often fail or turn choppy; contrast fades and photos pixelate); start on time (set up 30 minutes ahead); reduce **personal noise** (no bangle bracelets or earrings against a headset; no finger drumming, pen clicking, paper shuffling, or sipping near the mic); **environmental noise** (close the door, kill fans and music, quit alerting apps, mute when others speak, phone off, office phone on Do Not Disturb — never hold, or they hear your hold music); **visual noise** (hide extra windows and desktop icons; use the mouse as a pointer, don't zing it around); **communal noise** (one voice at a time; repeat in-room questions for remote listeners); and use a **facilitator** to wrangle technology, room setup, agenda and slide distribution, chat monitoring, surveys, and making sure all locations get heard.

## Key Concepts
- **Backchannel of subtext** — the larger issue or unspoken motive lurking behind a surface question.
- **Verbal tic** — filler word substituting for a pause.
- **Reexperiencing** — narrating a story from inside the moment rather than reporting it.
- **Planted question** — one a facilitator raises because the audience is too intimidated to.
- **Whispering interpretation** — interpreter translating audience input to the presenter, not the reverse.

## Mental Models
- Think of an angry questioner as a room-management problem, not a debate: acknowledge, answer to the rest of the room, move on.
- Use the pause where you'd use a filler word; silence makes speakers uncomfortable and audiences thoughtful.
- With an interpreter, think in complete short thoughts — pacing is a co-authored decision, not yours alone.
- Remote: assume you are competing with an inbox, and design accordingly.

## Worked Example
**The birthday-party question.** Hosting an orientation for employees from a recently acquired company, someone asks why they no longer get monthly employee birthday parties. The tempting move is to brush it off as trivial. But the parties are a symbol of a bigger problem, and the question *behind* the question is: "The culture we used to have isn't valued here — how can we hang on to traditions that made our organization feel like a family?" Answering the surface question loses the room; answering the subtext addresses the actual resistance.

**A tic found on video.** Reviewing rehearsal footage revealed an unnoticed habit of saying "Right?" with a lilt after every key point. Watching the video repeatedly cemented it; at the next speaking engagement it slipped out once — and sounded like fingernails on a chalkboard — then was caught and stopped twice more. Self-awareness, not willpower, is the correction mechanism.

**Reexperiencing at scale.** A CEO reenacts the moment his CFO walked into his office recommending they not invest in subprime mortgages. It's riveting partly for the hindsight stakes, but mostly because he brings people *into* the scene: the wood-paneled room, the view out the window on a clear day, the razor's-edge decision that saved the company hundreds of millions — closing by acknowledging his CFO's judgment. As Duarte notes: nobody says "I loved your third point on leadership," but people do say "I still think of the story you told."

## Key Takeaways
1. Open, exaggerated, content-matched gestures read as confidence; closed posture reads as discomfort.
2. Vary pitch, tone, volume, pacing, and enunciation deliberately — flat is not neutral, it's boring.
3. Replace filler words with pauses; find them by watching yourself on video.
4. Tell stories from inside the moment with sensory specifics.
5. With an interpreter, halve your material, send notes a week ahead, and speak in complete short thoughts.
6. Never fake a Q&A answer; always listen for the question behind the question; never end on a Q&A cliff.
7. Remotely, manufacture presence: eye-level camera, standing delivery, 20-second slide changes, strategic pauses, and a facilitator.
