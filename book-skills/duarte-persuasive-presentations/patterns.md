# Patterns & Techniques

## Audience-as-Hero Reframe
**When to use**: Any draft that reads as a list of things the audience must do for you.
**How**: Recast yourself as mentor. Deliver a gift (insight that improves their lives), a magical tool (a skill or mind-set serving their objectives and yours), and a way to get unstuck (a solution to a painful situation). Rewrite the opening so "why this matters to you" precedes the nitty-gritty.
**Trade-offs**: Takes longer to prepare and cedes the spotlight; in exchange you get willing advocates instead of grudging compliance.

## Audience Segmentation and Curation
**When to use**: Mixed-role rooms; internal decision meetings.
**How**: Profile along politics, demographics, psychographics, firmographics, ethnographics. Identify who most affects adoption. Write mostly to that subgroup, keeping enough general content to avoid alienating the rest. Prepare responses for known objectors and pre-sell the saboteurs.
**Trade-offs**: Over-specialize and you lose the room; under-specialize and you move no one.

## The Executive Compression
**When to use**: Senior executives, short slots, decision meetings.
**How**: Build for the allotted time, then rehearse as if cut to 5 minutes. Lead with findings, conclusions, recommendations, and call to action. Set expectations up front ("5 minutes of summary, then discussion"). Front-load executive summary slides at ~10% of deck length; the rest is appendix you pull from on demand. Rehearse with someone who succeeds at that level.
**Trade-offs**: You surrender narrative suspense — which executives don't want anyway.

## Big Idea Sentence Construction
**When to use**: Before generating any content.
**How**: Start from the topic. Add your point of view. Add what's at stake. Result must be one complete sentence with a subject (often "you") and a verb. Use pain words to make the status quo uncomfortable and reward words to lure.
**Trade-offs**: Committing to a POV invites disagreement — which is the point.

## Divergent–Convergent Brainstorm Loop
**When to use**: Content generation for any substantial talk.
**How**: Alone (mind map on paper), then group (fast facilitation, one idea per sticky note, no judgment), then alone again to develop latent ideas. Then converge: filter every idea against the big idea and cut mercilessly.
**Trade-offs**: Slow, and produces material you'll discard — quantity is the mechanism, not waste.

## Resistance Triage
**When to use**: Before finalizing content; again before Q&A prep.
**How**: Classify likely objections as logical (read the opposing literature), emotional (identify the bias, dogma, or moral code your idea violates and address the value at risk), or practical (name the sacrifice, share the burden visibly). Pre-empt in the talk; pre-sell the loudest critics privately.
**Trade-offs**: Raising objections yourself risks planting doubt — but it demonstrates an open mind and invites reciprocity.

## What Is / What Could Be Toggle (Sparkline)
**When to use**: As the skeleton of any presentation meant to change belief or behavior.
**How**: Establish what is until heads nod. Introduce what could be, opening a gap. Toggle repeatedly through the middle, drawing rows from your dynamic-opposite pairs. End in the new bliss. Never announce the transition.
**Trade-offs**: More work than a linear report; the only structure that reliably moves people.

## Three-Level Ending
**When to use**: The close of any persuasive talk.
**How**: Describe future rewards at three levels — benefits to them, to their sphere (peers, reports, customers), and to the world. Then, and only then, action items.
**Trade-offs**: Can read as grandiose if the third level is a stretch; skip it rather than inflate it.

## Emotional-Impact Story Template
**When to use**: Converting a dry supporting point into something that lands.
**How**: When/who/where → context → conflict → proposed resolution → complication → actual resolution. Place discomfort stories on the what-is side and awe/inspiration stories on the what-could-be side.
**Trade-offs**: Costs time in a tight slot; buys retention and retelling.

## S.T.A.R. Moment Design
**When to use**: Once per talk, at the climax.
**How**: Choose one of four — amplified shocking statistic, evocative visual contrast, memorable dramatization (props, live demonstration), or emotive anecdote. Tie it directly to the big idea.
**Trade-offs**: Two S.T.A.R. moments dilute each other; a S.T.A.R. moment serving showmanship rather than the idea reads as manipulation.

## Vehicle Selection Before Software
**When to use**: Before opening PowerPoint or Keynote.
**How**: Match medium to how the audience processes information and to the venue: memo, video, whiteboard conversation, props, handouts, demo, or deck. Use low-tech deliberately when you want to signal that thinking is still formative.
**Trade-offs**: A polished deck signals finality — sometimes desirable (investors), often not (collaborative decisions).

## The 60% Time Budget
**When to use**: Every talk with a defined slot.
**How**: Plan content for 60% of the slot. Cut deck by a third, moving slides to a slide cemetery. Rehearse with the clock counting *up* while trimming; switch to counting *down* once in range, with quarter-mark time stamps. Plant a false ending and a real ending.
**Trade-offs**: Feels like leaving value on the table; nobody has ever been faulted for ending early.

## Glance Test Pass
**When to use**: On every slide.
**How**: Can it be understood in three seconds? Start from a clean surface with master prompts off, justify each added element, limit and enlarge text, unify typeface and palette, align and size deliberately.
**Trade-offs**: Sparse slides carry less standalone information — which is why documents exist.

## Sticky-Note Storyboarding
**When to use**: After the title outline, before building anything.
**How**: Sketch each idea on a 1.5″ × 2″ note. One idea per slide. Concepts that overflow the note are too complex — eliminate and re-brainstorm.
**Trade-offs**: Feels like a detour; it saves more time than it costs and surfaces reusable diagram ideas.

## Bullets-to-Diagram Conversion
**When to use**: Any slide with a related bullet list.
**How**: Circle the verbs or nouns and identify the relationship. Match it to network, segment, join, flow, or stack. Replace the list with a diagram from that category.
**Trade-offs**: Diagrams take longer to make and can over-formalize a loose grouping.

## Data Clarification Pass
**When to use**: Every data slide.
**How**: Decide the one thing to be remembered and give it visual emphasis; gray out the rest; mute gridlines, borders, axes, labels. Kill 3-D where there's no z-axis; keep axis proportions honest. Choose the chart for the argument (matrix for relationships, line for trajectory). Consider showing one huge number instead. Explain why and how, not just what. Anchor big magnitudes in concrete comparisons.
**Trade-offs**: Deemphasized context must be supplied by you, so it doesn't survive redistribution — send a document for that.

## Rehearsal Stack
**When to use**: Every talk that matters.
**How**: Honest feedback from a skilled presenter on a printed deck; a rehearsed short version; continued slide tweaking as familiarity-building; runs in slide-show mode; and on-camera recording reviewed for presence, gestures, and tics.
**Trade-offs**: Time-expensive. Memorizing instead is cheaper and worse — it produces stiffness.

## Technology Walkthrough Checklist
**When to use**: 30+ minutes before every in-person talk.
**How**: Befriend the AV person; test projector, clicker, audio; bring your own backups plus cloud, drive, and paper copies; prerecord demos; click through every slide in-room; verify comfort monitor legibility from the stage; confirm all media files transferred and play; confirm aspect ratio and projection direction, taping the floor; check for remote attendees.
**Trade-offs**: Pure overhead when nothing breaks; the one time it does, it's the whole talk.

## Q&A Control
**When to use**: Every business presentation — trim content to make room.
**How**: Decide up front when questions come. Prepare answers to mundane through hostile questions. Answer the subtext, not just the surface. Admit ignorance and commit to follow up. Use a moderator or a one-question-per-person rule; acknowledge hostile questioners while answering to the rest of the room; use a filtering facilitator for charged topics. Close with a summary that recaps the new bliss.
**Trade-offs**: Taking questions throughout breaks your build; saving them all risks a pile-up.

## Remote Presence Manufacturing
**When to use**: Any webinar, teleconference, or streamed talk.
**How**: Camera at eye level, stand, eyes on the lens, gestures kept in frame. Break content into small bites and change slides ~every 20 seconds. Build in interactivity and rewards. Vary voices with a co-host. Pause strategically to cut through white noise. Picture (or photograph) real faces. Test slides on the presenting machine — webinar software breaks builds and animations. Suppress personal, environmental, visual, and communal noise. Use a facilitator.
**Trade-offs**: High preparation cost for an audience whose attention you only partly control.

## Backchannel Engagement
**When to use**: External talks with broad audiences (never confidential or client meetings).
**How**: Create and display a hashtag; invite use before, during, and after. Assign a quick-witted moderator to tweet key phrases, raise questions, redirect drift, and post slide links. Repeat and validate audience sentiment. Stream the talk. Post photos live. Invite bloggers. Don't flag your own sound bites, and be novel.
**Trade-offs**: The channel that spreads your idea can also organize against it; participation is the mitigation.

## Structured Follow-Up
**When to use**: Immediately after every talk that asked for something.
**How**: Handwritten notes; thank-you e-mail recapping big idea, key points, call to action, and new bliss; open-door or lunch accessibility; promised materials sent immediately; one-on-one calls with people carrying the burden; a next-steps working meeting; and further presentations to other groups.
**Trade-offs**: Sustained effort long after the adrenaline stops — which is exactly why most presenters skip it and lose the adoption.
