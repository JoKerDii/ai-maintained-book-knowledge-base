# Chapter 1: Putting On the Hats — How the Method Works

## Core Idea
A hat is a **direction to think in**, put on or taken off at will. The method works by deliberate role-play (the hat gives permission), disciplined switching announced by one facilitator, and two modes of use: single hats called on demand, or a pre-set **sequence** of hats run through in order.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Single use**: A hat is invoked as a symbol to request one type of thinking in the middle of an ordinary discussion.
  - When to use: Anytime a conversation needs a specific mode — fresh options, caution, feelings, facts — without a full structured session.
  - How: Name the colour ("I think we need some green hat thinking here"; "Maybe we should have some black hat on this"). Everyone shifts to that direction; you need not specify a hat every time you speak.
- **Sequence use**: Two or more hats run one after another to explore a subject or solve a problem.
  - When to use: Group meetings, individual problem-solving, or reports where every aspect should be covered systematically.
  - How: Use a **pre-set sequence** (laid out in advance under an initial blue hat) rather than an **evolving sequence** (next hat chosen as you go) until you are experienced — evolving sequences waste time arguing over hats and look like manipulation. Any hat may repeat; no need to use every hat; two, three, four or more hats is fine.
- **Blue-hat bookends**: A blue hat always opens and closes a session — like two bookends — to define the focus and to harvest the outcome.

## Key Concepts
- **Deliberate role-play**: You put on a hat and then follow its requirements; the positive/creative stance precedes seeing merit, it does not wait for motivation.
- **Signalling**: A hat can request thinking of others, indicate a desirable mode, signal your own mode to others, or (crucially for green) signal to yourself to set aside time for that thinking.
- **Discipline**: Members must stay with the current hat; only the leader/chairperson/facilitator changes hats. You may not say "I want to put on my black hat here" — that is a return to argument.
- **Timing**: Set a short time (roughly one minute per person present) and extend it while genuine points still come, rather than set a long time and let people waffle.
- **Colour never function**: Always refer to hats by colour, never by function — "red hat" is neutral where "give me your emotional reaction" invites a dishonest or defensive answer.
- **Individual-in-group thinking**: The chair can pause a hat for two minutes of solo thinking (useful for green, yellow, black) or go round the table person by person.

## Mental Models
- **Use single-hat requests when** a live discussion drifts into one register too long ("Yes, that's fine black hat thinking. Now let's try the yellow hat.").
- **Use a pre-set sequence when** you want the whole subject mapped without mid-meeting arguments over what to do next — set it at the start under blue.
- **Set a short clock**: it is much better to under-allot time and extend than to over-allot and have people sitting around. The red hat is fastest — often one minute total, no explanations.
- **Let one voice change the hat**: distributed hat-switching becomes argument; centralised switching keeps parallel thinking.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assigning one person a fixed hat for the whole meeting**: The opposite of the method — everyone must wear every hat at its appointed time so all the group's intelligence is used in each direction.
- **Evolving the sequence before you are skilled**: The group spends the session arguing which hat comes next, and the sequence-setter looks like they are rigging the outcome.
- **Naming the function instead of the colour**: "Stop being negative" is feeble and personal; "take off your black hat for a moment" is a neutral move in a game.

## Worked Example
Ron Barbara, head of Prudential Insurance, would float an idea; his executives would pile on reasons it was risky, illegal, or unpopular. Instead of getting defensive, he listened and said: "Yes. That's fine black hat thinking. Now let's try the yellow hat." One neutral phrase switched the whole room from criticism into a deliberate search for benefit — no ego bruised, no argument, just a change of direction.

## Key Takeaways
1. A hat is put on or off on request; you can ask others to switch, or switch yourself.
2. Use single hats for live discussions and pre-set sequences for structured sessions; stick to pre-set until experienced.
3. Only the facilitator changes the hat, and the blue hat bookends every session.
4. Timebox short — about one minute per person — and extend only while real points flow; keep the red hat briskest.
5. Refer to hats by colour, never by function, so requests carry no personal charge.
