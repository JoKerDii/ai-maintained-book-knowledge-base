# Chapter 2: The White Hat — Facts and Information

## Core Idea
White is neutral and objective. Under the white hat the thinker plays a computer: give the facts and figures on demand, without interpretation, opinion, or argument. The focus is equally on what we **have** and what we **need**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Play the computer**: Present information "just the facts, please" — neutrally, on demand, never bent to support a conclusion.
  - When to use: Usually near the start of a session to establish the informational base map before judging or creating.
  - How: Answer the five white-hat questions — What information do we have? What do we need? What is missing? What questions must we ask? How will we get it?
- **Focusing questions**: The requester narrows a broad information request to draw out exactly what is needed and avoid being "drowned" in data.
  - When to use: When a general request ("give me the facts on X") would flood you with everything or invite a selective, argument-loaded reply.
  - How: Move from broad to specific ("Give me your broad white hat thinking on unemployment… now give me the figures for school-leavers six months out").
- **Two-tier facts + likelihood frame**: Separate checked, proven "first-class" facts from believed-but-unchecked "second-class" facts, and frame every claim by its likelihood.
  - When to use: Whenever information is uncertain but still usable.
  - How: Label with the appropriate frame — "always true," "by and large," "sometimes," "occasionally," "believed" — so nothing is stated more firmly than it is known.

## Key Concepts
- **Neutrality**: White (absence of colour) signals no slant; the white hat reports on the world, it does not push a case.
- **Facts embedded in argument**: The Western habit of giving a conclusion first and marshalling facts to defend it — the very thing the white hat exists to switch off.
- **First-class vs. second-class facts**: Checked/proven facts vs. facts believed true but not yet verified.
- **Spectrum of likelihood**: Truth runs from "always" to "never" with usable levels between; state which one applies.
- **Information overload as evasion**: Answering a request with seven million documents (as IBM did) is a way of hiding, not informing — hence focusing questions.
- **Separating information from judgement**: The white hat's formality forces a person to stick to information and hold back opinion.

## Mental Models
- **Use the White Hat when** you need the map before the route: lay the facts out like specimens on a table before anyone assesses or invents.
- **Ask "what do we know vs. what do we need"**: the white hat is as much about identifying gaps and framing questions as about reciting data.
- **Frame the confidence**: never let a "believed" fact pass as a "checked" fact — attach its likelihood so decisions rest on honest ground.

## Anti-patterns
- **Facts as ammunition**: Using figures only to support a position — facts can never be treated objectively when put forward as part of an argument.
- **Drowning the asker**: Dumping all information so no one can use it; the fix is focused requests, not more data.
- **Stating belief as certainty**: Presenting an unverified or partial claim without its likelihood frame.

## Worked Example
A courtroom witness testifies: "He returned at six-thirty in the morning because he had spent the whole night gambling." The white-hat challenge separates fact from inference: "Did you actually see the defendant gambling that night, or did he tell you he had been?" — "No, Your Honour. But he goes gambling almost every night." The white hat strips the conclusion out and exposes what is actually known versus assumed. De Bono notes lawyers cross-examining do exactly this: draw out facts with focusing questions and keep the witness in white-hat mode.

## Key Takeaways
1. Imitate a computer: neutral, objective, no interpretation, facts on demand.
2. Give equal weight to what you have and what you still need — gaps and questions are white-hat output too.
3. Use focusing questions to get the right facts and avoid information dumps.
4. Separate first-class (checked) from second-class (believed) facts, and frame likelihood explicitly.
5. Never let facts arrive pre-packaged inside an argument.
