# Chapter 0: Introduction — Parallel Thinking & the Six Hats

## Core Idea
Ordinary thinking fails because we try to do everything at once — emotion, information, logic, hope, and creativity all crowd in like juggling too many balls. The Six Hats method lets a thinker (or a group) do **one thing at a time**, replacing adversarial argument with **parallel thinking**, in which everyone looks in the same direction at the same moment.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Parallel thinking**: The core alternative to argument. Instead of each party defending an opposed position, at any moment *everyone* thinks in the same direction (wears the same hat). Contradictory views are laid down side by side "in parallel" rather than fought out; a choice between them is made only later, if it must be.
  - When to use: Any group discussion, decision, or exploration that would otherwise become adversarial or ego-driven.
  - How: Pick a direction (a hat); everyone thinks that way together; then switch direction. Like four people walking around a house to each see all four sides, rather than arguing about their fixed view.
- **The six hats / six directions**: White (information), Red (feelings), Black (caution), Yellow (benefits), Green (creativity), Blue (process control). The hat is a **direction to think in**, not a description of what was said.
  - When to use: To request or signal a specific mode of thinking without personal friction.
  - How: "Let's have some green hat thinking here" = a deliberate call to look for new ideas now.
- **Map-making vs. judging your way forward**: Explore and lay out the whole terrain ("what can be") *before* choosing a route, rather than picking a conclusion first and using facts to defend it.

## Key Concepts
- **Directions not descriptions**: A hat asks what is *about to* happen ("look east"), not what already happened ("you looked east"). Never use hats to label people ("she's a black-hat person").
- **Argument (adversarial thinking)**: The Western "Gang of Three" (Socrates/Plato/Aristotle) inheritance — dialectic, boxes, proving the other side wrong. Fine but insufficient; often both sides are right about different aspects.
- **Sensitization**: The brain chemically tunes itself in one direction at a time (seeking danger vs. seeking benefit). You cannot be optimally sensitized in two directions simultaneously — the physiological basis for separating modes.
- **Removal of ego**: In argument the ego is tied to being right, so people withhold points that would help the "other side." Under the hats you show off by performing well as a thinker in every direction.
- **The game**: The hats are rules of a game; refusing to switch hats reads as refusing to play. Behavior change via Confucian focus on behavior, not Freudian analysis of personality.

## Mental Models
- **Use parallel thinking when** a discussion is repetitive, adversarial, or ego-clogged — align everyone like the particles in a magnet so their combined ability focuses like a lens.
- **Use "one thing at a time" when** a problem feels like confusion or overload — separate the strands (facts, feelings, caution, benefit, ideas) and address each in its own slot.
- **Treat the hats as behavior, not diagnosis**: don't change the aggressive person; direct their aggression into the current hat.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assigning fixed hat roles**: Making one person "the black hat thinker" for the whole meeting is the *opposite* of the method — everyone wears each hat at its appointed time.
- **Using hats to describe or box people**: "He's green hat" destroys the point, that everyone can look in every direction.
- **Giving the conclusion first, then the facts**: The argument habit; make the map first, then choose the route.

## Worked Example
De Bono's Australian Constitutional Convention story: a man paints half his car white and half black so that, whenever he has an accident, the courtroom witnesses contradict each other. The chairman adopted the tale because in argument *both sides are usually right — they are simply looking at different aspects of the same situation*. Parallel thinking has all parties walk around and view each side together, so the subject is fully explored without the contradiction being a fight.

## Key Takeaways
1. Do one thing at a time; separate emotion, information, logic, caution, benefit, and creativity into distinct slots.
2. Replace "who is right?" argument with parallel thinking: everyone looks the same direction, then changes direction together.
3. A hat is a direction to think in, never a label for a person.
4. The method's power, speed, and ego-removal come from alignment — all the group's intelligence pointed one way.
5. Refer to hats by colour, never by function, so requests carry no personal charge.
