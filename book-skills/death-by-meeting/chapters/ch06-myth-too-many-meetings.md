# Chapter 6: The Myth of Too Many Meetings & Sneaker Time

## Core Idea
The instinctive objection to the four-meeting model — "I already have too many
meetings; where's the time?" — rests on a myth. Good meetings are *time savers*. The
hidden cost they eliminate is **sneaker time**: the uncounted hours executives spend
after bad meetings running around to clarify what should have been settled in the room.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Myth of Too Many Meetings**: the belief that the solution to bad meetings is
  *fewer* meetings.
  - When to use: countering resistance to adopting more structured meetings.
  - How to refute: the problem isn't quantity, it's quality. Properly run meetings
    accelerate decisions, prevent re-litigating issues, and slash redundant
    communication downstream. Measure the *total* time spent on alignment, not just
    the hours labeled "meeting."
- **Sneaker Time**: the time burned sending email, leaving voicemail, and "roaming the
  halls" to clarify issues that should have been made clear in a meeting.
  - When to use: making the business case for investing in better meetings.
  - How to see it: it's invisible because it's smeared across the whole day and never
    tallied as a category — "the most subtle, dangerous, and underestimated black hole
    in corporate America."

## Key Concepts
- **The geometry of alignment**: a 7-person executive team has **21 one-to-one
  relationships** to keep aligned — already near-impossible to track manually. Add the
  dozens of direct reports who must also stay in sync and the communication burden
  explodes. Failing to get clarity in the meeting "sets in motion a colossal wave of
  human activity" as everyone scrambles to figure out what others are doing.
- **"Real work" fallacy**: executives lobbying to end a meeting so they can "do real
  work" are often just going back to clean up confusion the meeting failed to resolve.

## Mental Models
- **Pay for clarity once, in the room — or pay repeatedly, in the hallways.** There's a
  direct correlation between skipping clarity/closure/buy-in during a meeting and the
  cleanup time required afterward.
- **Count the iceberg, not just the tip**: meeting hours are visible; sneaker time is
  the submerged mass.

## Anti-patterns
- **Optimizing for fewer meeting-hours** while ignoring the sneaker-time those cuts
  create.
- **Ending meetings without clarity, closure, and buy-in** — guaranteeing downstream
  rework.

## Worked Example
The recurring scene Lencioni describes: executives check their watches at the end of a
meeting and lobby the CEO to wrap up so they can "go do some real work." But the "real
work" is frequently returning to their offices to answer email and voicemail generated
*only because* so many people are confused about what to do — confusion the meeting
itself should have dispelled. It's as if they're saying, "Can we wrap this up so I can
run around and explain what I never explained after the *last* meeting?" Shocking, yet
understandable: intelligent people routinely fail to connect the skipped in-room
clarity with the cleanup it forces.

## A Final Thought
Bad meetings aren't merely inefficient — they generate real human suffering: anger,
lethargy, and cynicism that spill into self-esteem, families, and outlook on life.
Improving meetings is therefore both a performance lever and a way to positively impact
people's lives — including the leader's own.

## Key Takeaways
1. The fix for bad meetings is *better* meetings, not fewer.
2. Good meetings are net time-savers because they eliminate sneaker time.
3. Sneaker time is invisible because no one counts it — name it to manage it.
4. Clarity, closure, and buy-in in the room prevent expensive downstream rework.
5. Meeting quality is a human and cultural issue, not just an efficiency one.
