# Chapter 4: Problem #2 — Lack of Contextual Structure ("Meeting Stew")

## Core Idea
Meetings are ineffective because organizations throw every kind of issue — tactical,
strategic, administrative, cultural — into a single undifferentiated "staff meeting."
Without clear context, participants can't tell whether they're meant to debate, vote,
brainstorm, or just listen, so little gets decided. The fix is **multiple, distinct
meeting types**, each with its own purpose, format, and timing.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Meeting Stew**: the single biggest structural problem — tossing every type of
  issue into one meeting "like a bad stew with too many random ingredients."
  - When to use: diagnosing the classic 2–3 hour weekly staff meeting that satisfies
    no one.
  - How (to fix): stop mixing magnitudes; separate issues by type into purpose-built
    meetings (see Ch 5's four meetings).
  - Why it fails: different attendees want incompatible things from the same hour —
    some want quick tactical data exchange, some want deep strategic analysis, some
    want to talk culture/people, some just want decisions. Everyone is "right," which
    is exactly why one meeting can't serve them all.
- **Contextual Structure**: clearly distinguishing the *purpose, format, and timing*
  of each meeting so participants know what mode they're in.
  - When to use: designing a team's meeting rhythm from scratch or fixing stew.
  - How: define separate meetings and protect their boundaries (e.g., strategic topics
    raised in a tactical meeting get parked, not discussed).

## Key Concepts
- **Magnitude mismatch**: executives can't cleanly shift between decisions of wildly
  different weight (business-class travel policy vs. a merger) in one sitting.
- **"What are we doing right now?"**: the question attendees silently struggle with in
  a stew — debating? voting? brainstorming? weighing in? just listening?
- **Parking lot**: a place to capture an out-of-context issue so it gets the right
  meeting later (the Monthly Strategic serves as the parking lot for strategic issues
  raised during Weekly Tacticals).

## Mental Models
- **Don't decide dinner and your child's discipline problem in the same breath**:
  Lencioni's analogy for why tactical and strategic topics must be separated.
- **Different programs for different audiences/expectations**: the TV-genre framing
  from the fable — context sets expectations (see Ch 1).

## Anti-patterns
- **The one big staff meeting**: weekly/biweekly, 2–4 hours, "hash everything out so
  we can get back to real work." Guarantees an ineffective, unsatisfying meeting.
- **Reconsidering settled strategy when a tactical obstacle appears**: a hidden danger
  of mixing magnitudes — leaders back off long-term decisions under short-term pressure.

## Worked Example
**The 9–11 a.m. Monday stew.** Leader emails a 5–6 item agenda; no one responds.
Item one (not the most important) eats the first hour because people know they're
stuck there anyway, so they find things to say. Item two soaks up 45 minutes, leaving
15 minutes for the final three topics plus whatever new issues surface. Meeting
adjourns at 11:20. Everyone leaves frustrated *for different reasons*: one is late for
her next meeting; one's issue came up too late for any energy; one wanted strategy and
got admin; another wanted tactics and got too much brainstorming; another is mad the
picnic date *still* wasn't set. The leader walks away "bloodied," vowing the next
meeting will be more strategic, more tactical, shorter — an impossible combination in
one meeting. That impossibility *is* the point.

## Key Takeaways
1. Ineffective meetings are a *structure* problem — too many issue-types in one pot.
2. One meeting cannot serve incompatible purposes; everyone wanting something
   different is the diagnosis, not a personality clash.
3. Separate meetings by purpose, format, and timing — and protect the boundaries.
4. Park out-of-context issues for the right meeting instead of derailing the current one.
