## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The central thesis**: Executive presence (EP) is the *missing link between merit and success*. EP is a measure of **image, not performance** — whether you *telegraph* that you're "the real deal." Competence qualifies you; EP gets you seen as ready to lead. It's learnable and doable — you don't need straight A's, just ~3 strong picks per pillar plus avoiding serious blunders.

### The Three Pillars of EP (the master framework)
Everything in the book hangs on these three, which are **unequal and interactive**:
- **Gravitas — how you act.** The *core* pillar (67% of senior execs). Without it, no title, dress, or eloquence makes you a leader.
- **Communication — how you speak** (28%). It's *how* you say it, not what; content persuades only ~15%.
- **Appearance — how you look** (5%). Least important long-term, but the **first filter** — colleagues judge competence/likability/trust in **250 milliseconds**. Fail it and no one assesses the rest.

### The Six Gravitas Behaviors (Ch 2)
Prioritize ~3 to your strengths: **Confidence/grace under fire** (#1, 79%) · **Decisiveness / showing teeth** (70%) · **Integrity / speaking truth to power** (60%+) · **Emotional intelligence** (~60%) · **Reputation / personal brand** (56%) · **Vision** (50–54%).
- Key rule: *"Handling catastrophe confers gravitas; avoiding it only demonstrates competence."* Own mistakes publicly.
- Career-enders: **lack of integrity** and **sexual impropriety**.

### The Six Communication Traits (Ch 3)
Superior speaking · command a room · assertiveness · read the room · humor/banter · body language.
- **Medium beats message**: passion 27% + voice 23% + presence 15% > content 15%.
- Lower your pitch (~125 Hz optimal), slow down, use **silence** ("a musician's impact lies in the rests"), tell **stories not bullet points**, **lose the props** to make eye contact, be succinct.

### The Five Appearance Aspects (Ch 4)
Polish & grooming (top, learnable) · fitness/wellness (not thinness) · stylish clothes for your next job · height · youth/vigor.
- **Golden rule**: *minimize distractions from your skill sets and performance.*
- *Dress for the job you want.* Look "appropriate for your environment, authentic to you." Earn latitude, then set your own code.

### The Great Feedback Formula (Ch 5)
Great EP feedback is **Timely + Specific + Prescriptive + Business-framed**. Feedback is scarce precisely for those who need it most (women, minorities, LGBT) — so *ask for it explicitly*. Bad feedback = a "narrow band of acceptability" or vagueness ("be more vulnerable").

### The Tightrope & Goldilocks Syndrome (Ch 6)
Women/minorities face a **narrow band of acceptability** — "never just right," the signature of hidden bias. The deepest bind is **likability vs. competence**. Recognize contradictory feedback as *structural bias*, not personal failing. Widen latitude: argue for the team (take out the "I"), use humor, snipe don't spray, build a bold brand, wield credentials, show you care.

### Authenticity vs. Conformity (Ch 7)
Every org demands conformity, but bleaching out your identity (the **"bleached-out professional"**) costs you twice — alienation *and* invisibility. Distinguish "playing the game" from "selling out." Your difference is now a competitive asset (the **diversity dividend**) and what wins you a **sponsor**. *Don't downplay your difference — own it.*

## Scope & Limits

This skill covers the book's content: the framework of executive presence and its three pillars, with particular attention to how bias shapes the EP demands on women, people of color, and LGBT professionals. It is drawn from CTI (Center for Talent Innovation) survey research of ~4,000 professionals circa 2012–2013; statistics and cultural references reflect that period. For applying EP to your specific situation, combine with real feedback from a trusted sponsor. For adjacent topics (sponsorship mechanics, negotiation), check related skills or ask directly.
