# Chapter 5: Feedback Failures

## Core Idea
Unvarnished, concrete feedback on your EP is scarce — hardest of all if you're a woman, a person of color, or LGBT. Yet giving *actionable* EP feedback is a core leadership competency, and getting it is often the turning point in a career. The chapter supplies a formula for great feedback and tactics for both getting and giving it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Great Feedback Formula** — great EP feedback is:
  1. **Timely** — delivered right before or right after the blunder.
  2. **Specific** — about one discrete behavior, not a global condemnation.
  3. **Prescriptive** — explicit about what to do to course-correct.
  4. **Framed in business outcome** — tied to your success or the team's.
  - After good feedback you know: *what* the problem is, *why* it must be addressed, and *precisely what to do*.

- **Why feedback fails**: Senior men "can't afford to have their motives misconstrued" (fear of harassment perceptions → the "third rail"), so they withhold from women. Fear of discrimination litigation makes execs withhold from people of color — or pass them over rather than have the honest conversation. Result: the "marzipan layer" (just below top management) stays thick with underdeveloped talent.

- **Bad feedback** = a *narrow band of acceptability* (too angry / too nice; too young / too old) or vagueness ("be more vulnerable" — unactionable). Most respondents say they couldn't act on the feedback they got.

## Key Concepts
- **The two-pronged fix**: (1) rising stars must get better at eliciting, receiving, and acting on criticism; (2) leaders must get better at giving it while modeling how to receive it.
- **Marzipan layer**: The rich, sticky talent layer just below top management where qualified women/minorities stall for lack of development feedback.
- **The "ring of truth"**: Not all feedback is accurate or kind, but tune your radar for the ping that helps you course-correct. "There's no such thing as a bad review."
- **Sponsor vs. mentor**: Sponsors are powerful leaders vested in your outcome; they'll give the blunt EP feedback mentors won't.

## Mental Models
- **Feedback is a two-way trust deal**: Gardiner "struck a deal" with a promising attorney — asking permission to give courtroom-manner feedback, and securing agreement it would be heard as a tip, not a threat. "Feedback works only if there's mutual trust and respect."
- **"It's the culture crying!"**: Vague criticism is usually cultural misalignment, not a personal defect. Ask, "How is what I'm doing getting in the way of my job?" (Carolyn Buck Luce)
- **Catch people getting it right**: Especially on appearance, praise the good choice rather than criticize the bad one (Christina raved when a chronically-inappropriate dresser showed up polished — "overnight she bought new clothes to continue eliciting praise").

## Anti-patterns
- **"You're doing great!"** — the cop-out that stalls careers; one consultant *quit a lucrative bank job* because she couldn't get real feedback.
- **Bursting into tears / getting angry**: "Nothing cuts off the spigot of vital feedback more effectively than a loss of emotional control." Save tears for later.
- **Semi-annual "downloads"**: Hoarding a laundry list of criticisms paralyzes rather than incentivizes.
- **Giving feedback while angry**: Wait 24 hours; heat-of-the-moment feedback lets the subordinate shift blame to you.
- **Burning a bridge** when you decide to leave over feedback — you may need the reference; manage the terms of your own exit.

## Worked Example — What Great Feedback Sounds Like
Christina's boss, before a high-stakes meeting: *"Your extensive knowledge of the business will earn you credibility. But I notice when you're nervous you talk fast, and that can come across as junior. Take a deep breath. Take a moment to adjust to your audience. Don't be afraid to create some silence."*

Note the formula in action: **specific** (talking fast when nervous), **prescriptive** (breathe, pause, use silence), **timely** (right before the meeting), **business-framed** (credibility with a senior leader). Contrast with the useless "You're doing great!" or the vague "be more vulnerable." **Lesson: actionable feedback names the behavior, the cost, and the fix — anything less is noise.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Great feedback is timely, specific, prescriptive, and framed in business outcome.
2. Feedback is scarce precisely for those who need it most — so *ask for it explicitly*, laser-focused on a recent high-EP encounter.
3. To get it: recognize you need it, develop a thick skin, request specific/timely/prescriptive input, hire a coach if superiors won't, build a peer feedback circle, cultivate a sponsor.
4. To give it: frequent discrete pointers (not downloads), never while angry, good things first, embed correctives, catch people getting it right, preface with "I want you to be successful," discuss appearance via personal brand, enlist a third party if litigation is a risk.
5. Demonstrate you'll act on feedback — or leaders conclude you're not worth the effort.
