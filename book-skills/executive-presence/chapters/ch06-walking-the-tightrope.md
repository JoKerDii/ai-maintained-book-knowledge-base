# Chapter 6: Walking the Tightrope

## Core Idea
Women (and, differently, minorities) face a **narrow band of acceptability** across all three EP pillars — the "Goldilocks syndrome" of being always "too this, too that," never "just right." Behaviors that build a man's gravitas often read as flaws in a woman. The chapter names the double binds and offers strategies to widen your latitude.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Goldilocks Syndrome / Narrow Band of Acceptability**: "You're never 'just right.' You're too this, you're too that — and you always will be, because what's behind it is hidden bias." (Carolyn Buck Luce) Rooted in "think leader, think male" (Virginia Schein) — masculine traits = "taking charge," feminine = "taking care."
  - When to use: To recognize that contradictory feedback ("too aggressive"/"too passive") is *structural bias*, not a personal failing — then strategize around it rather than internalize it.

- **The Likability vs. Competence Double Bind**: The most tenacious bind. Successful women (unlike men) suffer social rejection; the Heidi/Howard experiment showed identical accomplishments judged "selfish/political" as a woman, admirable as a man. Replayed as Kathryn/Martin (2013): liked slightly *more*, but trusted far less (6.4 vs 7.8).

- **The Three-Pillar Tightrope** (how the band narrows in each dimension):
  - **Appearance**: too much makeup (undermines credibility) vs. too frumpy; the "just right" age band is ~3 years (39–42); blunder list twice as long as men's.
  - **Communication**: "too shrill" vs. "too tentative"; "tooting your own horn" (29% dock EP) vs. "self-deprecating" (24% dock EP); too blunt vs. too nice.
  - **Gravitas**: showing teeth reads as "bitch"/"not a team player"; forceful vs. circumspect; even a stellar reputation (Christine Lagarde) gets recast as "not populist enough."

## Key Concepts
- **Goldilocks syndrome**: Never "just right" — the signature of hidden bias against those who don't fit the leader stereotype.
- **"Damned if they do, doomed if they don't"**: The catch-22 for professional women; 31% say "too bossy" undermines a woman's EP, 31% say "too passive" does.
- **Invisible knapsack of privilege**: The kit of inborn advantages the dominant group can't perceive they carry (McIntosh, via Hewlett).
- **The angry-black-woman stereotype**: The specter African-American women expend energy to disprove; the same anger that "burnishes" an Alec Baldwin destroys a Paula Deen.

## Mental Models — How to Win Greater Latitude
- **Show teeth for the team, not yourself**: "Take the I out of your argument." Come from "what's best for the company," not "what's bugging me." Consider "how will this idea be heard by those in power?" (Rosalind Hudnell — "arguing with grace").
- **Speak truth to power with humor**: Humor widens the band of receptivity (Stella defused a table-pounding boss: "No, Bob, they're waiting to see *you* do it" — then privately reframed it as ensuring he "had the whole picture").
- **Be a sniper, not a machine gun**: "Pick your target, pick your moment, fire your best shot." Don't speak unless you have a strong point; wait to make a counterpoint or ask an insightful question. (Lori Massad) — but when *leading*, take charge immediately with a bold statement.
- **Build a brand that grants you latitude** (Richard Branson): a consciously built, relentlessly projected brand "outside the box" ensures others "won't dare box you in."
- **Wield your credentials consciously**: Request "Dr." / mention your faculty post when it buys gravitas (Laura Sherbin).
- **Show you care**: Public activities benefiting the disenfranchised win latitude on the likability front (Gillibrand, Warren "learned from Hillary's mistakes").

## Anti-patterns
- **Framing arguments personally** ("what's bugging *me*") — brands you "not a team player" and underscores outsider status.
- **Adopting a man's abrasive style** (see Ch 7, Adachi's failed cold call) — it backfires when it's not you.
- **Internalizing contradictory feedback as a personal defect** rather than recognizing structural bias.

## Worked Example
*Stella and the table-pounding boss.* Stella's boss was so abrasive ("he'd pound the table, curse, and denigrate anyone who took issue") that "no one dared to challenge him." When she objected — objectively, not confrontationally — to his sales strategy, he went nuclear: "Does anyone want a piece of Stella before I get mine?" The room went silent for two heartbeats. Stella: **"No, Bob, they're waiting to see *you* do it."** He burst out laughing. Then, crucially, she sought him out afterward: "I just want to make sure you had the whole picture… it's my responsibility to put the facts on the table so every one of your decisions is fully informed." His respect "probably tripled." **Lesson: humor widens the band of receptivity in the moment; a private, team-framed follow-up converts a confrontation into gravitas.**

## Key Takeaways
1. Contradictory feedback is the signature of hidden bias (Goldilocks) — not your failing.
2. Likability-vs-competence is the deepest bind: success costs women trust and warmth.
3. The band narrows across all three pillars — appearance, communication, gravitas.
4. Widen your latitude: argue for the team (take out the "I"), use humor, snipe don't spray, build a latitude-granting brand, wield credentials, show you care.
5. The goal isn't to erase the bias but to navigate it deliberately while staying authentic (→ Ch 7).
