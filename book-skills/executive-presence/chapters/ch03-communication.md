# Chapter 3: Communication

## Core Idea
Communication is "not so much *what* you say but *how* you say it" — and it's something you can condition and control. You are "always on": every email, hallway comment, and pitch conveys your authority. Effective communication is about **engagement** — how powerfully you connect with your audience — and, strikingly, content is the *least* important element.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Six Communication Traits of EP**:
  1. **Superior speaking skills** (top pick). Articulateness; voice free of tics (uptalk, "um/you know," shrillness).
  2. **Ability to command a room** (~half of respondents).
  3. **Assertiveness / forcefulness** (48%).
  4. **Ability to read an audience/room** (EQ in action — 39% women, 33% men).
  5. **Sense of humor & ability to banter**.
  6. **Body language & posture** (21% named, but anecdotally far higher impact).
  - When to use: Master at least three to be "heard above the din."

- **The medium beats the message**: A 2012 analysis of 120 financial spokespersons: persuasiveness = passion (27%) + voice quality (23%) + presence (15%); **content only 15%**. "Effective communication is about the medium, not the message." Think TED talk, not MIT physics seminar.

- **The lower-voice payoff** (timbre & pitch): A voice in the lower-frequency range reads as successful, sociable, smart. Optimal ~125 Hz. A **22 Hz drop in a CEO's voice frequency correlated with +$187,000 compensation** and a $440M-larger company (Duke/UCSD study of 792 CEOs).
  - How: Speech coaching, deep breathing, warm water with honey & lemon (Thatcher's trick). You can't judge your own voice — you hear it through your skull bones — so get feedback.

## Key Concepts
- **"Always on"**: Every verbal encounter is a vital chance to build a positive impression.
- **Command a room**: Grab and keep an audience's attention; "never be boring."
- **Reading a room**: Sensing mood and cultural cues, then course-correcting on the spot.
- **Uptalk**: Ending declaratives on a high note (like a question) — denies closure and "undermines your whole message."
- **Shrillness**: The single most-cited destroyer of a woman's EP; the "hint of hysteria."
- **Props**: Notes, slides, lists, glasses, podiums — anything that blocks eye contact and signals you don't command your material.

## Mental Models — Techniques to Command a Room
- **Establish connection in 5 seconds**: "Touch the audience" by unveiling just enough of your inner core that they start pulling for you. (Hewlett opened a Latina-leadership keynote with her own working-class Welsh story — tension dissolved.)
- **Deliver words as a musician delivers notes**: Use phrasing, inflection, pace, and *silence*. "A musician's impact lies in the rests." "There is nothing so powerful as silence to make people sit up." (Krawcheck)
- **Use narrative, not bullet points**: Stories grab and hold. Ape the actor, not the policy wonk. An exhaustive fact-laden deck signals a *lack* of confidence.
- **Don't snow people with data**: Get to the bottom line; use a few supporting data points; get to Q&A quickly.
- **Get rid of props → make eye contact**: "Nothing boosts your leadership presence more than signaling you're totally present." (Brady Dougan)
- **Be succinct**: "The more you keep speaking, the more you dilute your core message." Don't preface with "I talked to thirty-seven people."

## Anti-patterns
- **Making too many lists / burying your head in notes** (Elaine was passed over for the C-suite: "she comes across as some kind of glorified executive assistant").
- **Crying / any sign of nerves** (Patricia Schroeder's tears "erased the perception" she was fit to be chief executive).
- **Checking your smartphone / obvious inattention** — a keystone EP-killer.
- **Being long-winded, breathless, or reliant on props.**
- **For assertiveness: charging in with "This is what I want and I want it now"** — frame demands around what's best for the company; "take the I-word out."

## Worked Example
*Barbara Adachi's ultimatum.* Promoted to regional head of Human Capital at Deloitte, Adachi asked to sit on the management committee. A partner told her, "People just don't see you as a leader, Barbara." Instead of storming out, she retorted, "That's because I'm not on the management committee!" — breaking the ice with humor. Then she raised the stakes with a framed, non-idle ultimatum: "If the regional director won't step down… then I don't want to be leader in Northern California, because I'd have all the responsibility and none of the authority." She was willing to walk away. **He put her on the committee.** Lesson: assertiveness lands when it's forceful *and* framed around the role/company, backed by genuine resolve — not personal pique.

## Key Takeaways
1. It's *how* you say it, not what — and you can control it.
2. Content is the least persuasive element; passion, voice, and presence dominate.
3. Lower your pitch, slow down, use silence, and lose the props to make eye contact.
4. Command a room by connecting fast, telling stories, and being succinct.
5. Read the room and course-correct — obliviousness signals you're a "closed circuit."
6. Assertiveness works when framed around the company's interest, delivered with controlled tone.
