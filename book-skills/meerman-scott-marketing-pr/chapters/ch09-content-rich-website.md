# Chapter 9: The Content-Rich Web Site

## Core Idea
Your Web site is the intersection where every other online initiative (blogs, podcasts, news releases, media room) comes together to tell a unified story. Content — how it's organized and how it drives action — is what matters, not design or technology for their own sake.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Content first, design/tech second**: begin any new site or redesign with the *content strategy*, thinking like a publisher.
  - **When to use**: every site project — resist the pull of agencies chasing hip design and IT chasing architecture.
  - **How**: identify buyers and their problems, then organize content to inform each persona and drive them to action; treat "content as king, president, and Pope."
- **The site as unifying hub**: integrate interrelated media (podcasts ↔ blogs ↔ releases ↔ media room ↔ division/country sites) into one coherent personality.
- **Rigor of process for large sites** (Textron model): a defined content-management process — freshness, review, legal sign-off, cross-unit collaboration, annual usability labs and site audits.

## Key Concepts
- **Content-rich Web site**: the comprehensive place that pulls together buyers, markets, media, and products; organizes your organization's online personality.
- **Widgets & badges**: small shareable applications/graphics (RSS-style links, banner-like badges, social-bookmarking tools) that let supporters spread your content and link back.
- **Voice of the customer**: capturing what buyers actually want and building it into all electronic communications.
- **"More art than science"**: great sites reflect the passion and personality of someone who cares; the right mix of content, design, and technology "just feels right."

## Mental Models
- Prefer **"content is king, president, and Pope"** over technology-worship: the days of "the guy with the server under the desk" are over.
- For a **large multi-brand company**, a unified corporate hub matters *more*, not less — a unified personality reaps benefits (and search logs reveal buyers hunting for product info on the corporate site).
- Include **opposing viewpoints** in your content (Hinerfeld): it makes content more interesting and more authentic — "People don't want PR, they want something that's real."

## Anti-patterns
- **Webmaster/technology domination**: focusing on plug-ins, HTML/XML, server tech while content withers.
- **Designer/agency domination**: obsessing over Flash and pretty looks with no content substance.
- **Throwing content "at a Webmaster" and assuming it works**: large sites need process, coordination, and departmental cooperation.

## Worked Example
**NRDC (Natural Resources Defense Council).** Its 1.2 million members are "the best storytelling asset available," so the site is built to enlist their voices. It offers widgets and badges (biofuels badge, social-bookmarking to del.icio.us/Digg, Squidoo lenses) that bloggers embed to spread the message and link back. Landing pages like the Pierce Brosnan-narrated *Lethal Sound* whale-sonar video bundle calls to action — message elected officials, donate, send postcards, read the linked release and report. Daniel Hinerfeld (ex-NPR) produces broadcast-quality podcasts that deliberately include opposing views and hard questions to stay authentic, and profiles young staff to personalize the institution and reach the MySpace generation. **Textron** (contrast, large multi-brand): $10B, 37,000 employees, dozens of brand sites (Bell Helicopter, Cessna, E-Z-GO). Search logs showed buyers seeking product info on the *corporate* site, so Sarah Garnsey built it out with CEO videos, photos, employee stories — governed by a rigorous content process, annual usability labs, site audits, and a company-wide Web Summit.

## Key Takeaways
1. The content-rich site is the hub that unifies all your online media into one personality.
2. Start every site project with content strategy; design and technology serve content.
3. Give supporters widgets/badges so they spread your content and link back.
4. Large multi-brand orgs need a unified corporate hub plus disciplined content process.
5. Authenticity — including opposing views and real personality — beats polished PR.
