# Chapter 3: Reaching Your Buyers Directly

## Core Idea
Stop describing your products from an egotistical perspective; instead understand distinct buyer personas, then think and act like a publisher — delivering content that solves your buyers' problems and drives them toward a clearly defined goal.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Buyer Personas (the basics)**: break your audience into distinct groups and catalog everything you know about each, so you can build content targeted to each demographic.
  - **When to use**: before creating any content — "once you understand the audience very well, then (and only then)" set out to satisfy their needs.
  - **How**: identify each group, its goals, its problems; a single site may serve several (e.g. a college site: young alumni, older alumni, prospective students, parents, current students — each with a distinct goal). Full method in Ch 10.
- **Think Like a Publisher**: manage content as a valuable asset with a content strategy first, mechanics/design second.
  - **How**: answer the publisher's questions — Who are my readers? How do I reach them? What are their motivations? What problems can I solve? How do I inform *and* entertain? What content will compel them to act?
- **Know the Goals and Let Content Drive Action**: define the business goal first (sell, generate contributions, get votes/joins), then build content that funnels buyers toward the action.

## Key Concepts
- **Buyer persona**: a profile of a distinct target audience segment, defined by its problems and information needs, not by your product features.
- **Targeted content**: content written for one narrow persona's specific problem, in their language.
- **Call to action**: the clear next step (products button, white-paper form, donate link) that content funnels buyers toward.
- **Thought leadership**: content that positions you as smart and market-savvy — a person/organization worth doing business with.
- **Vanity metrics trap**: traffic and Google rank don't matter in themselves; they matter only if they lead to the real goal (revenue, retention).

## Mental Models
- Ask **"What's the goal?"** first — Scott's standard response when critiquing any site or blog; astonishingly, most marketers can't answer.
- Write about **the buyer's pain, not your own**: "It is easy to write what I think, but much more difficult to write about what my buyers are thinking."
- Treat every page as a **path to action**: link from every page both to something free and to the products page.

## Anti-patterns
- **Egocentric content**: describing what your organization/product does instead of the buyer's problems — "What fails is an egocentric display of your products and services."
- **Made-up metrics as goals**: optimizing leads and traffic while ignoring revenue and retention.
- **No defined goal**: running marketing programs, sites, and blogs with no established objective.

## Worked Example
**Mike Pedersen, golf-fitness expert.** His one relentless buyer persona: "the sixty-year-old golfer who has rapidly declining physical capabilities." Pedersen is himself a fit 40-year-old, so he deliberately does *not* write for himself — "If I just wrote for myself, I'd be shooting myself in the foot because I'm not the target market." He publishes hundreds of free targeted articles ("Golf-Specific Warm-ups"), ranks #1 for "golf training," and puts a call to action at the bottom of every page ("Get my Free Golf Fitness Ebook and find out!"). Registrants join a 40,000-person email list that mostly sends *content alerts with no sales pitch* — "I know that if I provide valuable content, then I'll get more sales." He then converts to a $150 Golf Fitness Training System.

## Key Takeaways
1. Define buyer personas before creating content; a single site often needs several, each with its own goal.
2. Think like a publisher: content strategy first, then design and mechanics.
3. Lead with the buyer's problems and language, not your product's features.
4. Set a real business goal; treat traffic and rank as means, never ends.
5. Give away valuable content freely — it builds the relationship that produces sales.
