# Chapter 1: The Old Rules of Marketing and PR Are Ineffective in an Online World

## Core Idea
Before the Web, organizations had only two ways to reach an audience: buy expensive advertising or beg the media for "third-party ink." The Web breaks that monopoly — you can now reach buyers directly — so the old interruption-and-gatekeeper playbook is obsolete.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Old Rules of Marketing** (the list to unlearn):
  - Marketing = advertising (and branding); ads must appeal to the masses; ads interrupt to grab attention; ads are one-way company-to-consumer; ads exist to sell products; campaigns have a limited life; creativity matters most; winning agency awards beats winning customers; marketing and PR are separate disciplines.
- **The Old Rules of PR** (the list to unlearn):
  - The only way to get ink was through the media; you communicated only via press releases to a handful of reporters; nobody saw the release but journalists; you needed "significant news" to justify a release; jargon was fine because reporters understood it; releases required third-party quotes; buyers only learned of a release if the media covered it; success was measured in clip books.
- **When to use both lists**: As a diagnostic — if your current program matches these bullets, you are playing by dead rules.

## Key Concepts
- **TV-industrial complex** (Seth Godin): the old system where creatives dream up ways to interrupt people with one-way messages.
- **Interruption marketing**: forcing prospects to stop what they're doing to receive a message they didn't want.
- **Third-party ink**: coverage earned when a journalist writes about you — the old measure of PR success.
- **Journalistic black hole**: the fate of the ~25,000 untargeted press releases that reach a reporter and produce zero stories.
- **Gobbledygook**: jargon-laden release language that means something to a trade reporter but is noise to everyone else.
- **Clip book**: the scrapbook of press mentions PR people showed bosses to prove they'd done their job.

## Mental Models
- Think of advertising as **a money pit** when your audience is niche: mass buys work only for megabrands with broad appeal (P&G, blockbuster films), not for niche products, local services, or specialized nonprofits.
- Use the **"when I arrive at your site, you already have my attention"** test: stop trying to grab attention you already own; educate instead of interrupt.
- Treat the media as **still important but no longer the only door** — if you tell your story well directly, the media find you.

## Anti-patterns
- **Making the Web behave like TV**: agencies great at "creative work" assume interruption skills transfer online. They don't.
- **Broadcast press releases**: spraying untargeted releases to reporters who never read them.
- **Egotistical, jargon-first messaging**: writing for insiders while millions searching the Web for solutions see only spin.

## Worked Example
Scott shops online for a compact SUV. The big-three automaker sites give him flash-video TV commercials, pretty pictures, and low-financing offers — "Ask Dr. Z" turns out to be a cartoon that first wants him to watch more ads. He finds no real people and no authentic content. Where does he actually make his decision? On Edmunds' Car Space, a consumer social-networking site with 2,000+ owner messages on the Toyota FJ Cruiser — "dozens of clicks removed" from the automaker sites. Lesson: buyers educate themselves on independent, content-rich sites, not on your ad-driven brochure-ware.

## Key Takeaways
1. Advertising and earned media are no longer your only options — the Web lets you reach niche buyers directly at a fraction of the cost.
2. Audit your program against the Old Rules lists; every bullet you still follow is a liability.
3. Untargeted press releases produce nothing — journalists now go looking for stories via search and blogs.
4. The media still matter for many organizations, but "do a good job telling your story directly, and the media will find you."
5. If your real goal is a Super Bowl ad, an agency award, or getting your CEO on TV, the new rules aren't for you.
