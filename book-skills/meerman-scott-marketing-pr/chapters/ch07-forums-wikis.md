# Chapter 7: Forums, Wikis, and Your Targeted Audience

## Core Idea
Your best customers gather in online forums, list serves, and wikis — a "massive focus group offering thoughts for free." Monitor them, participate authentically as a credible community member (not a shill), and respond fast when a crisis erupts where it's happening.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rapid, in-forum crisis response**: react quickly and honestly *in the same forums* where discussion is happening.
  - **When to use**: the moment a negative thread about you gains traction.
  - **How**: post once, in your own name, acknowledge the problem, apologize, state your plan. The Web's linking amplifies your response across other sites — you don't need to post everywhere.
- **Authentic forum participation (the B&H model)**: earn standing by contributing broadly, then you've banked trust for when trouble comes.
  - **How**: monitor ~a dozen boards daily; contribute on topics where you have genuine expertise; take sales conversations offline; let others praise you and simply thank them — don't self-promote.
- **Wikipedia hygiene**: search Wikipedia for your company, brands, CEO, board; correct factual inaccuracies (employee counts) but never spin — the community reverts biased edits and enforces "neutral point of view."
- **Create your own forum/wiki**: if no community exists for your niche, start one and sit at the center of your market's information.

## Key Concepts
- **Going "dark"**: not participating at all during a crisis — reads as "what are they hiding?"
- **List serve**: a group email list; members post, messages go out by email (embed your URL in your signature).
- **Wiki**: a site anyone can edit (Wikipedia); red links = pages not yet written — an invitation to contribute expertise.
- **Mini-market study**: previewing items or ideas in a forum to gauge demand (collectSPACE dealers).

## Mental Models
- Treat the community's space as **their turf**: "go to their turf instead of expecting them to come to yours."
- **Credibility is the asset**: don't chime "that's right" just to get your name in — protect legitimacy above reach.
- To succeed at a **blog you need something to say; at a wiki you need to be an expert** and have resources to maintain it (Goldstein).

## Anti-patterns
- **The Sony BMG rootkit disaster**: Sony stayed dark during a blogstorm over its DRM rootkit; an exec finally went on NPR *radio* (wrong forum) and dismissed concerns ("why should they care what a rootkit is?"). Result: state lawsuits, settlements with 40 states, lasting reputation damage.
- **Standing on the sidelines**: posting only when you have something to sell.
- **Manipulating wiki entries**: removing unflattering-but-true references — the community fights back.

## Worked Example
**B&H Photo-Video — the counter-example to Sony.** During the Nikon D200 supply shortage, angry photographers on Nikonians and DPReview forums accused B&H of taking orders then canceling them and jumping phone customers ahead of the alert list. Henry Posner, B&H's director of corporate communications (a former working photographer, so he has legitimacy) — for whom forum participation is literally in the job description — posted under his own name: "you're right," explained what happened (Nikon USA's supply constraints, beyond B&H's control), apologized ("we regret and apologize for having vexed you"). "One guy with one post changed the whole tone of the thread and the reputation of B&H" — subsequent posts turned "incredibly positive." Because Posner had banked credibility through months of genuine contribution, customers extended sympathy and belief.

## Key Takeaways
1. Monitor the forums/boards/wikis where your customers actually congregate — far outside mainstream media.
2. Participate all the time, adding value — don't wait for a crisis.
3. When crisis hits, respond fast and honestly in the same forum, in your own name.
4. Never go dark; silence reads as guilt.
5. Keep Wikipedia accurate, never spun; consider founding a forum/wiki if your niche lacks one.
