# Chapter 4: Blogs — Tapping Millions of Evangelists to Tell Your Story

## Core Idea
A blog is the easiest way to push your ideas into the market, get instant feedback, and be found by search engines — but before you write one, learn the medium by monitoring and commenting.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Three Uses of Blogs for Marketing and PR** (do them in this order):
  1. **Monitor** — track what millions are saying about you, your market, and your products.
  2. **Comment** — participate in conversations on other people's blogs to become known and voice your view.
  3. **Create/write** your own blog to begin and shape conversations.
  - **Why the order**: monitoring teaches you the influential voices and unwritten etiquette; commenting builds relationships before you publish; only then take the plunge. Becoming known internally as a "blog-monitoring expert" also makes it a smaller leap to win permission to blog.
- **Web as a City (not a newspaper)**: a mental model for interpreting blogs and online content.
  - **How to use**: treat blogs as independent citizen voices (the soapbox preacher, the friend who recommends books); Craigslist = the corner-store bulletin board, eBay = a garage sale, Amazon = a bookstore. Consider the source; take blogs with a grain of salt, but ignore them at your peril.

## Key Concepts
- **Blog**: a special kind of Web site, usually by one passionate person with "fire in the belly," posts in reverse-chronological order, tagged into categories, built on easy CMS-like software (no HTML needed).
- **Blogosphere monitoring / text mining**: technology that extracts and measures trends across millions of blogs (e.g. Technorati) — a source of unsolicited market intelligence.
- **First-mover advantage**: early bloggers in a category earn a reputation for innovation; opportunities still exist.
- **Employee blogging guidelines**: govern *behavior*, not the technology.

## Mental Models
- Prefer **guiding people over blocking technology**: don't write draconian blog-specific rules; have behavior policies (no harassment, no revealing secrets, no insider trading, no trashing competitors) that already cover blogs, email, forums.
- Treat **negative comments as credibility**: opposing views show two sides and a passionate readership — don't delete them.
- Compute ROI crudely but honestly: ask every new contact **"How did you learn about me?"**

## Anti-patterns
- **Dismissing bloggers as "not real journalists"**: the Rathergate lesson — CBS ignored pajama-clad bloggers who spotted forged documents; it cost Dan Rather his job. Bloggers never claimed to be journalists; they're a different, valuable source.
- **Command-and-control blogging**: forcing all posts through corporate communications kills authenticity.
- **Not monitoring at all**: only ~20% of companies respond when mentioned — the rest are blind to their own reputation.

## Worked Example
**Draft Mark Warner** (Eddie Ratliff): a lone volunteer with "a view of a cow pasture" built a grassroots presidential draft movement starting on election night 2004 — four years early. He created a blog, Yahoo! Groups, and Meetups, then systematically left comments on hundreds of political blogs praising Warner's record ("best-managed state in America"). Result: 10,000+ blogs mentioning Warner, organizations in ~38 states, and 20 speaking invitations for every one Warner could accept. The tactic — concerted, on-topic commenting that points back to your own site — works for any organization, not just politics. (Warner ultimately declined to run, but the model stands.) Contrast: **Alacra** (Steve Goldstein, "You are what you publish") — a 100-person firm out-published 40,000-employee rivals like Thomson because its CEO blogged early and none of theirs did.

## Key Takeaways
1. Monitor first, comment second, publish third.
2. Interpret the Web as a city of independent voices, not a newspaper demanding balance.
3. Monitoring the blogosphere is non-negotiable reputation management — run queries on your names, products, and executives.
4. Govern employee behavior, not the blogging tool; encourage authentic voices.
5. Early bloggers still capture first-mover advantage in many categories.
