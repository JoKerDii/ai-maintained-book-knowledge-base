# Chapter 12: How to Write for Your Buyers

## Core Idea
Every time you write — even a news release — start with your buyers and their problems in their own plain words, then offer proof. Kill the jargon ("gobbledygook") that makes companies sound identical and communicate nothing.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The one rule of effective writing**: "When you write, start with your buyers, not with your product."
  - **How**: (1) segment buyers into personas (Ch 10); (2) identify each persona's situations, problems, business issues, needs; (3) only *then* communicate your expertise, in their real-world language, with proof.
- **The Gobbledygook test / substitution test**: take your marketing language and swap in a competitor's name and product — if it still makes sense, the language is generic and useless; it fails to explain why *you* are the right choice.
- **The gobbledygook diagnosis** (why the jargon happens): marketers who don't understand buyers or how the product solves their problems bug product managers for features, reverse-engineer buyer language from features (not buyer input), find-and-replace "product"→"solution," and slather on superlatives.

## Key Concepts
- **Gobbledygook**: meaningless overused adjectives/phrases — *next generation, flexible, robust, world class, scalable, easy to use, cutting edge, mission critical, market leading, industry standard, best of breed*. (Factiva study: of ~388,000 releases in 9 months, ~74,000 used at least one; "next generation" led with 9,895.)
- **Editors' overused-words list** (Schmidt survey of 80 editors): *leading* (94%), "we're excited about…" (76%), *solutions* (68%), "a wide range of…" (64%), *unparalleled* (62%), *unsurpassed* (53%).
- **Proof**: buyers want the problem named in their words, then evidence the product works.
- **Quote-worthiness**: quote spokespeople with words you'd actually want to see in print (not "we're thrilled…").

## Mental Models
- **Write as if to a relative you don't see often**: friendly and familiar but respectful — not jargon-stiff, not trying-too-hard phony.
- **Say "movies and TV shows," not "quality entertainment content"**: plain concrete nouns beat inflated abstractions.
- If **everyone in your industry writes this way**, that's the opportunity — plain language makes you stand out.

## Anti-patterns
- **Jargon as a cover for not understanding buyers**: "industry-leading solutions that streamline business process" — Huh?
- **Find-and-replace "solution" marketing**: decreeing your product is a solution instead of proving it.
- **Interchangeable copy**: language that works equally for your competitor isn't selling you.

## Worked Example
**The Disney corporate-overview rewrite.** Scott quotes Disney's actual page: "[Company X] has remained faithful in its commitment to producing unparalleled entertainment experiences based on its rich legacy of quality creative content and exceptional storytelling… integrated, well-connected businesses that operate in concert to maximize exposure and growth worldwide." His buyer-first rewrite starts with the *buyer's problem*: "Many television and cinema fans today are frustrated with the state of the American entertainment industry. They believe today's films and shows are too derivative and that entertainment companies don't respect their viewers' intelligence." Then plain, human reassurance: "Like our audience, we care about and enjoy movies and TV shows — that's why we're in this business in the first place. As such, we pledge to always…" The lesson: name the buyer's frustration in real words before describing yourself. **The Gobbledygook Manifesto** meta-example: Scott coined the phrase (0 Google hits), blogged the study + sent a release; in three weeks ~500 hits and reader-contributed additions (best practices, proactive, synergy, paradigm shift) — proof of the blog feedback loop.

## Key Takeaways
1. Start every piece with the buyer's problem, in the buyer's own words — then prove it.
2. Run the substitution test; interchangeable copy is worthless.
3. Ban gobbledygook adjectives; they signal you don't understand your buyer.
4. Write friendly-but-respectful, concrete, human — like talking to a relative.
5. Even news releases and quotes are writing opportunities; make quotes print-worthy.
