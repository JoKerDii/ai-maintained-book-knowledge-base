# Chapter 21: Make It Happen

## Core Idea
Don't be overwhelmed — the Web is iterative, not linear, so implement the New Rules in bits and pieces. Pick a first step, experiment, and just get out there: "on the Web, you are what you publish."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Start in bits and pieces** (three suggested first steps):
  1. **Do buyer persona research** — read what buyers read, attend their webinars, read a few blogs, interview a few buyers — to narrow the book's big list to the initiatives most appropriate for you.
  2. **Add a few pages of targeted thought-leadership content** to your existing site (with homepage links) — no redesign needed.
  3. **Read and comment on blogs in your market** to coax out your voice, then start your own (password-protected first, then open it).
- **Iterative vs. linear**: unlike a print/direct-mail campaign that demands perfection, approvals, third parties, and money before one "release day," Web content can go live fast, be checked, changed on the fly, or just deleted — far less stressful, so experiment rather than trying to get everything perfect at once.

## Key Concepts
- **You don't have to do it all**: even Scott doesn't — he blogs but has no podcast and no original YouTube videos. "Do what you can and what works for you."
- **Word of mouse**: the online multiplier of word of mouth (Batterson) — one click preaches to thousands.
- **The Web as front door / test drive**: your site and blog let people experience you before committing (attending, buying, joining).
- **Universal applicability**: works for corporations, nonprofits, rock bands, politicians, and "just a ___" (pastor, painter, lawyer, sales rep, real estate agent).

## Mental Models
- **"The greatest message deserves the greatest marketing"** (Batterson): be as smart about delivering your message as Madison Avenue and Hollywood.
- **Delete-ability lowers the stakes**: a Web page that doesn't work can just be removed — you can't do that with a print ad.
- **Expect resistance and proceed anyway**: colleagues will insist the old rules apply; "By now you know that they are wrong."

## Anti-patterns
- **Analysis paralysis / trying to do everything at once**: the overwhelm that stops people — the antidote is selective experimentation.
- **Waiting for perfection**: importing the linear, big-release-day, approval-heavy mindset to the iterative Web.

## Worked Example
**Mark Batterson / National Community Church (TheaterChurch.com)** — a Washington, D.C. pastor with *no church building*. He runs church in theaters and uses a content-rich site, weekly-service podcasts, a motivational webcast series, video, an email newsletter, and his popular Evotional blog to build community on- and offline. Staff hold titles like "media pastor," "digital pastor," and "buzz coordinator." His site and blog are "the front door" and a "test drive" — more people listen to the podcast and watch the webcast than attend services. Attendance exceeds 1,000 adults/weekend (70% single twentysomethings), drawn by the church's authenticity and creativity. When he blogged about his new book and asked blogging friends to post too, it hit #44 on Amazon and sold out that day. His frame: John Wesley rode 250,000 miles to preach ~40,000 sermons; "with one click of the mouse, I preach that many sermons with my podcast — that's word of mouse. The message has not changed, but the medium has."

## Key Takeaways
1. Implement in bits and pieces — no one does all of it, not even the author.
2. Good first steps: persona research, a few content pages, or reading/commenting on blogs before starting your own.
3. The Web is iterative — publish fast, change on the fly, delete what fails; experiment instead of perfecting.
4. It works for any organization and any individual — "Web content is for everyone."
5. Expect old-rules pushback; the 50+ profiled innovators prove they're wrong. Get out there and make it happen.
