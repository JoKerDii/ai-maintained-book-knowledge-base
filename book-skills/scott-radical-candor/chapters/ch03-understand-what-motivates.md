# Chapter 3: Understand What Motivates Each Person — Helping people take a step toward their dreams

## Core Idea
To build a cohesive team you need a balance of **rock stars** (stability) and **superstars** (growth). Replace judgmental "talent management" (potential) with "growth management" (trajectory), and manage each person according to the growth trajectory they *want* right now — which changes over time.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rock Stars vs. Superstars** (from a leader at Apple):
  - **Rock star** = gradual growth trajectory; solid as the Rock of Gibraltar; loves and has mastered the craft; doesn't want the next job. Recognize and reward — **don't promote them out of a role they love**.
  - **Superstar** = steep growth trajectory; needs constant new challenge and learning; source of a team's growth.
  - Neither is better; both are essential. People in a superstar phase are bad at rock-star roles and vice versa.
  - When to use: Role assignment, recognition, retention, deciding whom to stretch vs. stabilize.
- **The Growth-Management Framework (2×2)**: Axes are *past performance* (bad→good) and *future growth trajectory* (gradual↕steep). Unlike the performance-potential matrix, the vertical axis has **no "bad" end** — bottom-right (excellent/gradual = rock star) is just as good as top-right (excellent/steep = superstar).
  - How: Assess performance on results + intangibles (set by the employee, as objective as possible). Describe the *future* by current growth trajectory, not a permanent label.
- **"Growth" not "potential"** (Scott Forstall): Changing one word reframes the question from the judgmental "Is this high/low potential?" to "What growth trajectory does this person *want* right now, and have I given them matching opportunities?"

## Key Concepts
- **Steep growth trajectory**: Rapid change, learning/deepening skills fast, increased impact — *not* the same as promotion or becoming a manager.
- **Gradual growth trajectory**: Stability, mastery, incremental improvement — *not* "capped out" or "B-player."
- **The Peter Principle**: Promoting people beyond their competence — an unhappy outcome for everyone.
- **No permanent markers**: Trajectories and performance change with life circumstances (kids, illness, ambitions outside work). Never lock a label onto a person.

## Mental Models
- **The problem with "passion"**: Your job is *not* to provide purpose. It's to know each person well enough to understand how *they* derive meaning. (Christopher Wren's three bricklayers each found their own meaning in the same wall.)
- **Be a partner, not an absentee manager or micromanager.** Ignoring top performers "because they don't need me" is a terrible way to build a relationship. "Choose and ignore" is as crazy for management as for marriage (Dick Costolo).
- **"It's better to have a hole than an asshole"** (Steve Jobs) — poor performers often create as much work as they do.
- **Everyone can be excellent at *something* — there's no such thing as a B-player.** But that's different from "anyone can be good at anything."

## Anti-patterns
- **Promotion = the only reward**: Promoting rock stars into roles they don't want, or making management the only path to higher pay, destroys value (the man who quit rather than accept a forced promotion).
- **Undervaluing rock stars**: Reserving top ratings only for the promotion-bound; treating tenure as a "badge of shame." (Apple honored tenure with designed awards; the WWII US Air Force sent its best pilots home to *train*, unlike the Germans who flew their aces until shot down.)
- **Ruinous Empathy toward mediocrity**: Keeping people in the wrong job for years is "the worst case of Ruinous Empathy" — it wastes human potential and is unjust to top performers.
- **Conflating management with growth**: Forcing a brilliant individual contributor (an "Einstein") into management wastes their gift and demoralizes their team.

## Worked Example
**"Look at yourself in the mirror" (low performance / steep trajectory).** Kim put "Mareva," a superb *people* leader, on the team's hardest *analytical* problem (a job requiring 75% solo spreadsheet time). Mareva grew bored, disengaged, got her lowest-ever ratings. Kim pushed harder — nothing changed. Then it dawned: the role was wrong, not the person. Mareva's gift was leading large teams. Moved to a 300-person management challenge, she became a top performer again. Diagnosis before firing: **wrong role, new-to-role, personal problems, poor culture fit, or genuinely wrong person** — and the first is *your* fault.

**Firing decision (three questions).** For "Peggy," terrible at her job and not improving, ask: (1) Have I given *Radically Candid* guidance repeatedly over time? (2) Do I understand the impact on her colleagues (usually driving them nuts long before it reaches you)? (3) Have I sought a second opinion? Managers wait too long, telling themselves four lies: *it'll get better; somebody's better than nobody; a transfer is the answer; it's bad for morale* — when keeping a poor performer is what actually hurts morale.

## Key Takeaways
1. Every strong team needs **both** rock stars and superstars; neither trajectory is superior.
2. Manage to the trajectory a person **wants right now**, not the one you'd choose for them.
3. Reward rock stars with recognition, top ratings, "guru" status, tenure honors — **not** unwanted promotions.
4. Keep superstars **challenged**; don't block or squash their ambition (build safeguards so managers can't).
5. **Raise the bar** on true underperformers — tolerating mediocre work is Ruinous Empathy toward the whole team.
6. **No permanent labels** — see each person with fresh eyes as their life and ambitions change.
