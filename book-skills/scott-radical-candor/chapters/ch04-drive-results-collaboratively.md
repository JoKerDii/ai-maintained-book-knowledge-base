# Chapter 4: Drive Results Collaboratively — Telling people what to do doesn't work

## Core Idea
The ultimate goal of Radical Candor is to achieve collaboratively what you could never achieve alone. Even at Apple, telling people what to do didn't work. The **Get Stuff Done (GSD) wheel** is the repeatable process that lets a team "burst the bounds of their brain."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Get Stuff Done (GSD) Wheel** — seven steps, cycled quickly. Skipping a step *or* getting stuck on one both waste time.
  1. **Listen** — hear team ideas; create a culture where they listen to each other.
  2. **Clarify** — help sharpen half-formed ideas so they aren't "squished" before understood. "As the boss, you are the editor, not the author."
  3. **Debate** — rigorously test ideas ("the rock tumbler"). Focus on ideas, not egos.
  4. **Decide** — push decisions to the person closest to the facts; you're usually *not* the decider.
  5. **Persuade** — bring along those who weren't in the room (emotion + credibility + logic).
  6. **Execute** — minimize the "collaboration tax" so the team can do the work.
  7. **Learn** — learn from results and start the cycle again.
  - When to use: Any significant team decision or initiative.
  - How: You don't have to be in every step; you must ensure each *happens* and none drags.
- **Two listening styles** — pick the one natural to you:
  - **Quiet listening** (Tim Cook): stay silent, keep a neutral face, let people fill the space. Risk: people waste time guessing what you think; reassure them by sometimes saying what you think.
  - **Loud listening** (Steve Jobs): state a strong point of view and *insist they challenge it back*. Paul Saffo's "strong opinions, weakly held." Only works if people feel safe to push back.
- **Persuasion = Aristotle's three elements**: **Pathos** (the *listener's* emotions, not yours), **Ethos** (credibility = expertise + humility; use "we" not "I"), **Logos** (logic — "show your work").

## Key Concepts
- **Collaboration tax vs. collaboration investment**: If any GSD step drags, collaboration feels like a tax. As boss, absorb much of the tax yourself so the team can execute.
- **"Get it right" vs. "be right"** (Andy Grove on Steve Jobs): Great bosses care more about reaching the right answer than being right; they *insist* people tell them when they're wrong.
- **Garbage-can decision-making** (James March): Letting whoever's around the table (or the most senior) decide, instead of the person with the best information.
- **"Push decisions into the facts"** (Jack Dorsey): Get *facts, not recommendations* — people bake ego into recommendations. Go to the source; "spelunk" into details occasionally.

## Mental Models
- **The rock tumbler** (Steve Jobs's neighbor): Debate is friction that polishes both ideas and people. Your job is to turn the tumbler *on*, not off — but not leave it running until there's only dust.
- **"You are the editor, not the author."** In the clarify step, help people get ideas ready for debate; don't write their ideas for them.
- **The burden of understanding is on the explainer, not the listener** (FDR & Keynes): If a genius idea stays locked in someone's head, it may as well not exist. Show, don't tell; select, eliminate, emphasize (Georgia O'Keeffe).
- **"Give the quiet ones a voice"** (Jony Ive) — the goal of listening, whichever style you use.

## Anti-patterns
- **Spinning the rope too fast** (Kim's AdSense reorg): Making the *right* structural change the *wrong* way — deciding unilaterally, skipping listen/clarify/debate/persuade — cost her three of five reports. "You can't do that to people and expect them to stick around."
- **"I am the decider"** (George W. Bush): Assuming the title grants decision power; skipping straight to "persuade" while the team knows the plan is impractical (the "Mark's OKRs" story).
- **Three solutions and a recommendation**: Demanding people arrive with polished answers robs them of the space to brainstorm — you abdicate the clarify step (Russ's correction).
- **Grabbing a decision because debate got painful**: Ending debate early (the seating-chart mutiny) undermines the team; set a "decide-by" date instead.
- **Skipping the persuade step**: Expecting people to execute a decision they weren't persuaded of is "a recipe for terrible results."

## Worked Example
**Creating a culture of listening (the ideas team + programmable keypads).** Overwhelmed by more good ideas than she could handle, Kim built an "ideas team" that had to *listen to any idea anyone brought*, explain rejections clearly, and help implement winners. Sarah Teng, a recent grad, suggested programmable keypads to auto-type repeated customer phrases — a 133% efficiency gain. Kim didn't just thank her; she showed a graph of the impact, framed it as making work more fun and freeing time, and re-circulated an HBR article on how hundreds of small innovations (impossible for rivals to copy) beat one big idea. Result: people started listening to *each other's* ideas without waiting for management's blessing.

**Persuasion via the listener's emotion (Jason & the deaf-accessibility feature).** Jason, whose mother was deaf, couldn't get engineers to prioritize accessibility features despite pouring emotion into his pitch. Kim asked: "What were the *engineers'* emotions?" — "Exhausted, on a death march." He'd addressed *his* emotions, not *theirs*. (Contrast: Jobs announcing iTunes for Windows led with "Hell froze over" to acknowledge the Mac faithful's sense of betrayal before the logic.)

## Key Takeaways
1. **Telling people what to do doesn't work** — even for geniuses; collaboration produces better, more executable results.
2. Run the **full GSD wheel** — skipping *or* stalling on any step wastes time downstream.
3. **You're usually not the decider**; empower the person closest to the facts, and collect facts, not recommendations.
4. **Debate is essential** — turn on the rock tumbler; keep it on ideas, not egos; set a clear end.
5. **Persuasion needs all three of Aristotle's elements** — especially the *listener's* emotions, which people most often neglect.
6. Use the **listening style natural to you** (quiet or loud); the goal either way is to give the quiet ones a voice.
