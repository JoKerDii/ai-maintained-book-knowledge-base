# Chapter 6: Guidance — Ideas for getting/giving/encouraging praise & criticism

## Core Idea
The practical toolkit for a culture of guidance. It's a two-way street that *starts with you soliciting criticism*, then giving it well (Humble, Helpful, Immediate, In Person, Praise in public/criticize in private, don't Personalize), and finally encouraging it between team members.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **HHIPP — the principles of giving impromptu guidance**: **H**umble, **H**elpful, **I**mmediate, **I**n person (praise **P**ublic / criticize in private), don't **P**ersonalize. Radical Candor "gets measured at the listener's ear, not the speaker's mouth."
- **Situation–Behavior–Impact (SBI)** (Center for Creative Leadership): Describe (1) the *situation* you saw, (2) the *behavior*, (3) the *impact*. Keeps you humble and avoids blanket judgments. Works for praise too: "In your presentation *(situation)*, the way you talked about diversifying *(behavior)* was persuasive because you showed you'd heard the other side *(impact)*."
- **The Left-Hand Column** (Argyris & Schön): Draw a line down a page; right column = what you *said*, left column = what you *thought*. Surfaces arrogant snap judgments and forces you to question them ("Is Sally really hoarding info, or did she just forget?").
- **Listen, Challenge, Commit** (Andy Grove/Intel→Apple): A strong leader has the humility to listen, the confidence to challenge, and the wisdom to know when to stop arguing and get on board — the way out of the middle-manager Catch-22.

## Key Concepts
- **Reward candor to get more of it**: If someone criticizes you, *do not critique the criticism*. Listen to understand, not to respond ("So what I hear you saying is…"). If you agree, change fast or show visible effort; if you disagree, find something to agree with, check understanding, then explain *why*.
- **Guidance is a gift, not a whip or a carrot**: Sometimes the only help you can offer *is* the conversation itself.
- **"Say it in 2–3 minutes between meetings"**: Impromptu guidance is like brushing your teeth, not a root canal. Don't "save it up" for a 1:1 or review. Guidance has a **short half-life**; unspoken criticism "explodes like a dirty bomb."
- **Say "that's wrong," not "you're wrong"** — depersonalizes and invites receptivity.
- **"Don't take it personally" is worse than useless** — work *is* personal; the phrase negates real feelings.

## Mental Models
- **Prove you can take it before you dish it out.** Start by soliciting criticism; the boss is the *exception* to "criticize in private" — invite public criticism of yourself to model that everyone should embrace it.
- **Have a go-to question**: "Is there anything I could do or stop doing that would make it easier to work with me?" Then **embrace the discomfort** — count to six of silence, don't fill it, don't reassure. Ask again if needed.
- **Gauge your guidance** — measured at the listener's ear. Explain the framework to your team and have them tag where your guidance lands (praise vs. criticism × quadrant). Most managers *fear* being jerks while employees fear the boss isn't shooting straight.
- **You're the editor of the moment, not the diplomat** — never let one person talk to you about another behind their back ("prevent backstabbing").

## Anti-patterns
- **Reply All criticism**: Never correct/criticize a person via Reply All. Reply to just that person and ask *them* to Reply All. (Praise on small things via Reply All is fine.)
- **The Abrasive Trap** (gender bias): "Abrasive/shrill/bossy" labels penalize women who challenge directly. A 5% bias in ratings can turn 58% women at entry level into 29% at leadership level. Men: don't pull punches with women. Women: demand criticism. Everyone: switch genders as a test, be specific, drop gendered language, never just say "be more likeable."
- **Personalizing** (fundamental attribution error): "You're stupid/lazy/greedy" — inaccurate and unfixable. Attack the behavior/situation, not the essence.
- **Public criticism of the person** (vs. corrections): Factual corrections, disagreements, and debates can be public; criticizing *a person* is private.

## Worked Example
**Reward criticism visibly — the "radical bander."** Russ told Kim she interrupted people (true — an "inveterate interrupter"). Rather than promise to stop (a hollow reward for his candor), she put a fat blue rubber band on her wrist and asked him — then her whole staff, then an all-hands — to *snap it* every time she interrupted. It reduced interrupting, but more importantly it broadcast: *I heard the criticism, I'm acting on it, give me more.*

**Skip-level meetings (for managers of managers).** Once a year, meet the people who work for your direct reports *without those reports present*, to help those managers get better. Rules of thumb: explain-show-explain (start by having someone skip-level *you*); never without the manager's prior consent; do it for *everyone* (not just problem teams, or it's punishment); keep it "not for attribution"; **take notes yourself and project them**; kick-start with "What's your manager doing well? → What could they do better? → What really sucks?"; prioritize issues; **share notes with the boss within ~8 minutes of the meeting's end** so it doesn't feel behind-their-back; ensure the manager makes and communicates one or two concrete changes.

## Key Takeaways
1. **Start by getting criticism** — prove you can take it; use a go-to question and embrace six seconds of silence.
2. Give impromptu guidance with **HHIPP**; keep it 2–3 minutes, immediate, in person.
3. Use **SBI** and the **left-hand column** to stay humble and avoid the fundamental attribution error.
4. **Reward candor**, never critique the criticism; act visibly on it.
5. **Gauge** your guidance at the listener's ear; watch for gender bias and the Abrasive Trap.
6. Encourage peer guidance ("Whoops the Monkey," peer gauging), **prevent backstabbing**, and don't save guidance for formal reviews.
