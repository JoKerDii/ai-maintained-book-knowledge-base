# Chapter 1: The Gap Instinct

## Core Idea
The **gap instinct** is the irresistible urge to divide everything into two distinct, conflicting groups with an imagined chasm in between (rich/poor, West/rest, developed/developing). In reality there is usually no gap — the majority sits in the middle.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Income Levels** (the single most important framework in the book): Replace the binary "developed/developing" with four levels by daily income.
  - **Level 1** (~$1/day, ~1 billion people): extreme poverty — walk hours for dirty water, gray porridge, no antibiotics, ~5 children, some die.
  - **Level 2** (~$4/day, ~3 billion): bike, sandals, gas stove, kids in school, electricity unstable; one illness throws you back.
  - **Level 3** (~$16/day, ~2 billion): cold-water tap, fridge, motorcycle, multiple jobs, kids reach high school.
  - **Level 4** (>$64/day, ~1 billion): >12 years education, car, hot/cold water, air travel — *where book readers live*.
  - When to use: Whenever you'd reach for "poor countries" vs "rich countries." Sort by level instead.
  - How: Ask "which level?" Improvement means moving up a level (each step ≈ 4× income); it usually takes generations.
- **The Four Levels as a computer game**: Everyone wants to move up; Level 1 is the *hardest* level, not the starting tutorial.

## Key Concepts
- **The mega misconception**: "The world is divided in two" — the worst misconception because it distorts all proportions.
- **Child mortality as a thermometer**: Deaths per 1,000 under-fives measures the health of a *whole society* (water, food, health care, literacy), not just children.
- **The missing majority**: Only ~9% live in low-income countries; ~75–91% live in middle/high-income — "right where the gap is supposed to be."

## Mental Models
- **Look for the majority**: The control rule for the gap instinct — the bulk of cases is almost always in the middle.
- Think of "developing/developed" as a **1965 map**: you wouldn't navigate or get a diagnosis with 50-year-old information.

## Anti-patterns
- **Comparisons of averages**: Two average numbers hide overlapping spreads, so you see a gap that isn't there (e.g., US vs. Mexico income, men vs. women SAT math scores overlap heavily).
- **Comparisons of extremes**: We recall the richest billionaire and the South Sudan famine, ignoring the middle majority (even unequal Brazil has its hump on Level 3).
- **The view from up here**: From Level 4, Levels 1–3 all look equally "poor" — like looking down from a tall building, all buildings look short. The distinctions are crucial to those living them.

## Worked Example
A student insisted "they can never live like us." Rosling drew out the binary ("the West and the rest") and tested it against bubble-chart data of babies-per-woman vs. child survival. In **1965** the data did split neatly into two boxes with a clear gap (15 small countries, 2% of people, between them). But on **today's** chart the "developing" box is nearly empty: 85% of humanity is already inside the box once labeled "developed," and only 13 countries (6% of population) remain in the old "developing" box. The gap closed — only the worldview stayed stuck.

## Key Takeaways
1. Stop sorting the world into two boxes; use the **Four Income Levels** instead.
2. Most people live in the middle (Levels 2–3) — there is no gap.
3. When you hear a gap story, suspect comparison of averages, comparison of extremes, or the view from up here.
4. Low-income countries are far more developed, and far less populated, than people imagine (~9% of humanity).
5. The Four Levels recur throughout the book to explain everything from drownings to rhinos.
