# Chapter 2: The Negativity Instinct

## Core Idea
The **negativity instinct** is our tendency to notice the bad more than the good, producing a systematically too-negative impression of the world. Things can be — and usually are — **both bad and better at the same time**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The three sources of negativity** (the mechanism):
  1. **Misremembering the past** — we romanticize history; most things used to be worse.
  2. **Selective reporting** — journalists and activists report the bad; "good news is not news."
  3. **Feeling vs. thinking** — it feels heartless to say things are improving while problems remain.
  - When to use: Diagnose *why* a negative belief feels true before accepting it.
- **Bad AND better (two thoughts at once)**: Hold a *level* (bad) and a *direction* (better) simultaneously.
  - How: For any trend, separately ask "Is the level acceptable?" and "Is it improving?" — both can be true.
- **The Possibilist stance**: Neither an optimist (sounds naïve) nor a pessimist — someone who "neither hopes without reason, nor fears without reason," resisting the overdramatic worldview.

## Key Concepts
- **The secret silent miracle of human progress**: World-changing improvements too slow/fragmented/small to ever qualify as news.
- **Statistics as therapy / "a new kind of happy pill"**: Official UN data, shown plainly, makes people feel better because reality beats their negative worldview.
- **Extreme poverty trend**: Fell from ~85% (1800) to 29% (20 years ago) to 9% today — almost halved in 20 years; <10% of people know this.
- **Improved surveillance of suffering**: More bad news partly reflects *better reporting*, not a worsening world.

## Mental Models
- **The world as a premature baby in an incubator**: Health is critically *bad*, yet improving on every measure — *better*. Saying "improving" does not mean "relax." Refuse the false choice between bad and better.
- **"Objects in your memories were worse than they appear"**: Like a mirror warning — the past was harsher than nostalgia admits.

## Anti-patterns
- **Balancing bad news with positive news**: Wrong fix — like balancing too much sugar with too much salt. Aim for accuracy, not a feel-good counterweight.
- **Letting a temporary dip define the trend**: Activists make every dip look like the end of the world (e.g., US violent crime fell from 14.5M reports in 1990 to <9.5M in 2016, yet most believe it's rising).
- **Censoring history / rosy pasts**: Hiding past miseries robs us of perspective and hope.

## Worked Example
Rosling maps Sweden's own climb up the Four Levels onto today's countries using the **World Health Chart**: the Sweden he was born into in 1948 was where **Egypt** is today (mid-Level 3, with open sewage ditches where children drowned). His mother born 1921 → Sweden like **Zambia** (Level 2); grandmother born 1891 → like **Lesotho** (border of Level 1–2); great-grandmother born 1863 → like **Afghanistan** (Level 1, extreme poverty). The lesson: every country has climbed, and today's Level 1 lives are *longer* than 1800s Swedish lives because basic modernizations (soap, plastic buckets, vaccines) have spread.

## Key Takeaways
1. Expect bad news — negative stories are selected for drama and reach you; positive change does not find you, you must find it.
2. When you hear something terrible, ask: "If an equally large improvement had happened, would I have heard about it?"
3. Distinguish *level* from *direction*: things are both bad and better.
4. Be a possibilist, not an optimist or pessimist.
5. Losing hope is the most devastating consequence of the negativity instinct — it makes people abandon methods that are actually working.
