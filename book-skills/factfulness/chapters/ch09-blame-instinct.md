# Chapter 9: The Blame Instinct

## Core Idea
The **blame instinct** is the urge to find a clear, simple reason — a guilty individual or group — for why something bad happened. It steals focus and blocks understanding: once we've found someone to "punch in the face," we stop looking for the real, systemic causes.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Look for causes, not villains** (control rule, bad outcomes): When something goes wrong, resist blaming an individual; spend energy understanding the multiple interacting causes — the *system*.
- **Look for systems, not heroes** (control rule, good outcomes): "Claim" comes as easily as "blame." When someone claims credit, ask whether it would have happened anyway.
- **The unsung heroes — institutions and technology**: The invisible drivers of progress.
  - **Institutions**: It's the *many* (plumbers, nurses, teachers, civil servants, local health workers) who build societies and win battles like Ebola — not heroic leaders.
  - **Technology**: The washing machine ("the magic machine") freed mothers' time — "In went the laundry, and out came books."

## Key Concepts
- **Blame blocks problem-solving**: Blaming a crash on a sleepy pilot makes no progress; you must ask *why* he was sleepy and how to regulate against it. Stop at the villain → no learning.
- **We blame to confirm existing beliefs**: We pick villains we already dislike — evil businessmen, lying journalists, foreigners.
- **Leaders are credited with too much power**: Mao's one-child policy — the fertility drop (6→3) happened *before* it, and many countries without it fell lower. The Pope — contraceptive use is 60% in Catholic-majority countries vs. 58% elsewhere. "Leaders don't have remote controls that reach into the bedroom."
- **"We cannot live like us"**: On CO₂, the richest billion burn >half of all fossil fuel; the poorest billion, 1%. Don't blame China/India — look in the mirror.

## Mental Models
- **The foreign disease**: Syphilis was the "Polish disease" in Russia, "German" in Poland, "French" in Germany, "Italian" in France — scapegoating is so core to human nature no one names a plague after themselves.
- **Look in the mirror**: We rarely blame ourselves — the hardest, guilt-inducing causes are often our own (immigration policy → refugee drownings).

## Anti-patterns
- **"Let's punch them in the face"**: Blaming Big Pharma for not researching poor people's diseases. Rosling traces it up the chain — boss → board → shareholders → *retirement funds* → grandma → you. No single villain; a system of incentives.
- **Blaming journalists**: They score as badly as the public on fact questions — they're misled humans, not liars. "Independent is not the same as representative." Don't expect the media to give you a fact-based worldview, any more than holiday snaps of Berlin as a GPS.
- **Blaming the refugee smugglers**: The real cause is EU policy — the 2001 directive penalizing airlines for undocumented passengers (so refugees can't fly) plus boat-confiscation (so only one-trip death-traps are used). Our own policies create the smuggling market.

## Worked Example
Investigating a suspiciously low UNICEF malaria-pill bid from tiny Rivopharm in Lugano, Rosling arrived primed to expose a scam — but was met by a limousine and silk sheets. The factory's price was *below the cost of raw materials*. The trick wasn't fraud: they'd built the world's fastest pill-making robot, so a year's supply for Angola went from raw chloroquine (Monday) to packed boxes in Genoa (Thursday). Hungary gave 30 days' credit; UNICEF paid in 4 — leaving 26 days of interest on the money. "My mind had been blocked with the idea that UNICEF were the good guys and pharma the bad guys." The villain was imaginary; the real story was innovation.

## Key Takeaways
1. When you've found someone to blame, you've stopped thinking — look past the villain to the system.
2. "Claim" is as misleading as "blame" — credit systems, not heroes.
3. Leaders (and the media) have far less direct power over outcomes than we assign them.
4. Look in the mirror: the guilt-inducing cause is often our own policy or consumption.
5. Saving the planet needs a realistic plan and new technology — not expecting 5 billion people to forgo washing machines.
