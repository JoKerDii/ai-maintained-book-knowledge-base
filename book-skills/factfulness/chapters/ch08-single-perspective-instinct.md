# Chapter 8: The Single Perspective Instinct

## Core Idea
The **single perspective instinct** is our attraction to simple ideas — a single cause we're always against, or a single solution we're always for. It feels like understanding but it blinds us. Get a **toolbox, not a hammer**, and look at problems from many angles.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Get a toolbox, not a hammer** (control rule): No one tool solves everything. If your favorite idea is a hammer, seek colleagues with screwdrivers and wrenches.
- **Four sub-rules**:
  - **Test your ideas** — have people who *disagree* with you find the weaknesses; don't just collect confirming examples.
  - **Limited expertise** — experts are experts only within their field; be humble, and be aware of others' limits too.
  - **Hammers and nails** — if you're good with a tool, you'll overuse it (mathematicians fixate on numbers, doctors on medicine).
  - **Numbers, but not only numbers** — the world can't be understood without numbers, nor with numbers alone. And **beware simple ideas/solutions** — welcome complexity; solve case-by-case.
- **"It's both, and it's case-by-case"**: The recurring answer to public-vs-private, free-market-vs-equality debates — not either/or.

## Key Concepts
- **Why a single perspective is tempting**: It saves time — you get opinions without learning the problem. "Wouldn't you rather have few opinions that are right than many that are wrong?"
- **Two sources of single-mindedness**: professional (experts overusing their tool) and ideological (free-market vs. equality fanatics).
- **Experts fail outside their field**: Nobel laureates in medicine scored 8% on the vaccination question — worse than the public. Intelligence/education is no shortcut to global knowledge.
- **Activists exaggerate their cause**: Feminists didn't know girls have nearly closed the schooling gap; the public didn't know tiger/panda/rhino populations had *risen*. Progress goes uncelebrated.

## Mental Models
- **The photo of the foot**: Relying on the media for your worldview is like judging a person from a photo of their (ugly) foot — a real part, but not the whole.
- **Watch the shoes, watch the bricks**: Mozambique's PM gauged progress not only by unreliable GDP but by people's *shoes* on May Day and whether new *bricks* kept appearing on construction sites — numbers, but not only numbers.

## Anti-patterns
- **Eradicating one disease in isolation**: Mahler's TB X-ray buses failed because villagers wanted their *actual* urgent problems treated — the insight: build general primary health care, not single-disease campaigns.
- **Ideology over measurement**: Cuba ("the healthiest of the poor" → really "the poorest of the healthy") vs. the US ("the sickest of the rich" — spends 2×+ per capita on health for 3 years' shorter life, lacking basic public insurance). Both are single-perspective failures.
- **Claiming democracy *causes* growth**: It doesn't (9 of 2016's 10 fastest-growing economies score low on democracy; South Korea rose under dictatorship). Argue for democracy as a goal in itself, not a means.

## Worked Example
After Rosling presented in Havana, the Cuban Minister of Health leapt onstage and summarized: "We Cubans are the **healthiest of the poor**" (Cuba had US-level child survival on a quarter of the income) — applause, session over. But a young health statistician quietly pulled Rosling aside and whispered: "Your data is correct but the conclusion of the minister is completely wrong. We are not the healthiest of the poor, **we are the poorest of the healthy**." Same numbers, two perspectives — and the second exposes the real question: why settle for health without the wealth and freedom the Cuban people deserve? The single perspective (central planning solves everything) hid it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Distrust single causes and single solutions — they feel like insight but misrepresent a complex world.
2. Test your favorite ideas against people who disagree; seek disconfirming information.
3. Experts (and you) are competent only within a narrow field — stay humble across boundaries.
4. Numbers are essential but incomplete; pair them with real observation of people's lives.
5. The honest answer to most big debates is "both, and case-by-case," not "either/or."
