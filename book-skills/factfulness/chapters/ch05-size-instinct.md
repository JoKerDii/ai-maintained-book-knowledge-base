# Chapter 5: The Size Instinct

## Core Idea
The **size instinct** makes us misjudge the importance of things: a lonely number looks big, and a single visible victim grabs all our attention. Get things in proportion by **comparing** and **dividing**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The two magic tools: Compare and Divide** (the core technique):
  - **Compare**: Never let a number stand alone. If offered one number, always ask for at least one more (a past value, a comparable region, a total). Big numbers always look big.
  - **Divide**: Turn amounts into rates (per person, per capita). Amounts are easier to count, but rates are more meaningful — essential when comparing different-sized groups.
- **The 80/20 rule**: Given a long list, find the few largest items (usually ~20% of items make up ~80% of the total) and understand those first. Ask "Where are the 80%?"
  - When to use: Budgets, causes of death, energy sources, any prioritization. (Oil+coal+gas = 87% of energy.)
- **The two aspects of the size instinct**: (1) a lonely number seems impressive, (2) a single identifiable victim outweighs the statistics.

## Key Concepts
- **The cruel calculus of extreme poverty**: With finite resources, the most *compassionate* act is the cost-effective one — "In the deepest poverty you should never do anything perfectly. If you do you are stealing resources from where they can be better used" (Ingegerd Rooth).
- **The PIN code of the world (1-1-1-4)**: Population in billions — Americas 1, Europe 1, Africa 1, Asia 4. By 2100: **1-1-4-5** (Africa and Asia hold 80%+). The world market's center of gravity is shifting east.
- **"Out there" vs. "here"**: "Out there" is the sum of millions of places; of course more bad things happen there — it's bigger. Each place appears on your screen only on its worst day.
- **Numbers, but not only numbers**: "The world cannot be understood without numbers. And it cannot be understood with numbers alone."

## Mental Models
- **The deaths I do not see**: Look behind the visible victim to the statistics — and behind the statistics to the individual. Both directions matter.
- **Total vs. per-person**: Blaming China for total CO₂ is "like claiming obesity was worse in China than the US because the total bodyweight of the Chinese population was higher." Divide by population.

## Anti-patterns
- **Acting on the visible victim alone**: In Nacala, Rosling treated ~1.3% of the district's dying children in the hospital; pouring resources there would abandon the 98.7% he couldn't see.
- **Reporting a lonely emotional number**: "4.2 million babies died last year" sounds huge — but it was 14.4 million in 1950 and has never been lower (and the *rate* fell from 15% to 3%).
- **Coverage ∝ drama, not deaths**: Each swine-flu death got ~82,000× more news coverage than each TB death; one Swedish bear attack (once a century) eclipsed domestic murders (one every 30 days, a 1,300× difference).

## Worked Example
In Hanoi, Rosling asked his colleague Niem to show him the Vietnam War monument. Niem pointed to a tiny 3-foot stone with a brass plate — for the "Resistance War Against America" (20 years, 1.5M Vietnamese + 58,000 Americans dead). A 12-foot marble stone marked independence from 200 years of French rule. The *real* monument — a 300-foot gold pagoda — commemorated the 2,000 years of wars with China. The monuments put the wars in perfect proportion: only by *comparing* could Rosling grasp how relatively minor "the Vietnam War" was to the people who live there.

## Key Takeaways
1. Never trust a lonely number — compare it (over time, across regions) and divide it (into a rate).
2. Use the 80/20 rule to find what actually matters in any list or budget.
3. Rates per person beat raw amounts for fair comparison.
4. Resist spending all resources on the visible victim; cost-effectiveness saves the most lives.
5. Media attention tracks drama, not death tolls — recalculate the real proportions.
