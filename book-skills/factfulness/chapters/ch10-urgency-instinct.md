# Chapter 10: The Urgency Instinct

## Core Idea
The **urgency instinct** makes a decision feel like "now or never" — when it rarely is. Fear plus urgency shuts down analytical thinking and drives drastic, untested actions with unpredictable side effects. **Take small steps.**

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Four control rules**:
  - **Take a breath** — when urgency triggers, ask for more time and more information. It's rarely now-or-never or either/or.
  - **Insist on the data** — if it's urgent and important, it should be measured. Beware data that is relevant-but-inaccurate or accurate-but-irrelevant; only relevant *and* accurate data is useful.
  - **Beware of fortune-tellers** — every prediction is uncertain; demand a full range of scenarios (best/mid/worst), never just one, and ask how often such predictions were right before.
  - **Be wary of drastic action** — ask the side effects; prefer step-by-step, evaluated improvements.
- **The five global risks worth worrying about** (where urgency is *justified* — approach with cool heads, data, and baby steps): **global pandemic, financial collapse, World War III, climate change, extreme poverty.** (Plus a sixth: the unknown risk.)
- **"Crying wolf"**: Exaggerating to force action drains credibility; constant false alarms make us numb to real urgency — "a field full of dead sheep."

## Key Concepts
- **Urgency amplifies the other instincts**: It makes them harder to control, blocks analytical thought, and pushes snap decisions.
- **We're slothful about future risk**: We have no instinct to act on far-off risks (why few save for retirement), so activists fake immediacy to wake us — at a cost.
- **Data must tell the truth, not call to action**: "A long jumper is not allowed to measure her own jumps" — the problem-solver (who always wants more funds) shouldn't control the progress data (the CDC's "suspected cases" curve stayed rising for urgency's sake).
- **Round to your own disadvantage**: When forecasting, show a mid-estimate and a range; if rounding, round against your own argument to protect credibility.

## Mental Models
- **Forecast difficulty depends on system complexity**: Weather (a week), economic growth (hard — billions of changing variables) vs. demographics (accurate for *decades* — births/deaths are simple, ~70-year cycles).
- **Take small steps, not drastic leaps**: The reflex "We must do *something*" is the danger; the something is often worse than the problem.

## Anti-patterns
- **The roadblock reflex**: Nacala (1981), Kinshasa (1995), Liberia (2014) — frightened officials block roads to stop a disease. The cure is worse: blocked roads create hunger, panic, and death.
- **"We need to create fear"**: Al Gore asked Rosling to extend worst-case CO₂ bubbles beyond the science. Rosling refused — "No numbers, no bubbles" — because exaggeration, once exposed, makes people tune out a *real* problem.
- **"Climate refugees" / blaming everything on climate**: Far-fetched single-cause claims that entrap the activists themselves and risk the movement's credibility.

## Worked Example
In Nacala (1981), Rosling faced an unexplained paralytic disease and couldn't rule out contagion. The mayor, gripped by the same worst-case fear, asked, "Should I set up a roadblock and stop the buses from the north?" Rosling — having spent days investigating the disease but **less than a minute** on the consequences — said "Yes… you have to do something." The next morning, ~20 women and children, finding the bus canceled, paid fishermen to take them south by sea. The overloaded boats capsized; everyone drowned. (The disease turned out to be cassava poisoning — hungry farmers eating unprocessed roots after selling their processed stock.) "In the rush to do something, I did something terrible." He didn't speak of it for 35 years.

## Key Takeaways
1. "Act now or lose forever" is almost always a manipulation — take a breath.
2. When something is urgent, organize the data first; don't cry wolf.
3. Demand a range of scenarios; distrust single dramatic predictions.
4. Prefer step-by-step action with evaluation over drastic, irreversible moves.
5. Reserve real urgency for the five big risks — and even there, stay coolheaded.
