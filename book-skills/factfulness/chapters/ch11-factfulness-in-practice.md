# Chapter 11: Factfulness in Practice (+ Rules of Thumb & Outro)

## Core Idea
Factfulness isn't about education or memorized statistics — it's a practiced habit of recognizing dramatic instincts and controlling them with rules of thumb. Above all, it rests on two values: **humility and curiosity**. Apply it in education, business, journalism, and your own organization.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Factfulness Rules of Thumb**: The ten control rules (one per instinct) are the practical toolkit — the bullet points from the end of each chapter:
  1. Gap → **look for the majority**
  2. Negativity → **expect bad news**
  3. Straight line → **lines might not be straight**
  4. Fear → **calculate the risks** (risk = danger × exposure)
  5. Size → **get things in proportion** (compare & divide, 80/20)
  6. Generalization → **question your categories**
  7. Destiny → **slow change is still change**
  8. Single perspective → **get a toolbox, not a hammer**
  9. Blame → **resist finding a scapegoat**
  10. Urgency → **take small steps**
- **Humility**: Aware of how hard instincts make getting facts right; realistic about what you know; happy to say "I don't know"; ready to change your mind. (It's *relaxing* — you needn't have a view on everything.)
- **Curiosity**: Actively seeking new information; embracing facts that don't fit; letting mistakes trigger curiosity, not embarrassment ("How could I be so wrong? What can I learn?").

## Key Concepts
- **Hunt ignorance in your own organization**: Ask "What are the most important facts here, and how many people know them?" Test knowledge humbly — it "releases an avalanche of curiosity."
- **Knowledge has a sell-by date**: What you learned at school expires in 10–20 years. Adults need a "brain recall" — an upgrade, like a car recalled for new brakes.
- **Replace Sombreros with Dollar Street**: Folklore-costume world maps create an illusion of exotic difference; show how regular people actually live (dollarstreet.org) instead.
- **Don't expect the media to be representative**: Journalists, activists, and politicians compete for attention with the unusual; it's up to *consumers* to consume news factfully.

## Mental Models
- **The two reasons a fact-based worldview will spread**: It's more *useful* (like an accurate GPS) and more *comfortable* (less stress and hopelessness than the dramatic worldview).
- **Humility + curiosity as the foundation** beneath all ten rules — the disposition that makes the tools work.

## Anti-patterns
- **"A typo on your CV loses the job, but putting a billion people on the wrong continent gets you promoted"**: Businesses police spelling but not fact mistakes — missing the Asia/Africa growth markets.
- **Assuming you're "getting it kind of right"**: Until someone actually tests global knowledge, everyone over-rates their own — data snippets mislead just as badly as no data when the worldview is upside down.

## Worked Example
In 1989 in the remote village of Makanga (Zaire), Rosling — researching the paralytic disease konzo — failed to explain his work to the villagers first. A crowd of ~50 gathered, two men with machetes, accusing him of stealing/selling their blood. His translator whispered "I think we should run." Instead, Rosling asked to explain. Then a barefoot ~50-year-old illiterate woman stepped out and, with "razor-sharp logic and perfect rhetoric," named the instincts at work and reasoned the crowd down: "How do you think they discovered the measles vaccine? … That is what this doctor calls RE-SEAR-CHE." She rolled up her sleeve: "Here. Doctor. Take my blood." The mob became a queue. Her **factfulness** — courage plus critical thinking, with no formal education — literally saved his life, and shows the instincts can be recognized and controlled by anyone.

## Key Takeaways
1. The ten rules of thumb are the everyday toolkit; teach them as critical thinking.
2. Ground everything in humility ("I don't know") and curiosity ("how interesting that I was wrong").
3. Test for ignorance in your own field/org — the global pattern repeats locally.
4. Keep updating your worldview; facts expire.
5. A fact-based worldview is both more useful and less stressful — that's why it will win.
