# Chapter 6: The Generalization Instinct

## Core Idea
The **generalization instinct** makes us lump together things, people, or countries that are actually very different, and jump to conclusions about a whole category from one example. We can't stop generalizing (categories are necessary) — we must generalize *correctly*.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Question your categories — five tactics** (the core toolkit):
  1. **Look for differences within groups** and **similarities across groups** — split large groups, and if two "different" groups behave alike, suspect your category.
  2. **Beware of "the majority"** — it just means >50%; ask whether it's 51% or 99%.
  3. **Beware of vivid/exceptional examples** — they're memorable but may be the exception, not the rule.
  4. **Assume you are not "normal"** and other people are not idiots — ask "In what way is this a smart solution?"
  5. **Beware of generalizing from one group to another** — what's true for group A may not hold for group B.
- **Dollar Street**: A tool to find better categories — imagine all homes lined up by income, photographed (130+ aspects of daily life across 300 families in 50+ countries). Reveals that **income, not religion/culture/country, is the main factor in how people live**.
- **"Get a toolbox, not a hammer" (control rule)**: Use better categories (the Four Levels) in place of misleading ones (developed/developing).

## Key Concepts
- **Gap + generalization combine**: The gap instinct splits the world into "us" and "them"; the generalization instinct makes "them" all seem the same.
- **Cool chains**: 88% of children are vaccinated — requiring refrigerated transport/electricity/health infrastructure, the *same* infrastructure that supports new factories. Investors who think it's 20% miss the biggest opportunities.
- **Half-built houses as smart solutions**: On Levels 2–3, building with bricks-as-savings (no bank access, inflation-proof, theft-proof) is rational, not lazy.

## Mental Models
- **"Assume people are not idiots"**: When a behavior looks strange (flaking hospital walls, half-built houses), assume there's a smart reason and look for it.
- **The flip test for one example**: If one unsafe chemical makes you conclude all chemicals are unsafe, would one safe chemical make you conclude all are safe? If not, your generalization is broken.

## Anti-patterns
- **Generalizing across incomparable groups**: The deadliest example below — soldiers vs. babies.
- **Generalizing your Level 4 experience to all levels**: The student who stuck her leg in an Indian elevator door, assuming all elevators have Swedish-style sensors — nearly lost her leg.
- **Talking about "Africa" or "African countries" as one thing**: 54 countries, 1 billion people, every income level — Ebola in Liberia cut Kenyan tourism (a 100-hour drive away, farther than London to Tehran).

## Worked Example
For ~a decade, doctors (including Rosling) put sleeping babies to sleep **on their tummies**, generalizing from a wartime discovery: unconscious soldiers laid prone survived because vomit drained instead of choking them (the "recovery position," still global best practice). But a sleeping baby is *not* an unconscious soldier — babies have working reflexes and turn their heads on their backs, while some are too weak to lift their heavy heads when prone. The seemingly impregnable logic plus good intentions hid the error; SIDS deaths *rose*. It took until a 1985 Hong Kong study (and seven more years for Sweden) to reverse the advice. **Thousands of babies died from one sweeping generalization** — some even after the evidence was available.

## Key Takeaways
1. You must categorize — so make your categories accurate, and assume they're misleading until checked.
2. Look for differences *within* groups and similarities *across* groups.
3. "Majority" and single vivid examples are traps; ask for the percentage and more examples.
4. Don't assume your own experience is "normal" or that strange behavior is stupid.
5. The most dangerous generalizations hide behind impeccable logic and good intentions — stay ready to be proven wrong.
