## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The three scaling classes — the first question to ask of any quantity

| Class | Exponent | Meaning | Canonical cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sublinear** | < 1 | economies of scale — bigger needs *less* per capita | metabolic rate ~mass^**3/4** (**Kleiber's Law**); city infrastructure ~pop^**0.85**; companies ~size^**0.9**; strength ~weight^**2/3** (Galileo) |
| **Linear** | ≈ 1 | genuinely per-capita quantities | houses, jobs, household water and electricity, blood volume |
| **Superlinear** | > 1 | increasing returns — bigger yields *more* per capita | socioeconomic city output ~pop^**1.15**: wages, GDP, patents, professionals — *and* crime, AIDS, flu |

**Method**: plot on **log-log axes**; the **slope is the exponent**. A power law is a straight line there and invisible on linear axes.

### Quarter-power scaling and the magic number four
All biological exponents are multiples of ¼, because fractal, space-filling networks add an effective **fourth dimension of life** (4 = 3 spatial + 1). Life span ~mass^**+1/4**; heart rate ~mass^**−1/4**; their product is invariant at **~1.5 billion heartbeats per lifetime** for every mammal (humans ~2.5 billion, the outlier). Double a mammal's mass and every rate falls ~25% while every timescale stretches ~25%.

### The three network postulates — the mechanism behind every exponent
1. **Space filling** — the network reaches every locally active unit (capillaries to every cell; utilities to every building).
2. **Invariance of terminal units** — endpoints stay the same size at all system sizes: capillaries, alveoli, leaf petioles, **electrical outlets**; in cities, **people and their houses**.
3. **Optimization by minimization of energy dissipation** — evolution or competition selects the space-filling network that minimizes cardiac output (or travel time, or cost).

Use these three as a test: if all hold, expect a clean power law.

### The 15 percent rule
Double a city's population and you get **~15% more socioeconomic output per capita** while needing **~15% less infrastructure per capita**. Same magnitude, opposite sign, causally linked. The good, the bad, and the ugly share the exponent — you cannot buy the wages without the crime.

### Scale-adjusted metrics / residuals — the most actionable technique in the book
Never compare entities of different size directly or per capita. Instead: fit the scaling law within one system → compute each unit's **residual** (log-deviation from its size-predicted value) → rank by residual. Overperformers sit left of zero. These **Scale-Adjusted Metropolitan Indicators (SAMIs)** persist for decades, which means they measure structure, not management. Seven of the twenty largest US cities are top-20 on per-capita GDP; **none** are top-20 on SAMIs.

### Bounded vs. open-ended growth
- **Sublinear supply + linear maintenance ⇒ growth stops.** Double the mass and maintenance doubles while supply rises only 2^0.75 = 1.682. The surplus shrinks to zero. This is why organisms mature, and why **companies** do too.
- **Superlinear supply ⇒ growth accelerates.** The bigger the city, the faster it grows — **faster than exponential**, hence open-ended.

### The punchline: why companies die but cities don't
**Companies scale sublinearly (~0.9) like organisms, not superlinearly like cities.** Bureaucracy and economies of scale beat innovation; the company's **dimensionality contracts** with age while a city's expands. Result: constant, age-independent mortality with a **half-life of ~10.5 years**; ~45% die by merger or acquisition, only ~9% by bankruptcy. Cities are almost impossible to kill.

### The accelerating treadmill and the finite-time singularity
Superexponential growth reaches infinity at a **finite** time — categorically unlike exponential growth, whose singularity is harmlessly at infinity. Collapse is avoided only by **paradigm-shifting innovations that reset the clock**, and the theory requires the interval between them to keep shrinking. Diagnose any growth curve by its **doubling time**: constant ⇒ exponential (manageable); shrinking ⇒ a wall exists with a date.

### Working heuristics
- **Treat "per capita" as a red flag, not a metric.** It embeds a linear assumption that is almost never valid.
- **Think of a whale as a scaled-up elephant.** At the 80–90% level, size alone predicts the system. Ask what your entity is a scaled version of before assuming it is unique.
- **Rigidity is fragility; variance is resilience.** A smooth EKG signals disease.
- **Detrend before you benchmark** — and use the right exponent, not 1.0.
- **Metaphor is not theory.** "The DNA of the company" predicts nothing.

## Scope & Limits

This skill covers the book's content only. Exponents are West's reported values with their stated approximation (~0.85, ~1.15, ~0.9) — treat them as coarse-grained averages with real variance, largest for companies. Scaling laws hold **within** a single urban or market system; cross-system comparisons need an anchor. Forward-looking estimates (the timing of the next paradigm shift) are single-source and should be held loosely. For applying these methods to your own data, combine with statistical tooling; for topics beyond this book, check related skills or ask the agent directly.
