# Chapter 6: Prelude to a Science of Cities

## Core Idea
Cities superficially resemble organisms — they metabolize, grow, produce waste, get sick, and age — which motivates extending network scaling theory to them. But a city is fundamentally *its people*, not its physical fabric, and a century of urban planning failed precisely by inverting that.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Cities-as-organisms**: an old and seductive analogy — Aristotle's *polis* as a natural autonomous entity; the Japanese **Metabolism** architecture movement (Kenzo Tange); James Moore's 1993 "business ecosystem." West's verdict: **evocative but entirely qualitative**. No predictive power. Metaphor is not science.

- **Cities-as-social-networks** — West's actual position, following **Jane Jacobs** (*The Death and Life of Great American Cities*, 1961). Jacobs attacked "the pseudoscience of planning" and insisted people, not buildings, are primary: "a promenade needs promenaders." West's formulation: a city is an **emergent complex adaptive social network system**. The physical infrastructure exists to serve the social network, not the reverse.
  - When to use: whenever evaluating an urban intervention. Ask what it does to the *space of interaction*, not to the built form.

- **The Euclidean/planned vs. fractal/organic axis** — the chapter's organizing tension.
  - **Garden cities** (Ebenezer Howard): prescribed proportions of housing, industry and agriculture, laid out in circles and straight lines. Explicitly *not* fractal. Jacobs' verdict: nice towns "if you were docile and had no plans of your own."
  - **Modernism / form follows function** (Le Corbusier): proposed razing central Paris for high-rises; "cleaning and purging" the city.
  - **New Urbanism** (1980s): mixed use, pedestrian and transit emphasis; descended from Mumford and Jacobs.
  - The organic pole is Mandelbrot: real, evolved cities have fractal boundaries; planned ones have exact squares (Masdar) and geometric circles.

- **Cities > nation-states as economic drivers** — Jacobs' claim, later carried by Edward Glaeser, Richard Florida, and Benjamin Barber (*If Mayors Ruled the World*).

## Key Concepts
- **Urban buzz** — the indefinable social energy of a successful city; the thing planners cannot draw.
- **Space of interaction** — the real product of a city: bringing people together.
- **Urbanocene** — the era in which the fate of cities determines the fate of the planet.
- **Planned city** — one built ex nihilo to a master plan (Washington, Canberra, Brasília, Islamabad, Stevenage, Masdar).

## Mental Models
- **Think of a city as its people, and the buildings as plumbing.** Infrastructure is instrumental; interaction is the output.
- **Use "does this increase or decrease interaction?" as your test** for any urban proposal, in place of aesthetic or efficiency criteria.
- **Think of a soul as an emergent property that takes decades.** Washington needed ~150 years, London ~100, Brasília is still working on it after 50. But global urbanization is now adding ~1.5 million people a week — decades-scale evolution is too slow.
- **Treat metaphor as a hypothesis generator, never as evidence.** "The DNA of the company" and "the ecology of the marketplace" predict nothing.

## Anti-patterns
- **Planning for physicality instead of for people.** Buildings, highways, and infrastructure are means; planners keep treating them as ends.
- **Top-down utopian master plans where only the planner has plans** (Howard, Le Corbusier, Robert Moses).
- **Building new cities ex nihilo.** Almost all planned cities end up soulless and alienating; nearly none meet expectations. Masdar (Abu Dhabi, ~$20 billion, ~50,000 planned inhabitants, boundary an exact square) is the contemporary case; Brasília, despite Niemeyer's professed love of free-flowing sensual curves, delivered a concrete jungle where, in de Beauvoir's phrase, the street does not exist and never will.
- **Slum clearance and urban renewal that destroy community**; highways that sever neighbourhoods into isolated, crime-breeding islands.
- **Assuming a city will simply evolve a soul given time.** It will — but not fast enough at current urbanization rates.
- **Mistaking a compelling metaphor for a predictive theory.**

## Worked Example
**Jane Jacobs vs. Robert Moses — Greenwich Village.**

Robert Moses had reshaped New York's infrastructure over almost forty years and was at the height of his power. His Lower Manhattan Expressway would have driven a four-lane, limited-access highway through Greenwich Village, Washington Square, and SoHo, paired with razing city blocks for upscale high-rises. This was orthodox practice: urban renewal and slum clearance, executed by credentialed experts.

Jane Jacobs had no degree and no research credentials. She argued from anecdote, personal observation, and intuition that the plan would destroy something essential — the dense, mixed-use, short-block street life that generates a city's buzz. She was vilified not only by developers and politicians but by planners, including Lewis Mumford, who read her as a sentimental reactionary.

She won. What survived is the district whose galleries, restaurants, and, in West's phrase, crazy people supply much of what makes New York great.

West notes the irony: the Washington Square Village high-rises that *did* get built — NYU faculty housing where he has stayed — are now slated for demolition and restoration. *Plus ça change.*

The transferable lesson is not "amateurs beat experts." It is that the experts were optimizing the wrong variable. They measured traffic flow, floor area, and light; Jacobs measured interaction. Scaling theory (Ch 7) later supplied the quantitative case that Jacobs argued qualitatively.

## Key Takeaways
1. A city is a social network anchored in physical space, not a physical structure that happens to contain people.
2. Biological metaphors for cities and companies are worth taking seriously enough to test quantitatively — and worthless until you do.
3. Planned cities systematically underperform evolved ones. Suspect any design whose boundary is a perfect geometric shape.
4. Optimize for interaction density and diversity; infrastructure follows.
5. The urgency is real: China alone plans 200–300 new cities in twenty years, urbanizing ~300 million rural residents. There is no time for cities to grow souls organically.
6. Before you accept an urban intervention, ask whose plans it serves — the residents', or the planner's.
