# Chapter 9: Leading a Growing Team

## Core Idea
As a team doubles (and doubles again), the manager's job changes from direct, hands-on work to **setting vision, hiring leaders, and delegating** through other managers. The central skill becomes **delegation** — knowing when to dive in and when to step back — and the goal is to constantly **put yourself out of a job**.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Big teams vs. small teams — what changes**:
  - **Direct → indirect management**: past ~8 reports you can't 1:1 everyone weekly; you hire/develop managers and get further from the ground.
  - **People treat you differently**: authority makes people withhold the ugly truth ("you're kind of a big deal"). Counter: invite dissent, own mistakes, use "I may be totally wrong — tell me if you disagree."
  - **Context switching all day**: your time fragments; build calendar-scanning, note-taking, and reflection systems.
  - **You pick and choose your battles**: something is always sub-optimal; perfectionism isn't an option — prioritize.
  - **Skills become more people-centric**: at higher levels the job converges regardless of discipline — hire great leaders, build self-reliant teams, set a clear vision, communicate well.
- **The delegation tightrope**: "the art of knowing when to dive in yourself and when to step back and entrust others." Too far one way = **micromanager** (stifling, people leave); too far the other = **absentee manager** (no support, lost credibility). Most people lean one way based on their values and must correct.
- **Giving big problems is a sign of trust**: the best people *want* to be challenged. Hand over the tangled knot, publicly declare them the owner (the public declaration empowers), then stay "on the boat" coaching — don't walk away.
- **Two heads, one shared vision**: 1:1s aren't for status transfer. Instead ensure you see eye to eye — (1) "What are the biggest priorities right now?" and (2) "Are we aligned on people, purpose, and process?"
- **Put yourself out of a job**: constantly look to replace yourself. Delegate anything a report can do as well or better; "try to double your leadership capacity every year." The rule: spend your energy at the intersection of **(1) most important to the org** and **(2) what you're uniquely able to do better than anyone**.

## Key Concepts
- **What only you should do** (org-important + your unique value): identifying and communicating cross-cutting patterns; hiring top talent (senior leaders attract candidates); resolving conflicts between your reports' competing priorities (they lack the full picture — escalate to you).
- **When a manager struggles**: a manager should be a *positive multiplier*; when she isn't, costs are high. The clarifying question (from Grove's warning that "the subordinate's tuition is paid by his customers"): **"Assume the role were open — would you rehire this person or take a gamble on someone else?"** If no, make the move.
- **People trump projects**: Chris's review question — "Do we feel we have the right people on the right problems?" — cuts through the noise; a great team is the prerequisite for great work.

## Mental Models
- Humans "cooperate flexibly in very large numbers" only because we share the same vision in our heads (Harari). Your job at scale is to make that shared vision vivid.
- "If you want to build a ship, don't drum up men to gather wood… teach them to yearn for the vast and endless sea" (Saint-Exupéry) — sell the purpose, not the task list.
- Give a fish vs. teach to fish: resist "I'll take care of it" for problems a report could learn to solve.

## Anti-patterns
- **Believing good managers spare reports the hard problems** (the "grandmother" instinct) — it overestimates what one person can solve and underestimates how much talented people *want* challenge.
- **Getting attached to what you do** — Julie clung to running the Monday design meeting because it was part of her identity; during her leave, teammates ran it *better*. Hand things off sooner.
- **Doing knowledge-transfer in 1:1s** — poor use of report-focused time and unrealistic at scale.

## Worked Example
**"Would you rehire Raphael?"** A manager ("Raphael"), perfect for an early scrappy team, was now grappling with a much larger group that "felt as though it was pitching against a wild storm." Julie believed in him and knew the growth mindset says anyone can improve — but *how long, and at what cost to the team?* A friend's question reframed it: "Assume the role was open. Would you rather rehire your current leader or gamble on someone else?" That zeroed in on what mattered — the team's success over the next few years. She moved Raphael off the role; the experienced successor "sauntered up to the wheel and steered the ship out of the storm," and the team thrived.

## Key Takeaways
1. Scaling changes the job at every doubling: direct work gives way to vision, hiring, and delegation.
2. Authority mutes honesty — actively make it safe to tell you the truth.
3. Delegate to the trust-signal extreme: hand over big problems, declare ownership publicly, then coach.
4. Use 1:1s for shared vision (priorities + people/purpose/process), not status.
5. Always try to put yourself out of a job — delegate what others can do as well or better.
6. When a manager can't keep pace, ask "would I rehire them?" — if no, make the move.
