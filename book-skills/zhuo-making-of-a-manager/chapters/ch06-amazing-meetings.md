# Chapter 6: Amazing Meetings

## Core Idea
"Every meeting should have a purpose" doesn't go far enough — ask **"What does a *great outcome* look like?"** Then invite only the right people, help them come prepared, make it safe to contribute, and kill or skip the meetings that don't earn their cost.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The five meeting types** (know which one you're running — don't make one meeting do two jobs):
  1. **Making a Decision** — get a clear call *and* leave people trusting the process. Needs the affected people + a designated decision-maker; present options objectively with a recommendation; give dissent equal airtime. You don't need consensus.
  2. **Sharing Information** — group learns something valuable, conveyed memorably, with an intended emotion. Only worth in-person if it beats email (interactivity, aliveness).
  3. **Providing Feedback ("review")** — get everyone aligned on what success looks like; honestly represent status; frame open questions to get *useful* feedback; end with next steps. Not about whether the boss "likes" it.
  4. **Generating Ideas** — needs *both* quiet solo thinking (brains are most creative alone) *and* group discussion. Twelve people blurting out loud isn't effective.
  5. **Strengthening Relationships** — build trust, openness, and a sense of being cared for.
- **Great-outcome markers for any meeting**: attendees leave feeling it was a great use of time, they learned something, they know what to do next, everyone was engaged, they felt welcomed.
- **The calendar audit**: for a week, note every meeting and whether you participated / were critical / got value. Julie found ~40% failed the test; a Perlow study found 65% of managers say meetings block their own work.

## Key Concepts
- **Trust the process** (decision meetings): people share the same ultimate goals; a fair, efficient process lets them "disagree and commit" (Bezos). Skipping context and deciding on one side's story forces costly do-overs.
- **Curse of knowledge**: presenters who know the material forget what it's like to see it fresh. Fix: **send materials/agenda the day before** so people can process and contribute well.
- **The real cost of meetings**: a 500-person meeting that flops wastes 500 person-hours; spending 5 people × a few hours to prepare is trivially worth it.
- **Post-it note opening**: for a complex topic, have everyone silently write ideas for 10–15 min, post them, cluster, then discuss — lowers the barrier to participation.

## Mental Models
- "Happy families are all alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way." (Tolstoy) — bad meetings fail in many ways; good ones feel consistently great.
- Guard your time "like a dragon guards its treasure." If you trust the right outcome happens without you, don't attend — just get pre/post notes.
- Don't just cut yourself from bad meetings — cancel meetings that are valuable to *no one*.

## Anti-patterns
- **Inviting too many people** — lackluster energy, phones out, and reviews become high-pressure "lecture halls" where honesty dies. Shrink to who's needed for the outcome.
- **Making a decision without affected stakeholders present** — delays or forces re-escalation.
- **Letting a few voices dominate** — manage airtime: cover interruptions ("Hang on, Ann wasn't finished"), open doors ("John looks like he wants to say something"), and cap overtalkers politely.
- **Not setting participation norms** — if you want hard questions, *say so*: "Hard questions are good! Get them off your chest!"

## Worked Example
**The status meeting that had a purpose but no great outcome.** Julie started a weekly go-around where everyone shared what they were working on — standard practice, clear purpose (keep people informed). It bombed: glazed eyes, keyboard tapping, wildly varying detail. A team member emailed, "To be honest, this meeting doesn't feel like a good use of time." She realized she'd never asked what a *great* outcome looked like — she actually wanted people to feel closer and collaborate better, which the format didn't deliver. She killed it, switched to weekly email updates, and it "worked out beautifully."

## Key Takeaways
1. Define the *great outcome* before scheduling; pick one of the five types and stick to it.
2. Invite only those needed for that outcome — including a decision-maker and affected stakeholders.
3. Send materials the day before to beat the curse of knowledge.
4. Engineer safety and equal airtime; set norms explicitly and manage the flow.
5. Always end with agreed next steps and send a recap (decision, action items + owners, next check-in).
6. Audit your calendar; skip meetings that don't need you and cancel ones that help no one.
