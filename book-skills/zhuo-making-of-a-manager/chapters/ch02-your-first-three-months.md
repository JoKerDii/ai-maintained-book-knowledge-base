# Chapter 2: Your First Three Months

## Core Idea
How you should spend your first ninety days depends on *how you got the job*. Zhuo maps four transition paths, each with different advantages to exploit and traps to avoid.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The four paths into management** (choose your own adventure):
  - **Apprentice** — your manager's team grew and you took a slice. Easiest transition; most guidance.
  - **Pioneer** — you're a founding member now responsible for growth. Most autonomy, least support.
  - **New Boss** — you're hired to lead an established team. Comes with a "newbie window" and blank slate.
  - **Successor** — your manager left and you're taking their whole role. Apprentice's context plus a big jump in responsibility.
- **The "what's awesome / what could be better" list** (Apprentice/Successor): write two columns of the current state — what to preserve and what to change. "You don't need to fix what isn't broken, but neither should you feel stuck in a time machine."
- **The newbie card** (New Boss): you have ~3 months where nobody expects you to know anything. Use it to ask every question of everyone; don't wait until you "know enough."
- **The blank-slate reset** (New Boss): give every report the benefit of the doubt regardless of what you're told; ask each what their "dream manager" looks like.

## Key Concepts
- **Dream-manager questions** (first 1:1s as New Boss): "What did you and your past manager discuss that was most helpful?" / "How do you like to be recognized?" / "What kind of feedback is most useful for you?" / "Imagine we had an amazing relationship — what would that look like?"
- **Calibrating 'normal'** (New Boss): ask *your* manager — "What does a great vs. average vs. poor job look like? Examples?" / "What keeps you up at night?" / "Is X normal or should I be concerned?"
- **Show, don't tell** (New Boss trust-building): open up first — "Since I'm new you might not feel comfortable sharing everything. I'll start by sharing my biggest failure ever…"

## Mental Models
- Treat the transition as a *joint plan with your own manager*, not a solo scramble (Apprentice questions: scope, communication, people, goals, what success looks like at 3 and 6 months).
- "Be yourself; everyone else is already taken." (Successor) — don't try to be your predecessor; play to *your* strengths.

## Anti-patterns
- **Clinging to individual-contributor work too long**: virtually every apprentice does this. At 4–5 reports, have a plan to scale back IC duties — trying to be both manager and maker means doing neither well.
- **Jumping in to prove yourself** (New Boss): exerting opinions before they're informed backfires. First job is to listen, ask, and learn.
- **Framing feedback as gentle suggestions** to former peers when you now own the outcome. The manager–report relationship requires directness the peer relationship didn't.

## Worked Example
**The apprentice's IC trap.** When Julie's team hit ~six people she was still lead designer on a complex project. Whenever something unusual happened — a report needing extra 1:1 time, multiple reviews in one week — her own project starved. "The quality of my work suffered, my peers got frustrated, and the balls I was juggling plopped to the ground." She finally gave up wanting to be both design manager *and* designer. The lesson she urges: don't learn it the hard way — plan the handoff at 4–5 reports.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose your path (Apprentice / Pioneer / New Boss / Successor) — it sets your first-90-days playbook.
2. Have context? Ramp fast and make a keep/change list. New from outside? Use the newbie window to ask, listen, learn.
3. Build a joint transition plan with your own manager.
4. Scale back IC work early — don't wait until you're drowning.
5. Expect it to feel awkward and to take real time. ("How long till I know what I'm doing?" → "About three years.")
