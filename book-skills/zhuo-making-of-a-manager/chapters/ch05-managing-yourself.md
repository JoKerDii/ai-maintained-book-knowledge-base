# Chapter 5: Managing Yourself

## Core Idea
You can't manage a team well if you don't manage yourself. Get **brutally honest self-awareness** (strengths, weaknesses, triggers, ideal conditions), adopt a **growth mindset**, and build habits and support systems to climb out of the inevitable "Pit."

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Imposter syndrome is universal**: *every* manager feels like a fraud sometimes. It hits managers hard for two reasons — (1) you're constantly looked to for answers, (2) you're constantly doing things you've never done before (you can't "practice firing people" — you must go through the real thing).
- **Get to brutal honesty with yourself** — a two-part self-inventory:
  - *Strengths*: "How would people who love me describe me in 3 words?" / "3 qualities I'm proudest of?" / "Traits I credit for past success?" / "Most common positive feedback?"
  - *Weaknesses & triggers*: "What does my inner critic yell at me for?" / "3 gifts a fairy would give me?" / "3 things that trigger me?" / "Most common feedback on being more effective?"
- **Calibration**: make your self-view match reality by asking others for unvarnished opinions (guard against the *Dunning-Kruger effect*). Ask your manager to rate you 1–5 against a hypothetical "perfect person in my role."
- **Fixed vs. Growth mindset** (Carol Dweck): fixed mindset governs actions by fear (of failure, judgment, being found out); growth mindset seeks truth and feedback as the fastest path forward.
- **Triggers**: situations that provoke an outsize negative reaction *specific to you* (Julie's is injustice). Naming them lets you catch yourself and pause before reacting like a hothead. Triggers sit between your growth area and someone else's.
- **Escaping the Pit** (six tactics): (1) Don't beat yourself up for feeling bad (don't pay the "double tax"); (2) "The story in my head is probably irrational" — seek the truth ("Why wasn't I invited?"); (3) Visualize success/past wins; (4) Ask for help from people you can be real with; (5) Celebrate little wins (a *Little Wins* journal); (6) Practice self-care and set boundaries.
- **"How can I be twice as good?"** — the frame for on-the-job learning: ask for feedback constantly; treat your manager as a coach (not a judge); make a mentor out of everyone; reflect and set goals (weekly + six-month); take formal training.

## Key Concepts
- **Manager as coach, not judge**: your boss wants you to succeed (your wins are their wins). "What skills should I work on to have more impact?" beats hiding weaknesses.
- **Mentor lightly** (Sandberg): don't ask "Will you be my mentor?" — ask for *specific* advice, and many will help.
- **Reflection compounds learning** (HBS): people who reflected outperformed those who got more raw experience.

## Mental Models
- Your personality traits are recipe ingredients — great management comes from cooking well with what you have, not wishing for different ingredients. Top leaders come from opposite molds (Churchill/Lincoln, Thatcher/Mother Teresa).
- Visualization works: imagined free-throw and gym practice produced much of the benefit of the real thing. Make the mental scene as specific as a "color movie" (Nicklaus).
- Formal training: don't ask "Is it worth it right now?" — ask "One year from now, will I be glad I did this?"

## Anti-patterns
- **"Fake it till you make it" in isolation** — denying yourself others' empathy and advice. Admitting struggle is courage, not weakness.
- **Focusing only on weaknesses** — humans over-weight the negative (the cave-dweller scanning for lions); you'll under-invest in the strengths that actually drive your success.
- **Treating challenges as tests of worthiness** rather than chances to improve — traps you in anxious self-evaluation.

## Worked Example
**The 20-page report about Julie.** Overwhelmed after returning from maternity leave, Julie hired coach Stacy McCarthy and rattled off seven fires to fix. Stacy instead said, "Tell me about you," interviewed her closest colleagues, and returned with a 20-page report — nothing about the fires, everything about *how Julie worked* and how others saw her. Reading it alone at night, she felt "dissected," teared up at praise she hadn't credited herself for, and learned her anxiety was producing wishy-washy decisions — while nobody actually saw her as "weepy and less capable." It became one of the best things for her career: it calibrated her internal compass so she could move forward.

## Key Takeaways
1. Everyone feels like an imposter — normalize it and keep going.
2. Inventory your strengths, weaknesses, and triggers, then calibrate against others' honest views.
3. Choose the growth mindset: feedback is fuel, not a verdict.
4. Design your day around what makes you your best (sleep, morning momentum, thinking time, boundaries).
5. Have a Pit toolkit ready; ask for help and celebrate little wins.
6. Learn on the job — ask "how can I be twice as good?" and use your manager, peers, and reflection to get there.
