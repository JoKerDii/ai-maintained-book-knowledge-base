# Chapter 3: Leading a Small Team

## Core Idea
With a small team, purpose is easy and process is light — so **people are everything**, and the foundation of people-management is **trust**. Your reports must believe you care about them unconditionally so they'll be honest with you.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Grove's two barriers to good work**: someone does poor work for exactly one of two reasons — (1) they **don't know how** (skills gap → train or hire), or (2) they **know how but aren't motivated** (unclear picture of "great," role doesn't fit aspirations, or no consequences for effort). Diagnose which before acting.
- **The three-statement trust test** — you have a trusting relationship if:
  1. My reports regularly bring their biggest challenges to my attention.
  2. My report and I regularly give each other critical feedback and it isn't taken personally.
  3. My reports would gladly work for me again.
- **Managing is caring**: you can't fake respect — reports read the thousands of subconscious signals. Care means helping them succeed *and* readily telling them when they're wrong; respect must be **unconditional** (about the whole person, not their performance).
- **1:1s are the core ritual**: at least weekly, 30 min, focused on the *report* (not status updates). Prepare questions; let the report lead.
- **Play to strengths (individuals and teams)**: great management comes from capitalizing on what's unique in each person, not fixing weaknesses. Don't let the worst performers dominate your time — coaching a rising star to +10% often beats dragging a laggard to +33%.

## Key Concepts
- **1:1 question banks** — *Identify*: "What's top of mind?" / "Best use of our time today?" *Understand*: "What does your ideal outcome look like?" / "What's hard about getting there?" / "Worst-case you're worried about?" *Support*: "How can I help?" / "What was most useful today?"
- **Strive for awkward 1:1s** (Mark Rabkin): the most meaningful conversations feel a little awkward; superficial pleasantries build nothing.
- **"You have good values"** (Chris): specific, believed recognition of a strength restores confidence better than generic reassurance.
- **The divider effect / "brilliant asshole"**: a talented person who belittles others makes the whole team less effective. Robert Sutton's *No Asshole Rule* — the team gets *better off* when they leave.

## Mental Models
- Litmus test: if a report says "everything's fine" for multiple weeks, prod further — it's more likely they're shy about the gory details than that all is rainbows.
- "If you have to ask whether your report would work for you again and you're not sure, the answer is probably no."
- Fit is like dating: a catch by all accounts can still be wrong for *this* team. Departures aren't always failures.

## Anti-patterns
- **Tolerating brilliant jerks** because of their output — the fog they create costs more than they contribute.
- **Always trying to "make it work"** — some conflicts and fits aren't worth saving; "You're trying to save a situation that isn't worth your time."
- **Being slow on people moves**: 80% of the time, extra 1:1s and pep talks for a fundamentally mismatched report prove futile — and the drawn-out cycle is worse *for the report* than an honest move. Jack Welch: keeping low performers around is "false kindness."

## Worked Example
**"Be human, not a boss."** A talented manager who reported to Julie got feedback that he micromanaged. As she relayed it, he deflated. She said, "I feel you… I struggle with it too," and shared her own failure from the day before to walk the line between actionable feedback and micromanaging. He looked relieved: "Thank you for that — it was incredibly helpful." She'd given no tactical advice at all; what helped was that they *related* as two people wading through management, which made every future hard conversation easier.

## Key Takeaways
1. Small team → people dominate; trust is the load-bearing wall.
2. Diagnose poor work as skill vs. motivation before you act.
3. Respect must be unconditional, or honesty dries up when things get rocky.
4. Run weekly 30-min 1:1s about the report; prepare questions; let them lead.
5. Invest in your strongest people, not just your struggling ones.
6. Make people moves quickly; help someone find a better role or let them go — respectfully, decisively, non-negotiably.
