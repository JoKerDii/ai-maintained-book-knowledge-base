# Chapter 6: The Big Cycle of External Order and Disorder

## Core Idea
Relations between countries follow the same order/disorder cycle as within them — but driven far more by raw power, because there is no effective international law, police, courts, or enforcement. "The international order follows the law of the jungle." Before a shooting war there is almost always a decade of economic war.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 5 kinds of war between countries**: (1) **Trade/economic** (tariffs, import/export restrictions), (2) **Technology** (which tech is shared vs. protected), (3) **Geopolitical** (territory/alliances via negotiation), (4) **Capital** (sanctions, blocking capital-market access), (5) **Military** (actual shooting). The first four escalate over ~a decade before a military war; once shooting starts, all four are weaponized to the max.
- **"Have power, respect power, and use power wisely"**: The master principle of international relations.
  - *Have* power — it wins over agreements, rules, and laws when push comes to shove.
  - *Respect* power — don't fight a war you'll lose; negotiate the best settlement.
  - *Use* power wisely — generosity and trust ("soft power") often beat bullying ("hard power").
- **The 3 governance choices (and where fascism sits)**: Every country picks one from each: (1) bottom-up **democratic** vs. top-down **autocratic**; (2) **capitalist** vs. **communist** (socialist between) ownership of production; (3) **individualist** vs. **collectivist**. **Fascism = autocratic + capitalist + collectivist.**
- **The 3 classic economic/capital war tactics**: (1) **Asset freezes/seizures**, (2) **Blocking capital-markets access**, (3) **Embargoes/blockades**.
- **The classic wartime domestic economic policies** (a-k): government controls on production, rationing, imports/exports, prices/wages/profits, access to financial assets, capital mobility; plus monetized debt, gold for international settlement, more autocratic governance, and mutual sanctions.

## Key Concepts
- **Guns and butter**: Wealth = power; a country must fund *both* military strength (guns) and domestic living standards (butter) without excesses. **Financial strength to outspend rivals is one of the most important strengths** — how the US beat the USSR without a shooting war.
- **The greatest risk of military war**: when two parties have (1) roughly **comparable military power** and (2) **irreconcilable existential differences**. (Today: US–China over Taiwan.)
- **Win-win vs. lose-lose**: Negotiate by knowing what matters most to the other party and to yourself, and trade them. Winning = getting what's most important without losing what's most important.
- **The fight-or-back-down dilemma**: Both are costly — fighting in lives/money, backing down in status (showing weakness reduces support).

## Mental Models
- **"Power prevails."** When individual countries have more power than collectives (UN, League of Nations), the countries rule. Wealth and power among equals is rarely given up without a fight.
- **The two certainties about war**: (1) it won't go as planned, and (2) it will be far worse than imagined. Hence most principles aim to *avoid* shooting wars.
- **Timing power**: Fight (or negotiate/enforce) when your relative power is greatest — fight *early* if your power is declining, *later* if rising.
- **Hide the knife**: Best to not show power (showing it makes others feel threatened and arm up) — but reveal it when a threat improves your negotiating position.
- **"In war one's ability to withstand pain is even more important than one's ability to inflict pain."**
- **Win-win point allocation**: Each side ranks its top-10 wants with 100 points; trade what's low-priority for you but high for them (e.g., Taiwan is existential for China, less so for the US).

## Anti-patterns
- **Slipping into "stupid" wars**: Via (a) the prisoner's dilemma, (b) tit-for-tat escalation (a game of chicken), (c) the declining power's perceived cost of backing down, (d) misunderstandings under fast decision-making. Wars costing more in lives/money than benefits are stupid — yet happen constantly.
- **Untruthful, emotional appeals to rile the population**: Especially dangerous in a democracy and worse when leaders take over the media — raises the odds of a stupid war.
- **Raising tariffs in bad times**: Protects favored entities and wins political support, but reduces efficiency and, via tariff wars, deepens global weakness (Smoot-Hawley, 1930).

## Worked Example
**The road to WWII — the three cycles converging.** The 1929 crash → global depression → almost every country had big internal wealth conflicts → turn to populist/autocratic/nationalist/militarist leaders (fascist right in Germany/Japan/Italy/Spain; communist left in USSR/China; milder shifts in the stronger democracies US/UK). Germany: 25% unemployment → Hitler chancellor (1933), refused reparations, left the League, took autocratic control (1934), stimulated via forced bank bond-buying + Reichsbank monetization — unemployment fell to nil by 1938, real growth averaged >8% (1934–38), equities +70%. Japan (resource-poor island): exports fell ~50% (1929–31), went broke in 1931, abandoned gold, and seized resources by invading Manchuria. The economic war ran ~a decade before shooting: US Smoot-Hawley tariffs (1930), then escalating sanctions on Japan culminating in July–Aug 1941 — Roosevelt froze Japanese assets, closed the Panama Canal to Japanese ships, and embargoed oil, cutting off ~75% of Japan's trade and 80% of its oil. Japan calculated ~2 years of oil left → fight or back down → Pearl Harbor, Dec 7–8, 1941. **Investing lesson from the war years:** sell debt and buy gold — wars are financed by borrowing and printing (devaluing money/debt), stock markets close (German/Japanese markets shut ~5 years and were nearly wiped out), and open markets track battle outcomes (Allied equities rallied continuously after Midway, 1942).

## Key Takeaways
1. International relations run on power, not law — attempts at rule-based order fail whenever a country outweighs the collective.
2. Before a shooting war there is an economic war (~a decade of trade/tech/capital/geopolitical conflict).
3. Domestic and military strength go hand in hand — sustain guns *and* butter without excess; the financially strongest side can win without fighting.
4. Existential, irreconcilable differences + comparable power = highest war risk; win-win trading of red lines is the way out.
5. Harmful acts of nature (droughts, floods, plagues) combine with bad economics to produce great conflict (1930s droughts in the US and USSR).
6. No world power lasts forever — but the traumatic decline is avoidable if a rich power stays productive, earns more than it spends, serves most of its people, and builds win-win relationships with rivals.
