# Chapter 4: The Changing Value of Money

## Core Idea
Currencies are far more fragile than people assume: nearly all die or are devalued. Big devaluations come abruptly during debt crises, and the value of money must be watched against gold, goods/services, other currencies, and stocks — especially late in the long-term debt cycle.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"All currencies are devalued or die"**: Of ~750 currencies since 1700, only ~20% survive — all devalued. The 1850 world's major currencies (gulden, thaler, koban, ryo) are mostly gone.
- **Systemically beneficial vs. systemically destructive devaluations**: Both cost holders of money/debt, but tell them apart.
  - **Beneficial**: debt relief flows into productivity and profits → real stock prices rise; monetary system survives.
  - **Destructive**: damages the credit/capital-allocation system but is needed to wipe out debt and build a new monetary order.
- **MP1 / MP2 (monetary policy tools)**: **MP1** = allocate capital via interest-rate changes. **MP2** = print money and buy high-quality (government) debt. When both fail late in the cycle, monetary policy becomes a facilitator of a *political* system allocating resources uneconomically (implicitly MP3 — directed fiscal spending).
- **The three benchmarks for a currency's value**: measure money against (1) gold (the timeless alternative currency), (2) CPI baskets of goods/services (buying power), and (3) other currencies/stocks. When a devaluation is big enough, all three tell the same story.

## Key Concepts
- **Devaluation vs. loss of reserve status**: Not the same — both stem from debt crises, but **loss of reserve status is the product of chronic, repeated large devaluations**.
- **Real return**: Nominal interest minus inflation. Cash's real return since 1912 (fiat era) averaged **-0.1%**; gold returned **+1.6%**.
- **Inflation-hedge assets**: Gold, commodities, inflation-indexed bonds, other currencies (including digital) — where money flees when cash/debt returns turn negative.
- **The "run" dynamic**: More claims on the central bank than hard currency to satisfy them; forces the choice to devalue.

## Mental Models
- **Watch currency risk, not just asset risk**: Most people obsess over stocks going up/down and ignore that the *currency itself* can decline — the bigger blind spot.
- **Devaluations are abrupt and episodic, not evolutionary**: Long calm periods of prosperity punctuated by fast breaks.
- **Cash is not safe late in the cycle**: Holding interest-earning cash carries very large risk as a storehold of wealth, with ~10-year swings around the average.
- **Reserve-status decline can be gradual**: War winners (UK after the World Wars) with debts exceeding assets and lost competitiveness lose reserve status slowly, not in a single crash.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a currency as permanent**: The default assumption that "cash is the safest asset" is historically false.
- **Ignoring falling central-bank reserves**: Net reserves start falling *before* the devaluation — sometimes years ahead — and countries often suspend convertibility first (UK 1947/49, US 1971).
- **Confusing gold's price with a single verdict**: In lightly-devaluing countries (US) gold lagged inflation; in Germany it hugely outperformed — so cross-check gold against CPI.

## Worked Example
**How a devaluation self-reinforces (the policy trap).** Money/credit creation reduces the value of cash and debt — good for debtors, bad for holders. If debt relief flows into productivity and profits, real stock prices rise (the *beneficial* path). But when creation is large enough to make cash/bond returns unattractive, holders flee into gold, commodities, and other currencies — a self-reinforcing decline in the value of money. The central bank now faces a fork: let real interest rates rise (hurting the economy and angering the public) or print money to buy the cash/debt assets and cap rates. **It chooses to print**, which further worsens the returns of holding cash/debt — driving more flight. Late in the long-term debt cycle, when debt is already too large, the rate low enough to save debtors is *below* the rate creditors need to hold the debt — MP1 and MP2 stop working, and the system breaks toward a new monetary order.

## Key Takeaways
1. Currencies devalue primarily **against debt** — the whole point of printing is to lighten debt burdens.
2. Big devaluations happen fast, during crises, separated by long stable periods — don't expect a gentle slide.
3. Gold roughly matched inflation from 1850–1971; after the 1971 shift to fiat, unconstrained money creation drove gold's big real appreciation to 1980, then falling when rates went above inflation.
4. Reserve-status loss = chronic devaluation; watch for the classic "run" (claims > hard currency) tied to war spending (Fourth Anglo-Dutch War, the World Wars, Vietnam).
5. Losing a war → total currency/economy collapse; winning but over-indebted → slow reserve-status decline.
6. Learn to distinguish beneficial from destructive devaluations — it changes whether you hold stocks or flee to hard assets.
