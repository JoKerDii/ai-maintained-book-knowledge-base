# Chapter 10: The Big Cycle Rise and Decline of the British Empire and the Pound

## Core Idea
"The same story as the Dutch, a century later in different clothes." Britain built education, institutions (rule of law, a strong Parliament, the Bank of England), and Industrial-Revolution technology, defeated the Dutch and then Napoleon's France, and — with only 2.5% of world population — produced ~20% of world income and ran ~25% of the global population at its peak. The pound's slow post-1945 decline is the archetypal reserve-currency handoff.

## Frameworks Introduced (applied)
- **Rise sequence, illustrated**: competitiveness/education/innovation rose sharply (early 1600s) → output, trade share, military expanded together (1700–1900) → London became the financial center → (with the longest lag) the pound overtook the guilder as reserve currency.
- **Institutional foundation of the rise**: The English Civil War (executed Charles I) and Glorious Revolution (1688) weakened the monarchy, strengthened Parliament, and established **rule of law over rule of monarchy** — enabling moderately meritocratic leadership (Pitt, Peel, Gladstone, Disraeli, all from merchant families).
- **Financial strength as strategic weapon**: The Bank of England (1694) made UK debt liquid; the 18th-century tax burden was ~2× France's; the UK could borrow at half France's rates and *lend* to anti-Napoleon coalitions — decisive in the wars.
- **New-order-by-victors, repeated**: Congress of Vienna (1815) → balance-of-power order → Pax Britannica ("splendid isolation," the Imperial Century). Then Paris/Versailles (1919) — which, unlike Vienna, made a second war inevitable.

## Key Concepts
- **Why not the French?** In the early 1700s France looked as strong (Enlightenment hub, rising literacy). But John Law's Mississippi Company bubble (1720) burst, expensive wars (Louis XIV through the American Revolution) wrecked its finances, an inefficient/unrepresentative tax system amplified inequality → French Revolution, Reign of Terror, assignat hyperinflation → Napoleon (the classic "strong leader restores order") → overreach → defeat.
- **The Top's reversal**: peak wealth gap coincided with peak empire (~1900; top 1% owned >70% of UK wealth). Declining competitiveness (UK missed the Second Industrial Revolution's reorganization; US and Germany pulled ahead), rising inequality/labor conflict, and rising rivals (Germany, US) — the classic three decline drivers.
- **Germany's textbook rise**: post-unification (Bismarck) built mandatory education, research universities, state credit/grants to innovators, rule of law for development → manufacturing share 5%→13% (1860–1900), GDP surpassed Britain's by 1900. Wilhelm II forced Bismarck out, pursued world-power status, and started a naval arms race → WWI.
- **Reserve-status lag, quantified**: The pound took ~20 years after 1945 to fully lose reserve status; a third of world trade was still sterling-denominated in 1960 — like the English language, hard to displace.

## Mental Models
- **You couldn't have called it at the time**: Dalio notes his own indicators in the early 1700s would have shown the Dutch still at peak *and* France rising — the rise is "obvious in retrospect," not in real time. Hence the need for data and decision rules.
- **Empire profitability funds the military — until it doesn't**: The empire's economic activity more than paid for its military; when growth slows, the empire flips from profitable to a drain.
- **New order revolves around the victor**: Post-WWII order (UN in New York, Bretton Woods, IMF/World Bank, dollar) centered on the US, exactly as Vienna centered on Britain.

## Anti-patterns
- **Missing the next technological wave**: British inventions seeded the Second Industrial Revolution, but the UK failed to *reorganize its industries*, so output-per-worker fell behind the US and Germany.
- **Defending a peg you can't afford**: The UK's 1947 return to convertibility triggered an immediate run; austerity (Attlee's "another battle for Britain" speech *accelerated* the run) couldn't hold it → suspension, then 30% devaluation (1949), then again (1967).

## Worked Example
**The protracted death of the pound as reserve currency (1946–1968).** WWII forced the UK to borrow immensely from allies and colonies, all held in sterling. Post-war it couldn't service the debt without austerity, so it blocked colonies from selling their sterling bonds. Under US pressure to restore liquidity, the UK made the pound partially convertible in July 1947 (backed by a $3.75B US loan, ~10% of UK GDP). The pound was immediately hammered; by end-August convertibility was **suspended** — angering US and international holders. In 1949 it was **devalued 30%** vs. the dollar (no panic, because the US government and pegged Sterling-Area economies like India/Australia absorbed the hit). But recurring balance-of-payments strains (too indebted, too uncompetitive) forced another **devaluation in 1967**; the deutschemark took the pound's #2 spot. After 1967, even Sterling-Area countries would only hold pounds if Britain guaranteed 90% of their *dollar* value (Sterling Agreement, 1968) — i.e., they were holding de facto dollars. Central banks then sold sterling for dollars, marks, and yen, and the pound's reserve share collapsed within two years.

## Key Takeaways
1. Institutions first: rule of law + strong Parliament + liquid state debt (Bank of England) were the platform for the Industrial-Revolution rise.
2. Financial strength wins wars — Britain out-borrowed and out-lent France to beat Napoleon.
3. France's cautionary tale: bubble + costly wars + unrepresentative, regressive taxation → revolution → strongman → overreach.
4. The Top's three decline drivers: falling competitiveness, rising inequality/conflict, and rising rivals (Germany, US).
5. Reserve status is the last and stickiest strength to fade, then unwinds through a series of devaluations, not one crash.
6. The post-WWII order (UN, Bretton Woods, dollar) and the EU's federation project (Schuman/Adenauer: make war "materially impossible") both fit the new-order-after-war pattern — though the EU has since slipped to secondary power for the classic Big Cycle reasons.
