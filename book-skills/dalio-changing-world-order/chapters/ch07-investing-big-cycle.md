# Chapter 7: Investing in Light of the Big Cycle

## Core Idea
Markets are driven by just four determinants, and the debt/capital-markets cycle — the "Big Investing Cycle" — is the greatest force behind accumulations and losses of wealth. Most investors are blinded by survivorship bias; extend your view across 500 years and many countries and you see wealth gets wiped out regularly, so you must build in protection.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The 4 determinants of all markets**: **growth, inflation, risk premiums, discount rates.** "Tell me what each will do and I can tell you what the investments will do." All investments are lump-sum-today for future-payments; growth/inflation set the future cash flows, risk premium sets the required extra return over cash, discount rate sets present value.
  - Stronger-than-expected growth → stocks rise. Higher-than-expected growth *and* inflation → bonds fall.
- **Investment risk = failing to earn enough to meet your needs** — NOT volatility (standard deviation), the near-universal but wrong measure.
- **The 3 biggest risks investors face**: (1) portfolio won't return enough to meet spending needs, (2) portfolio faces ruin (devastating loss), (3) a large share of wealth is taken away (e.g., high taxes). Note (1) and (2) differ — you can have above-required average returns *and* a ruinous drawdown.
- **Central government vs. central bank division of labor**: Governments tax and spend (decide where money comes from/goes) but can't create money/credit; central banks create money/credit but can't decide where it goes in the real economy. Their interaction drives cycles.

## Key Concepts
- **The Big Cycle of Capitalism & Markets**: In the upwave, financial wealth (cash/bonds/stocks) rises relative to tangible wealth until promises can't be met → run-on-the-bank debt problems → money printing → devaluation → financial wealth falls relative to real wealth → cheap again → cycle restarts.
- **Financial wealth (invented ~1350, Italy)**: Before cash deposits, bonds, and stocks, all wealth was tangible. Financial wealth = promises to deliver money; it has no intrinsic value — only what it buys is useful.
- **Survivorship bias**: Using US/UK long-term returns as "representative" is misleading — they are uniquely blessed WWI/WWII winners in the best part of the Big Cycle. Few markets even survived WWII.
- **The devaluation-vs-interest question**: The single most important recurring question — *does the interest being paid more than make up for the devaluation risk?*

## Reference Tables
**Payback period on bonds (Aug 2021, real terms):** US ~45 yrs nominal / *may never* recover buying power; Japan ~150 yrs nominal / >250 yrs real; Europe negative — *likely never* get money back. Conclusion: with negative real rates you're near-guaranteed less future buying power — better to buy "any stuff" that keeps pace with inflation.

**Wealth destruction, 10 great powers of 1900:** 7 of 10 saw wealth virtually wiped out at least once; Germany and Japan (plausible "winners") had nearly all wealth and many lives destroyed. Even the US/UK had a few devastating decades.

## Mental Models
- **The all-weather / balanced portfolio**: Each asset is a "building block" that reflects the four drivers logically. Balance them so the portfolio has no bias to any environment (true diversification), then tactically tilt based on what will move the four drivers.
- **"Dropped into 1900" test**: Stress-test your strategy across every decade and many countries, not just US/UK since the 1950s — otherwise you never see the destruction periods that recur almost everywhere.
- **During devaluations, hard assets rise vs. cash**: Periods when the classic 60/40 stock/bond portfolio fell were periods when gold rose (a description of mechanics, not a buy recommendation).
- **Late-cycle math**: Real yields of reserve-currency bonds and cash are near the lowest ever — the classic late long-term-debt-cycle signature (money printed to reduce debt burdens devalues the currency vs. goods and other storeholds).

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating history and old returns as irrelevant**: Most investors base expectations only on their own lifetimes; even the diligent rarely look before the 1950s. No investor or policy maker Dalio knows has an excellent grasp of the distant past — a competitive-advantage gap.
- **Equating risk with volatility**: Standard deviation misses the risks that actually matter (unmet needs, ruin, confiscation).
- **Holding cash/bonds late-cycle for the "safety"**: When real yields are deeply negative you're almost guaranteed to lose buying power — the longest payback periods ever.
- **Ignoring the confiscation risk**: High taxes can take a large share of wealth; it's a distinct, real investment risk.

## Worked Example
**Why the "run on the bank" can't happen — until the central bank makes it not happen.** Financial wealth is now enormous relative to real wealth. If everyone holding bonds and stocks tried to convert them into real goods at once, it would be a run on the bank — the claims are too large relative to what they can buy, so it *couldn't* happen. But under fiat money the central bank can print and supply the money to meet the demand — a timeless, universal truth. That is exactly the late-cycle dynamic: policy makers print to relieve debt defaults and falling markets, which devalues money, which pushes real returns of cash and debt negative (interest rates *must* stay low to relieve debtors), which drives holders toward real/hard assets — until financial assets get cheap enough relative to real assets that peace, prosperity, and excellent real financial returns return.

## Key Takeaways
1. Master the four drivers (growth, inflation, risk premiums, discount rates) and you can connect the world to the markets and build an unbiased, diversified, tactically-tilted portfolio.
2. Define risk as failing to meet your needs — guard against unmet-needs, ruin, and confiscation, not just volatility.
3. Beware survivorship bias: study losers and pre-1945 history, not just the US/UK.
4. Financial wealth is only a claim on real wealth; when claims vastly exceed what they can buy, the debt/money cycle turns.
5. Constantly ask whether interest compensates for devaluation risk — late-cycle, it usually doesn't.
6. Because wealth destruction recurs almost everywhere, always hold protections against big adverse moves *and* against being wrong.
