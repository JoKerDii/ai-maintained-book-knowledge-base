# Chapter 13: US-China Relations and Wars

## Core Idea
The US (late in its Big Cycle, declining) and China (rising) are rival powers now fighting across multiple "wars." These are new versions of classic conflicts, resolved by tests of power, not laws. The most important war is the one each country fights with itself.

## Frameworks Introduced (applied)
- **The 5 (+2) types of war between the US and China**: (1) trade/economic, (2) technology, (3) geopolitical, (4) capital, (5) military — plus Dalio's additions: (6) **culture war** and (7) the **war with ourselves**. They are not separate conflicts but one evolving conflict.
- **The war ranking by seriousness**: The **technology war** is more serious than the trade war ("whoever wins the technology war will probably win the military war and all the others"), because **big data + big AI + big computing = superior decision-making** (China has ~4× the people, collects more data/person, ~8× the STEM grads).
- **The capital war logic**: "No money = no power." The US's greatest power is the reserve currency (print the world's money + control who gets it; ~8,000 sanctions as its most-used weapon) — and it is **at risk of losing reserve status** for the reasons in Ch 11.
- **The determinants review = "the enemy is us"**: The war with ourselves is decided by the 18 determinants — measurable, within our control. Whatever makes those lines go up is good.

## Key Concepts
- **Why they're in these positions (destiny)**: US success → reserve currency → over-borrowing (from China) → late-debt-cycle monetization at negative real yields → an awkward debtor-creditor bind. China's collapse → revolution → capitalist reforms → rise. Both are exactly where the Big Cycle puts them.
- **The reserve-currency scorecard**: Central bank reserves — USD 51%, EUR 20%, Gold 12%, JPY 6%, GBP 5%, CNY 2%. **No attractive alternative to the dollar** — but "currencies are still devalued and capital finds its way into other investments (gold, commodities, stocks, property)" even without a strong alternative.
- **Culture clash is the deepest divide**: Chinese = top-down, collective, know-your-role, filial respect; American = bottom-up, individual, personal freedom, revolutionary thinking. Neither will give up its values; forcing the other's system feels like subjugation worth dying over ("give me liberty or give me death" vs. collective stability).
- **The most dangerous flashpoint: Taiwan.** Because the US won the Pacific in WWII, *it* must choose to defend Taiwan or retreat. China is now militarily stronger in the East/South China Seas (US would likely lose there) but weaker globally. A US non-defense would be a great humiliation signaling Pacific decline — like Britain's loss of Suez signaled the pound's end.

## Mental Models
- **Watch the red lines and trip wires**: To prevent escalation, both sides must be clear about what signals a change in the seriousness of the conflict; the worst escalation is **cutting off essential imports** (US oil/tech to China; China's rare earths, GM/Apple markets) — the US-Japan-1941 analogue.
- **"No laws in war"**: Between countries there are no judges or juries; both sides publicly claim the moral high ground while privately doing the same things (IP theft, protectionism). Leaders rally support with "we are good, they are evil."
- **Timing favors the patient side**: Time is on China's side (faster improvement), so China's interest is a *later* conflict, the US's a *sooner* one. Enemies exploit internal disorder and leadership transitions (Japan invaded a divided China in the 1930s).
- **Alliances take down the leader**: Historically the most powerful country is defeated by an alliance of less-powerful but collectively stronger countries — so watch which countries align with whom (especially China–Russia). Countries choose China on economics, the US on military — but doubt the US will show up.
- **Multidimensional chess**: US-China is not bilateral — it requires reasoning across Russia, Japan, India, Europe, Saudi-Iran-Israel, etc. Impossible without data and computers; world leaders understand each other's thinking shockingly little.

## Anti-patterns
- **Slipping into an unnecessary/"stupid" war**: Tit-for-tat escalation where looking weak beats being wise — especially for declining empires, which fight more than logical because any retreat reads as defeat.
- **Untruthful, emotional rhetoric**: 73% of Americans now hold an unfavorable view of China (Pew); it "wouldn't take much" for publics on both sides to demand escalation.
- **Trying to force the other's internal policies**: Imposing democracy/human-rights demands (or China imposing its way) is impossible and dangerous — ability to impose is purely a function of relative power.

## Worked Example
**The decoupling underway (2021 snapshot, projected 5–10 years).** The trade war so far is limited (tariffs, a thin 2019 "phase one" deal — a test of power, not a WTO resolution); Dalio expects it to worsen, not ease, with bipartisan hawkishness the one thing both US parties agree on. The tech war is graver: US leads overall (tech market cap ~4× China's) but is losing the lead — ahead in advanced AI chips, behind in 5G; China leads in supercomputers (40% of the world's largest civilian ones), fintech payments, and some AI/quantum dimensions. The US weaponizes China's dependency on imported chips (TSMC in Taiwan is the pivotal chokepoint); cutting China off from essential semiconductors would signal major escalation toward a shooting war. Both sides are decoupling ("dual circulation" in China), and China's rate of reducing cut-off-able dependencies will far exceed the US's over 5–10 years, leaving China stronger and less squeezable. Conclusion: absent aggressive US disruption, evolution makes China relatively stronger, more self-sufficient, and less vulnerable.

## Key Takeaways
1. The US-China conflict spans 7 interrelated wars; the technology war is the most decisive, the capital war rests on the dollar's reserve status, and the culture war defines what each will die for.
2. Tests of power — not laws — resolve these conflicts; cutting off essential imports is the escalation marker to watch.
3. No attractive alternative to the dollar exists, but a currency can still be devalued as capital flees to gold, commodities, stocks, and property.
4. Taiwan is the most dangerous flashpoint; time favors China, so watch for leadership transitions + internal disorder as elevated-risk windows.
5. Cultural differences (top-down/collective vs. bottom-up/individual) are deeply embedded — peaceful coexistence requires accepting, not converting, the other.
6. **The war with ourselves is the biggest**: internal challenges outweigh external ones, are measurable via the 18 determinants, and are within our control — "Deserve Victory!"
