# Chapter 12: The Big Cycle Rise of China and the Renminbi

## Core Idea
China stood among the world's most powerful empires for most of 1,400 years, suffered a steep decline (~1840–1949, the "Century of Humiliation"), and has risen rapidly since Deng's 1978 reforms. To understand China you must see it through Chinese eyes — its philosophies, its ~250-year dynastic cycles, and its century-plus planning horizon aimed at 2049.

## Frameworks Introduced (applied)
- **The three Chinese philosophies** (govern how leaders lead and people relate):
  - **Confucianism** — harmony through everyone knowing and playing their role in the hierarchy (family up to ruler); values harmony, broad education, and meritocracy; superiors are benevolent but impose strict standards.
  - **Legalism** — rapid conquest and unification under an autocratic leader; the world is a kill-or-be-killed jungle demanding strict obedience without benevolence. (Western equivalent: fascism.)
  - **Taoism** — live in harmony with nature's laws; balance opposites (yin/yang).
- **The dynastic cycle = the internal-order cycle (Ch 5)**: ~250 years (±150), running Stage 1 (new order/consolidation) → 2 (build systems, meritocratic exams) → 3 (peace/prosperity) → 4 (excess) → 5 (bad finances/conflict) → 6 (civil war) → 1 again.
- **China's three-phase modern plan**: Phase 1 (Mao) — win the revolution, consolidate power. Phase 2 (Deng→Xi) — build wealth/power/cohesion *without threatening* the US. Phase 3 (Xi and successors) — reach the 2049 goal (PRC centennial): a "modern socialist country that is prosperous, strong, democratic, culturally advanced, and harmonious," with the economy ~2× the US's and gains broadly shared. Codified in Made in China 2025, China Standards 2035, and five-year plans with explicit metrics.

## Key Concepts
- **Top-down/collective vs. bottom-up/individual** (per Wang Qishan): Americans hold the individual above all; the Chinese put family and collective first. The Chinese word for "country" = "state" + "family" — leaders run the state like parents run a family. Result: Chinese are more humble, respectful, rules-bound; Americans more arrogant, egalitarian, rules-averse.
- **Why China rarely had a reserve currency**: Despite inventing paper money and the first stock companies (Song), China was less commercial (merchants ranked below scholars under Confucianism), never built a rival financial center, and stayed relatively isolationist — so it fell behind Europe in innovation and finance by the 1600s.
- **China's monetary cycles**: China ran the full Type 1 → 2 → 3 → 1 rotation repeatedly. Song Dynasty *invented* fiat money (huizi notes, 1160) *and* was first to overprint it (value fell >90% by the 1230s); Yuan and Ming repeated the pattern. After Ming's fiat failure, China used silver/copper by weight until the 20th century.
- **Inherited-determinant constraints**: China is a giant plain within big natural borders (mountains, seas) — most wars were internal, for control of it; it rarely occupied distant states, preferring a tribute system in which weaker states kept their customs in exchange for peace and trade.

## Mental Models
- **Win without fighting**: Traditional Chinese military philosophy (Sun Tzu) — the ideal is to develop power so overwhelming that merely displaying it makes the opponent capitulate; use psychology extensively.
- **Century-plus planning horizon**: Chinese leaders plan over 100+ years (a good dynasty's length), breaking it into multidecade phases and five-year plans — and hit most of their metrics. (Dalio notes his own 500-year/100-year historical lens produces a similar effect.)
- **See through the other's eyes**: Mao saw capitalism as imperialist exploitation because that's how China experienced it (opium, unequal treaties); Dalio saw it as opportunity. Both views are "true" — a lesson in radical open-mindedness.

## Anti-patterns
- **Isolation + intellectual conservatism**: Ming/Qing hostility to commerce and openness (vs. Song/Tang support) left China behind Europe in innovation, finance, and law.
- **Printing to fund deficits instead of taxing/cutting**: The Song, Yuan, and Ming each debased their fiat currency rather than face the discontent of higher taxes — the recurring late-dynasty failure.
- **Ignoring capital controls as a warning**: "When you see capital controls being put on a currency, especially when there is a big domestic debt problem, run from that currency."

## Worked Example
**The Century of Humiliation (1840–1949) — the Big Cycle in reverse.** By the early 1800s the Qing had become decadent while Britain rose. The British East India Company wanted tea/silk/porcelain but had nothing the Chinese wanted, so it paid in silver — until, running short, it smuggled **opium** from India into China to earn silver back. China fought to stop the trade → the technologically superior British Navy won the First Opium War (1839–42) → the treaty ceded Hong Kong and opened ports (Shanghai), later losing northern territory to Russia/Japan and Taiwan to Japan. The Qing borrowed heavily from foreigners to fight rebellions (Taiping) and paid crushing reparations (Boxer Rebellion: ~18,000 tons of silver on a 40-year debt secured by port tariffs). Five mutually reinforcing forces — weak leadership, unsound finances, internal rebellions, costly foreign wars, and disruptive acts of nature — drove the self-reinforcing decline. Seven of the eight powers bottomed ~1940–50. Then the classic upswing: Mao wins the civil war (1949) and consolidates; Deng's 1978 open-door reforms ignite competitiveness and trade (~1980); from 1984–2008 debt grew *in line* with strong growth (no debt loading); post-2008 debt rose to stimulate; Xi (2012) improved debt management, innovation, education, and military. China is now roughly tied with the US in trade, output, and innovation.

## Key Takeaways
1. China's culture (Confucian/Legalist/Taoist, hierarchical, collective) shapes how its leaders lead and negotiate — see it through their eyes.
2. Chinese dynasties ran the same ~250-year internal-order cycle as every empire, decade after decade.
3. China ran every monetary system and every debt cycle Chapter 3 describes — and invented both fiat money and its overprinting.
4. The Century of Humiliation is the formative memory driving modern Chinese leadership and its determination not to be exploited again.
5. China plans over 100+ years toward 2049, with explicit metrics — and generally hits them.
6. The strategic ideal is to win without fighting by developing overwhelming, visible power.
